# The Concepts of Process and Thread

The following can happen when Process 5 reads p_free and stores the value, 6, in a local variable called f_slot. Then, a clock interrupt occurs, so it switches to Process 6. **Process 6 also reads p_free**, and also gets a 7. It stores p_free its local variable f_slot. At this instant, both processes think that the next index is 16. Process 6 continues to run. It stores the handles of the files at index 16 and updates p_free to be 17. The system then puts Process 6 to sleep. Now, Process 5 runs again, starting from the place it left off. If it at f_slot, finds an 16 there, and writes its handle to the file, replacing the handle that Process 6 put there. Then, it computes f_slot + 1, which is 17, and sets p_free to 17. The buffer is now internally consistent, so the process will not notice anything, but Process 6 will never receive an output.

**Process 6 will run for an infinite period of time**, waiting for an output that will never occur. Situations when two or more entities are reading or writing some shared data and the final result depends on which runs first; these are called **race conditions**.

How to avoid race conditions? Most of the solutions involve shared memory, files, and everything is shared at the same time. In other words, need **mutual exclusion** or a mechanism that will provide exclusive access to a shared object, whether it is a shared variable or file, or any other object. The difficulty can occur when Process 6 starts inside its **critical section** or **critical region**. We need to ensure that no two processes get in their critical sections at the same time to avoid race condition. Even though race condition is avoided, this approach may not be efficient when having parallel processes, which are intended to cooperate correctly and efficiently. If we intend to use shared data, we must take care of the following conditions:

+   - No two processes may be simultaneous inside their critical regions
- No assumptions may be made about the speed or number of CPUs
- No process running outside its critical region may block other processes
- No process should have to wait to enter its critical region

In some time, **T2 (Process B)** attempts to enter its critical section but fails because another process is already in its critical section, and we allow only one process at a time in the critical section. **Process B** must be temporarily suspended until time **T3 when Process A leaves its critical section**, allowing Process B to enter immediately. Eventually, **Process B** leaves at (**T4**), and we are back to the beginning where no process is in its critical section.
