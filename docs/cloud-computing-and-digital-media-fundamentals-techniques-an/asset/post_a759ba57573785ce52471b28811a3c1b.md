## 11.2.5.1 Recovering Camera Geometry

In Section 11.2.3, we have examined the properties of $F$ and image relations for point correspondence $x'$. We now turn to one of the most significant properties of $F$ that may be used to determine the camera matrices of the two views.

### 11.2.5.1.1 The Essential Matrix

To compute the extrinsic parameters of a camera, the conception of essential matrix to extract extrinsic parameters should be introduced first. The essential matrix is the specialization of the fundamental matrix to the case of normalized image. Historically, the essential matrix was introduced before the fundamental matrix, and the fundamental matrix may be thought of as the generalization of the essential matrix in which the (inessential) assumption of calibrated cameras is removed. The essential matrix has fewer degrees of freedom and additional properties compared to the fundamental matrix. These properties are described below.

Consider a camera matrix decomposed as $P = K [R|t]$, and let $x=PX$ be a point in the image. If the calibration matrix $K$ is known, then we may apply its inverse to the point $x$ to obtain the normalized point $X$. Then we have $x=[R|t]X$, where $x$ is the image point expressed in normalized coordinates. It may be thought of as the image of the image $X$ with respect to a camera $[R|t]$ having the identity matrix $I$ as calibration matrix.

The camera matrix $K^{-1}P=[R|t]$ is called a normalized camera matrix and the effect of the known calibration matrix has been removed. Now, consider a pair of normalized camera matrices $\det(V) > 0$ and $P=[R|t]$. The fundamental matrix corresponding to the pair of normalized cameras is customarily the essential matrix, and according to Section 11.2.3, it has the form:

$$E = [t]_\times R R^T t^T \quad (11.14)$$

Snavely [1] uses the five-point algorithm to compute $E$; however, since $E = KFK^T$, we can easily compute the essential $E$ if the camera is calibrated $[K = \text{diag}(ff)]$, and $f$ can be loaded from photos. Once the essential matrix is known, $R, t$, and the camera matrices can be recovered.

### 11.2.5.1. Recover $R$ and $t$

According to the theory, the SVD of the essential matrix $E = U \text{diag}(1,0,V^T)$, where $U$ and $V$ are chosen such that $\det(V) > 0$ and $\det(U) > 0$. Then $t=[u_1u_2u_3]$ and $R$ is equal to $R_A UD^V$ or $R_B UD^VT$. Any combination of $R$ and $t$ according to the above prescription satisfies the epipolar constraint. To resolve the ambiguities, we assume that the first camera is $P=[I]$ and $t$ is of unit length. The second camera matrix is in the four possible solutions: $P_A=[R_a|t_a], P_B=[R_a|^{-t_a}], P_C=[R_b|t_b], P_D=[R_b|^{-t_b}$. One of the four choices corresponds to the true configuration.

The four solutions are illustrated in Figure 11.6, where it is shown that a reconstructed $X$ will be in front of both cameras in one of these solutions only. Thus, with a single point to determine if it is in front of both cameras is sufficient to decide between the four different solutions for the camera matrix $P'$.

## 11.2.5.2 Triangulate 3D Points

In Section 11.2.5.1, we have computed the camera matrices $P$ and $P'$, let $x$ and $x'$ be the two points in the two images that satisfy the epipolar constraint, $x'^TF=0$. This constraint can be interpreted geometrically in terms of the rays in space corresponding to the two image points. In particular, it means that $x'$ lies on the epipolar line $Fx$. In turn, this means that the two rays backprojected from image points $x$ and $x'$ lie in a common epipolar plane, which is, a plane passing through the two camera centers.

Since the two rays lie in a plane, they will intersect at some point, which is the 3D projection of the real point (Figure 11.7).

Now let come to the definition of triangulating 3D points: with the precondition of estimated camera matrices, to estimate a 3D point $X$ that exactly satisfies the supplied camera geometry, so it projects as

$$x = PX, x' = P'x'$$

the aim is to estimate $X$ from $x$ and $x'$. Note that $x=PX, x'=P'x'$ is an equation involving $X$, thus $x=PX, x'=P'x'$ is a redundant system. However, we need three points to solve for $X$. This is because the cross-product method results in:

$$\begin{matrix}
x = PX, x' = P'x'
\end{matrix}
\quad (11.15)$$

Since the two rays lie in a plane, they will intersect at some point, which is the 3D projection of the real point. In practice, because of noise, the rays may not intersect perfectly, so we find the point that minimizes the distance to both rays.
