# 15.2.7 Summary

Cloud computing changes the efficiencies and economics of providing ICT services. Large cloud “utilities” are being developed that will make it cost-effective to move many if not most ICT services “to the cloud”; the nature of the services provided can be negotiated with the cloud provider. Virtualization will enable several computing jobs (such as word processing or e-mail users) to run on a single computer, while elasticity makes it possible to have huge amounts of computing resources instantly available to meet the demands for intensive data processing. Cloud computing is evolving rapidly and new methods to ensure effective management and security will emerge. Currently, most applications of cloud computing are in administration and research, but the ability to build and share powerful new processes will rapidly expand the variety of services available. This is where the greatest potential lie for adult learning and literacy training.

Katz at al. [14] provide the following list of the benefits of a cloud computing approach:

- Driving down the capital costs of IT in higher education
- Facilitating the transparent matching of IT demand, costs, and funding
- Scaling IT
- Fostering further IT standardization
- Accelerating time to market by reducing IT supply bottlenecks
- Countering or channeling the ad hoc consumerization of enterprise IT services
- Increasing access to scarce IT talent
- Countering a pathway to a five nines (99.999%) system availability and 24 × 7 × 365 environment
- Enabling the sourcing of cycles and storage powered by renewable energy
- Increasing interoperability between disjointed technologies and within institutions

# 15.3 QUESTION 2: WHAT IS THE CURRENT STATE OF ADULT LITERACY EDUCATION IN CANADA AND IS A COHESIVE COMMUNITY APPROACH POSSIBLE?

It is beyond the scope of this chapter to completely portray the current state of adult literacy in Canada. There exist a number of excellent studies and literature reviews already published on this topic by researchers and government organizations [15–23]. Their portrayal is consistent with the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) [24] thematic report on adult learning: Canada is a vast country, and despite a wide variety of regional and federal programs that contribute to adult literacy, there remains a shortage of programs especially in rural and remote areas. There is a general need for additional programming for adults, particularly for Aboriginal peoples and for the working poor.

- The special needs of adults are generally neglected.
- There is no sense of adult education.
- Adult education is vulnerable to instability [24, pp. 42–43].

Adult literacy in Canada is also divided by different approaches. Federal funding is generally targeted to assist newcomers to become functional in one of the official languages. The OECD [23] identifies Aboriginals and the working poor as the two populations least served. Many Aboriginals grow up in isolated areas and learn English second language. The increasing urbanization of Aboriginal population brings many within reach of literacy programs, and there are a number of e-learning approaches that are being initiated to reach those in remote areas. However, low-literacy adults the least access to Internet connectivity and computers. Some 20% of Canadians form the “working poor” earning less than one-third of the median wage [23]. Many of them are also in rural and remote areas and traditionally earned their living in primary resources and agriculture sectors. With the decline of the resource economy, many lack sufficient education to access retraining for other jobs. Others cannot access the existing daytime literacy programs because of commitments to work or family care.

While there are lot of people falling through the cracks, some adult literacy practices are making inroads. Prior Learning Assessment and Recognition enables individuals to get recognition for life experiences and skills, and the resulting academic credits make academic credentials accessible. In British Columbia (BC), considerable work has also taken place in “laddering” or transferring credits in college or trades paths into higher education. In Alberta and the Northwest Territories, the Alberta–North collaboration of higher education institutions and community organizations that provide technology access and educational support in 87 remote communities enables a large number of learners to be first in their family to earn a degree.

Despite the low level of federal–provincial coordination in adult literacy, the community is organizing itself into regional and national networks to exchange information and educational resources. Of particular note is the National Adult Literacy Database (www.nald.com) which maintains a repository of up-to-date research and AlphaPlus (www.alphaplus.com), which also shares learning resources. When the Canada Council on Learning earned its mandate in 2009, the Adult Learning Center spun out the Movement for Adult Literacy, which is now the National Literacy Learning Network, a forum for all of the regional literacy networks across Canada.
