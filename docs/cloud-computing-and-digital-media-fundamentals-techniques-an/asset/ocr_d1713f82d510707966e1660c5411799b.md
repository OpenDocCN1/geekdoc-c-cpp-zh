The third concern is about switching. Many legacy software applications will need to be moved to the cloud environment and incompatibility in design standards can pose significant hurdles and be costly when portant them to a cloud platform. Fortunately, as has been mentioned earlier, very few adult literacy organizations have investments in ICT. The costs of “lock-in” cannot be avoided. The “Monkey and the Coconut” tale suggests that you can catch a monkey by chaining a coconut to a tree and boring a hole large enough for a monkey to reach its hand and grab a fistful honeyed rice. The closed fist is too large to go backward through the hole. For the monkey to be free of the trap, it has to get go of its investment in the bait. The costs of “letting go” from a cloud service or an ICT infrastructure may be insurmountable—just as it is difficult for a homeowner to dispute the rate hikes by the local electricity provider by threatening to get energy from another source. It is conceivable that in the future, the “free” Google and Microsoft academic and e-mail services might be charged as the providers will eventually need to recover their investment and operating costs. At that point, institutions may be “locked in” to those services.

The fourth concern is hype. Katz et al. [14] note that cloud computing seems to have caught the attention of almost every technology journalist to the point where it might be overlooked. While the cloud has arrived for common services such as e-mail, for other services the transition may take much longer. Technical and policy work remains to be done by the adult literacy community to determine which applications can go to the cloud and which require a more conservative approach.
