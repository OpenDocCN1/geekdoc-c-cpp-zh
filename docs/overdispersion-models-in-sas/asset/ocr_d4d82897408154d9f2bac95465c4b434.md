Early remarks regarding overdispersion under the Poisson model can be found in Student (1919). Examples of Poisson with overdispersion can be found (a) in the analysis of counts and rates of longitudinal studies, and (b) in behavioral studies and in studies of numbers where there is inter-subject variability. Real examples of overdispersion under the Poisson model are found in the analysis of number of tumors in rats, Gail et al. (1980) and Lawless (1987); in a longitudinal clinical trial to study the number of epileptic seizures, Breslow and Clayton (1993); and on the frequency of recurrence of bladder cancer, Dean and Banshaw (1997). An example with archaeological data was used by Efron (1992) to discuss the various patterns of overdispersion relative to Poisson variability; in another study, Zeger and Edelstein (1989) assess the efficacy of Vitamin C in reducing mortality in Indonesia.

# 1.3 A Common Characterization of Overdispersion

The phenomenon of binomial counts with overdispersion can be characterized in terms of the first two moments. Suppose that T is a Binomial variable representing the number of successes out of m trials. Then,

```
E(T) = m\pi,
```

where \pi represents the probability of success of each Bernoulli trial. Furthermore, if the Bernoulli trials Y_1, Y_2,...,Y_m that satisfy the assumptions of the Binomial model, Var(T) = m\pi(1-\pi). On the other hand, if they are positively correlated, the usual assumption of independence in the Binomial model is violated. Let denote the correlation between Bernoulli trials by \rho^2, 0 ≤\rho^2 ≤ 1. We use \rho^2 to indicate that the correlation is nonnegative. Then, it can be shown that

```
Var(T) = m\pi(1-\pi){1+\rho^2(m-1)}.
```
