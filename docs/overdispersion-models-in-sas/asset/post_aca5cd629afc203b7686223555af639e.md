It is impossible to reconstruct the probabilities under a prospective study from the information given above. However, all is not lost because the OR can be recovered from the information given above. The odds-ratio is now defined as the ratio of the odds of having the exposure factor when the disease is present and the odds of having the exposure factor when the disease is absent. These odds are defined as

> Odds(Having the Exposure Factor when the disease is Present) = $\frac{\pi_{11}}{\pi_{+1}} = \frac{\pi_{11}}{\pi_{21}}$

and

> Odds(Having the Exposure Factor when the disease is Absent) = $\frac{\pi_{12}}{\pi_{+2}} = \frac{\pi_{12}}{\pi_{22}}$

Thus, for a case-control study the odds-ratio is

> OR = Odds(Having the Exposure Factor when the disease is Present) / Odds(Having the Exposure Factor when the disease is Absent) = $\frac{\pi_{11}}{\pi_{21}} / \frac{\pi_{12}}{\pi_{22}}$

Note that the odds-ratio is the same both prospective and case-control (retrospective) studies. It is said that the odds-ratios are "invariant to the sampling scheme." This fact becomes important for epidemiologists, medical researchers and public health practitioners in case-control studies. In such studies, it turns out that the Relative Risk, RR = $\frac{\pi(X=1)}{\pi(X=0)}$

cannot be directly obtained. On the other hand, computing odds-ratio is straightforward. Under these circumstances, it is well known that the odds-ratio and the relative risk are practically the same (Cornfield, 1951). These two properties of the odds-ratio, invariance to the nature of the study and almost identical value to the relative risk, make logistic regression one of the most appealing statistical regressions

To illustrate the use of logistic regression in case-control studies we consider the Ille-et-Vilaine study of oesophageal cancer. Ille-et-Vilaine is a French department created from part of the province of Brittany during the French Revolution. Mortality from cancer concerning the oesophagus was known to be high in Brittany and Normandy. Tuyns et al. (1977) collected data in the department of Ille-et-Vilaine where 200 males (cases) were diagnosed with oesophageal cancer. A sample of 778 subjects (free of cancer) was drawn from electoral lists in each commune. Of those, 775 subjects provided sufficient information for analysis purposes. Breslow and Day (1980) used this example to illustrate different statistical methodologies for cancer research. We refer to the readers to Chapters 4 and 5 of their book. The data of the Ille-et-Vilaine case-control study are shown in Table 2.6.

data oesophageal_cancer;

proc genmod data=oesophageal_cancer;
    class age_stratum alcohol;

The Wald Chi-square statistic for testing the significance of the logarithm of the odds-ratios is 77.57 with one degree of freedom. The likelihood ratio tests (reported under Type 3) correspond to generalized analysis of variance discussed. The associated value of the likelihood-ratio statistic is 79.52. Both statistics are asymptotically equivalent. The estimated common-odds (reported under Contrast Estimate Results) is 5.31, with (3.66, 7.70) as an approximate 95% confidence interval. As seen in linear regression models, the estimate of the logarithm of the odds-ratios has been adjusted by the stratification variable (in the age in years). The common odds-ratio is usually estimated by the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) estimator. This estimator can be obtained using the programming statements given below. The value of the CMH estimator is 5.16 with an approximate 95% confidence interval (3.56, 7.47). It also provides the log estimator of the sample, which is 5.10 with an approximate 95% confidence interval (3.51, 7.41). The log estimator allows a correction for zero cell counts. Since two of the cell counts in the data set are zero, the latter is preferred. Note the similarities of the logistic regression and CMH results.

## 2.9 A Poisson Example of Failures of Electronic Equipment

From the examples in the previous sections we move to a Poisson example, which is the next most commonly used GLM. We will illustrate the comparison of the estimates and interpret the diagnostic plots. Jorgensen (1961) and Lomer et al. (1971) analyze data consisting of a poisson of electronic cycles under operation (weeks) in which each cycle was divided into two operating regimes. The data are provided in Table 2.7. The response variable is y_j, j=1,2,...,9, representing the number of failures in the j-th cycle. The components of the vector x_j = (x_{j1}, x_{j2}) are the times spent in regimes one and two during the j-th cycle of operation.

**Table 2.7: Number of Failures of Electronic Equipment Under Two Operating Regimes**

| Number of Failures y_j | 15 | 9 | 14 | 24 | 27 | 27 | 23 | 18 | 22 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time first regime x_{j1} | 33.3 | 52.2 | 64.7 | 137.0 | 125.9 | 116.3 | 131.7 | 85.0 | 91.9 |
| Time second regime x_{j2} | 25.3 | 14.4 | 32.5 | 20.5 | 97.6 | 53.6 | 56.6 | 87.3 | 47.8 |

As in From et al. (1971), we fit a Poisson model with λ_j = β_0 + x_{j1} + β_2 x_{j2}. Note that this model does not have any interaction and the link function is the identity function. Since this is the canonical link, by default the GLIMMIX procedure uses the Newton-Raphson algorithm with the Hessian matrix. GLIMMIX allows the use of the Fisher scoring method up to a specific iteration with the option SCORING=number. If the algorithm converges within the number of iterations specified in the SCORING option, the covariance matrix of the parameter estimates will be the inverse of the Fisher Information Matrix and not the inverse of the Hessian. To replicate the results of From et al. (1971) we use the identity link and the Fisher Scoring Method.
