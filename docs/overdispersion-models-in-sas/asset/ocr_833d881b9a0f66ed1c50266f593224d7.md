On the other hand, Berkson (1944) found the use of a cumulative Logistic distribution function (Logit model) more appropriate. The term "probit" is attributed to Bliss, whereas the term "logistic" is attributed to Pearl and Reed (1920) and embraced by Berkson in his 1944 paper. The probit and logistic analyses of dosage-mortality data are early examples of GLMs. These types of models are also known as Quantal Response Models.

Pyrethrins are a class of insecticidal chemicals found in many insecticides. Murray (1938) outlined a method of simultaneous evaluation of different insecticides, known as Peet-Greedy method, and the possible range of accuracy. Eleven different concentrations of total pyrethrins were tested on copies of flies. The dosage-mortality data on male flies are reported in Table 2.3.

We first fit a logistic regression model, which is the same as fitting a GLM with Binomial distribution and log link. Let $t_j$ represent the number of flies killed at dose level $D_j$, out of $m_j$ male flies, $j=1, 2, \dots, 11$. The logistic regression model assumes that each $t_j$ is a realization of $T_j \sim \text{Binomial}(\pi_j, m_j)$, with the log link

```
logit(\pi_j) = \ln\left(\frac{\pi_j}{1-\pi_j}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_j
```

where $x_j = \ln(D_j)$. Observe that associated with each pair $(t_j, m_j)$ is a vector of covariates, $\mathbf{x}_j = (1, x_j)$. We wish to obtain the MLE $\hat{\boldsymbol{eta}} = (\hat{eta}_0, \hat{eta}_1)$. We will use the GLIMMIX procedure.
