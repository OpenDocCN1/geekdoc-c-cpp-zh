## 2.6 Maximum Likelihood Estimation for Generalized Linear Models

Let $y_j$ represent a realization of the random variable $Y_j$, $j=1,2,...,n$, $Y_j$ from a given distribution in the exponential family. We are interested in finding the MLE of $\beta$. For certain distributions, in the Binomial and Poisson distributions, $\phi$ is known to be 1. In later chapters, maximum likelihood estimators will be obtained for both the vector of parameters $\beta$ and the dispersion (or overdispersion) parameter. Thus, from Section 1.6 we have that the log-likelihood function is

```
$L(\beta, \phi; \mathbf{y}) = \sum_{j=1}^n \left\{ \frac{y_j - b(\theta_j)}{\phi} + c(y_j, f(\phi)) \right\}$
```

Here $y$ represents the vector of observers $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, y_2, ..., y_n)$.

We will now consider the MLE of $\beta$ when $\phi$ is either fixed or known. The k-dimensional column vector of first derivatives of (2.15) with respect to $\beta$ is the gradient or score vector. That

```
$\mathbf{u}(\beta) = \frac{\partial L(\beta, \phi; \mathbf{y})}{\partial \beta}$
```

Technically, each term in (2.15) is a function of the canonical parameter $\theta_j$, and $\theta_j$ of the mean $\mu_j$, and $\mu_j$ a function of the linear predictor $\eta_j$, and $\eta_j = \mathbf{x}\beta$. Thus, obtaining the gradient (2.16) requires the chain rule. For further details, we refer the reader to Chapter 2 in Hilbe (2007).

The $k \times k$ matrix of second derivatives of (2.15) is the Hessian, given by

```
$\mathbf{H}(\beta) = \frac{\partial^2 L(\beta, \phi; \mathbf{y})}{\partial \beta \partial \beta}$
```

The Fisher information matrix (FIM) is negative of the expected value of the Hessian. That is,

```
$\mathbf{I}(\beta) = -E \left[ \frac{\partial^2 L(\beta, \phi; \mathbf{y})}{\partial \beta \partial \beta} \right] = E \left[ \frac{\partial L(\beta, \phi; \mathbf{y})}{\partial \beta} \frac{\partial L(\beta, \phi; \mathbf{y})}{\partial \beta} \right]$
```

In order to obtain $\hat{\beta}$, the MLE of $\beta$, one needs to solve the estimating equations

```
$$\frac{\partial L(\beta;\phi;\mathbf{y})}{\partial \beta} = \mathbf{0}. \quad (2.19)$$
```

These equations are known as the maximum likelihood score equations. More often than not, the solution of (2.19) does not have an explicit form and is thus solved iteratively. Several numerical methods are available. The iterative procedure for the Newton-Raphson Method is given by

```
$$\hat{\beta}_{(S+1)} = \hat{\beta}_{(S)} + {\hat{H}(\hat{\beta}_{(S)})\}^{-1}\mathbf{u}(\hat{\beta}_{(S)}). \quad (2.20)$$
```

and the widely used Fisher Scoring Method is implemented as follows

```
$$\hat{\beta}_{(S+1)} = \hat{\beta}_{(S)} + {\hat{I}(\hat{\beta}_{(S)})\}^{-1}\mathbf{u}(\hat{\beta}_{(S)}). \quad (2.21)$$
```

where $\mathbf{u}(\hat{\beta}_{(S)})$ is the k-dimensional vector of first derivatives of the log-likelihood function (2.16) evaluated at $\hat{\beta}_{(S)}$, $\hat{H}(\hat{\beta}_{(S)})$, is the $k\times k$ Hessian matrix (2.17) evaluated at $\beta=\hat{\beta}_{(S)}$, and $\hat{I}(\hat{\beta}_{(S)})$ is the $k\times k$ Fisher information matrix (2.18) evaluated at $\beta=\hat{\beta}_{(S)}}$. The subscript "$s$" refers to the step or iteration.

The preceding discussion applies to the computation of MLE in general. In the case of the exponential family, further simplifications due to the relationship between the derivatives of the log-likelihood function and the first moments given in equations (1.9) and (1.10). Nelder and Wedderburn (1972) and Wedderburn (1974) showed that the solution of the maximum likelihood equations (2.19) is equivalent to an iterative weighted least-squares procedure. If the current estimate of $\beta$ is denoted by $\hat{\beta}_{(S)}$, the improved estimate $\hat{\beta}_{(S+1)}$ is

```
$$\hat{\beta}_{(S+1)} = \hat{\beta}_{(S)} + (\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^T(\mathbf{y}-\mu). \quad (2.22)$$
```

All the terms except for the vector of observations $\mathbf{y}$ in the right-hand side of (2.22) are evaluated at $\hat{\beta}_{(S)}$ the $n\times k$ matrix of first derivatives

```
$$\mathbf{D} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial \mu_1}{\partial \beta_1} & \dots & \frac{\partial \mu_1}{\partial \beta_k} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial \mu_n}{\partial \beta_1} & \dots & \frac{\partial \mu_n}{\partial \beta_k} \end{pmatrix}^T,
```

$\mathbf{V} = (\mathbf{d}_1, \dots, \mathbf{d}_n),$ the $n\times n$ diagonal matrix of variance

```
$\mathbf{V} = \text{Diag}(\nu(\mu_1), \dots, \nu(\mu_n)) = \text{Diag}(v_1, \dots, v_n),$
```

and $\mathbf{\mu}$ the $n$-dimensional column vector of means $\mathbf{\mu}=(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n)$. Using these notations, (2.22) can be written as

```
$$\hat{\beta}_{(S+1)} = \hat{\beta}_{(S)} + \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n} \mathbf{d}_j v_j^{-1} \mathbf{d}_j^T\right)^{-1} \sum_{j=1}^{n} v_j^{-1} (\mathbf{y}_j -\mu)$$
```

Note that $\mathbf{d}_j$ is a k-dimensional column vector whereas the vector of covariates $\mathbf{x}_j$, as previously defined, a k-dimensional row vector. If the canonical link is assumed, the maximum likelihood score equations (2.19) become

```
$X^T(\mathbf{y}-\mu) = 0$.
```

In this case, equations (2.22) and (2.23) can be simplified further. The iterative procedure simplifies to a point it can be easily implemented in any computer language. Under canonical link we then have

```
$$\hat{\beta}_{(S+1)} = \hat{\beta}_{(S)} + (X^T\mathbf{V}X)^{-1} X^T(\mathbf{y}-\mu)$$
```

which can be written as

```
$$\hat{\beta}_{(S+1)} = \hat{\beta}_{(S)} + \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n} \mathbf{x}_j^T v_j^{-1} \mathbf{x}_j\right)^{-1} \sum_{j=1}^{n} v_j^{-1} (\mathbf{y}_j -\mu)$$
```

Note that under canonical link the Hessian does not involve $V$ and therefore will coincide with $I$, the FIM. Thus, the Newton-Raphson and the Scoring Method would be identical. This is indeed a pleasant result. The variance-covariance matrix of the MLE is estimated by this Hessian or the FIM, depending on the method of estimation.

- We will use a very well-researched approach to illustrate the computations in logit and probit regression models with covariates. We also show the use of some diagnostic plots available in GLIMMIX procedure which are useful in practice for assessing the quality of fit. In Dosage-Mortality studies we focus on the effect of a drug, the dosage usually expressed as a function of its logarithm. When the observed percents are plotted against the dosage (or the log of the dosage), the resulting plot resembles the sigmoid or S-shape of a cumulative probability function. Bliss (1933, 1935) assumed that such shape corresponded to a cumulative Normal distribution function (Probit model). On the other hand, Berkson (1944) found the use of a cumulative Logistic distribution function (Logit model) more appropriate. The term "probit" is attributed to Bliss, whereas the term "logistic" is attributed to Pearl and Reed (1920) and embraced by Berkson in his 1944 paper. The probit and logistic analyses of dosage-mortality data are early examples of GLMs. These types of models are also known as Quantal Response Models.

We first fit a logistic regression model, which is the same as fitting a GLM with Binomial distribution and log link. Under certain regularity, maximum likelihood estimators enjoy optimal properties. They are unbiased or asymptotically unbiased. MLEs are asymptotically efficient in the sense that they achieve the Cramer-Rao lower bound when the sample size tends to infinity. This means that no other unbiased estimator has a lower asymptotic mean squared error than the MLE. Furthermore, they are asymptotically normal; that is, as the sample size increases, the distribution of the MLE tends to the Gaussian distribution.
