# 4.6 An Example with Secondary Association of Chromosomes

Skellam (1948) analyzed a data set on the secondary association of chromosomes in Brassica, a group of Old World temperate herbs of the mustard family with bearded cylindrical poles (similar to cabbage). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first example in the literature where the Beta-binomial distribution was formally used to model overdispersion. At meiosis, the special process of cell division, there are three pairs of bivalents. A bivalent is a structure consisting of two paired homologous chromosomes. The paired chromosomes in each bivalent may or not show association. For a nucleus $j=1,2,...,n$, let $T_j$ represent the total number of pairs of bivalents showing association. Each $T_j$ takes one of the values $\{0,1,2,3\}$. If the probability of association is constant for all nuclei and is the same for all pairs of bivalents, and they act independently of each other, the Binomial distribution would be appropriate for this problem. We will see that Binomial distribution does not fit the data and that overdispersion is present. Only the Beta-binomial, the Random-clumped Binomial and the Generalized Binomial with Additive Interaction provide reasonable fits to the chromosome data.
