## 1.2 Overdispersion

Consider a study where experimental units are clusters, and where each element unit within the cluster is classified into two mutually exclusive categories. If we assume elemental units are independent with a common probability of success, the Binomial model is appropriate for analyzing the data. Clustering creates a lack of independence among elemental units. The probability of success may vary from one element unit to another within a cluster. Consequently, the data usually exhibit larger variances than the variance permitted by the Binomial model. This phenomenon, as previously stated, is known as **overdispersion or extra variation.**

Overdispersion might be caused by other factors. Omitted variables in the model can cause overdispersion. That is, the response probability is in fact a function of several covariates, the model contains a subset of them. This lack of fit manifests itself as overdispersion. Some of the diagnostic plots presented later are affected by this overdispersion.

Overdispersion is also observed on multinomial responses (on count data) analyzed under a Multinomial (Poisson) model may be greater than what can be attributed to the sampling variation assumed by the model. McCullough and Nelder (1989, pp. 124-125) point out:

> "Overdispersion is not uncommon in practice. In fact, some would maintain that overdispersion is the norm in practice and normal dispersion"

The phenomenon of **underdispersion** occurs less frequently. Cox and Snell (1988, p. 107) point out that "underdispersion is sometimes observed, although this is uncommon." It might take place when there is a competition for a positive response. In a case where a person is tasting sweet drinks, he or may feel the second drink is not so sweet—even if it is indeed. While overdispersion occurs here, this phenomenon will not be treated here.

As discussed in Stigler (1986), examples of overdispersion arising from a clustered population can be traced in the literature as far back as 1986. Examples of Multinomial models with overdispersion may arise (in a teratological study of a genetic trait which is passed with a certain probability to offspring of the same mother); in reproductive toxicity experiments where chemicals are administered to litres and responses are measured on individuals fetches; (c) in experiments where individuals receiving treatments are allowed to interact with each other via support groups, as in stop-smoking programs or stop-drinking programs; (d) in household surveys where respondents within a household (or a neighborhood) may be strongly influenced by one or two individuals.

There are many papers that contain examples which discuss and illustrate the overdispersion phenomenon and its consequences on erroneous inferences due to inflated Type I error rates. Some relevant examples in the areas of teratology and toxicology are given in Williams (1975, 1982), Williams et al. (1988), Kupper and Hashem (1978), Hashem and Kupper (1979), Kupper et al. (1986), Shirley and Hickling (1981), Paul (1982), Park (1986), Lefkopoulou et al. (1989), Ryan (1992), Liang and McCullough (1993), Carr and Porter (1993), Donner et al. (1994), Morel and Koehler (1995), and Morel and Neerchal (1997). Important examples of overdispersion are found in Rosner (1984) and Donner (1989) in ophthalmology, Altham (1977) and Cohen (1976) in studies of hospitalized siblings pairs, Crowter (in the analysis of seed germination, Heckman and Wills (1977) in connection with a panel study, Efron (1986) in an epidemiology example involving toxoplasmosis in 34 cities of El Salvador, and Moore (1987) in the modeling of chromosome aberration in survivors of the atomic bomb of Hiroshima.

Early remarks regarding overdispersion under the Poisson model can be found in Student (1919). Examples of Poisson with overdispersion can be found (a) in the analysis of counts and rates of longitudinal studies, and (b) in behavioral studies and in studies of numbers where there is inter-subject variability.

## 1.3 A Common Characterization of Overdispersion

The phenomenon of overdispersion can be characterized in terms of the first moments. Suppose that T is a binomial variable, then

$$E(T) = m\pi$$

where $\pi$ represents the probability of success. Furthermore, if the units are independent, the variance is

$$Var(T) = m\pi(1-\pi)$$

However, if the units are clustered, the variance is larger. If we let $\rho^2$ be the overdispersion parameter, then

$$Var(T) = m\pi(1-\pi)(1+\rho^2)$$

where $0 \le \rho^2 < \infty$. Similarly, the definition of overdispersion can be extended to the case of Multinomial distribution. A cluster consisting of m elemental units where the elemental units are classified into $(d+1)$ categories. Let T denote the $d$-dimensional vector of counts, corresponding to the first $d$ categories, where $i$-th component represents the number of elemental units classified into the $i$-th category. With clustered data, the vector T, which is usually modeled by a Multinomial distribution, often exhibit higher variability in the data than was allowed by the Multinomial model. In order to accommodate this overdispersion, the following moment structure is usually hypothesized:

$$E(T) = m\mu$$

$$Var(T) = m[Diag(\pi) - \pi\pi^T\{1+\rho^2\}$$

where $\pi = (\pi_1, \pi_2, ..., \pi_d)$ is a vector of probabilities, $0 < \pi_i < 1$, $\sum\pi_i \le 1$, and $\rho$ (or $\rho^2$) is the overdispersion parameter. If the multinomial-type T has mean and variance-covariance matrix it is said to have overdispersion relative to the Multinomial distribution. When $\rho=0$ the variance estimators in equations (1.2) and (1.4) reduce to that of the Binomial/Multinomial model.

Recall that for a Poisson random Y, $E(Y) = Var(Y) = \mu, \mu > 0$. Poisson random variable is a count of the number of occurrences of an event in a given time interval. As in the binomial case, there are underlying assumptions on the process for deriving the properties of a Poisson random variable. When these assumptions are not met, overdispersion may arise. When Y is a Poisson-type of random variable, Y is said to have overdispersion if its first two moments are of the form

$$E(Y) = \mu, Var(Y) = \mu(1+\rho^2)$$
