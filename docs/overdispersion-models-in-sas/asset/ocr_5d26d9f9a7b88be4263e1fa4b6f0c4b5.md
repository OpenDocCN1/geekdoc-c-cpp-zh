In case of LMMs ((10.6) and (10.7)), the model given by equation (10.9) is called Population Average (PA) as stated earlier. Similarly, the conditional specification in Equations (10.10) and (10.11) is known as Subject Specific (SS). Such models are most suitable when the response for an individual (rather description of the population as a whole) is the focus of the study, as in studies of growth curves. Population averages are most useful in epidemiological studies. Here, the focus is in studying the difference between responses of groups. A salient example given by Zeger, Liang, and Albert (1988) is as follows: Suppose $x_i$ indicates whether or not the subject $j$ at time $t$ smokes, and represents the presence or absence of a respiratory infection. The PA model would then describe the difference in infection rates between smokers and non-smokers, whereas the SS model estimates the expected change in the probability of infection of a given subject in changing behavior. The G-side and R-side distinction plays a much more important role in GLMM. (2019) provides a conditional model or G-side random effects specification for a GLMM. In LMM, the G-side random effect enters the mean function; therefore, it is amenable to specific interpretation. Note that does not contain an error term. So, the R-side random effects are incorporated by using a working correlation matrix for the marginal distribution of the response. It is worth noting that assuming an overdispersion model, such as DM or RC for the response vector, will be like specifying an R-side random effect. As commented earlier, this could be referred to as D-side specification to emphasize the advantage of a likelihood.

An estimation of GLMM can be done, in principle, by obtaining (by integration) the marginal (unconditional) likelihood of the data and then proceeding with likelihood maximization. In the case of LMMs, closed form for likelihood estimates may not be available. Havville (1977) implements this approach by assuming a normal distribution for the random effects and a normal distribution for the conditional data on the random effects. Anderson and Aiken (1985) and Gianola (1998) use maximum likelihood estimation in logistic and binomial models, where response is binomial. By general, being able to obtain estimates for GLMM depends on whether or not the model (unconditional) distribution of $y_j$ can be obtained by integrating with a justifiable distribution of the random effects. Zeger and Karim (1991) use the connection between GLMM and the Bayesian network to give a method of estimation that overcomes the computational computations of these integrals is to approximate the integrals by simple expansions or by Laplace's method of approximating integrals. This approach has been explored by Siratelli et al. (1994) and Breslow and Clayton (1993), Zeger, Liang, and Albert (1988) use the generalized equations approach to estimate GLMM. Shall (1991) gives an algorithm for estimation in GLMM which is easy to program. This algorithm also accommodates the specification of just the first two moments of the random effect.
