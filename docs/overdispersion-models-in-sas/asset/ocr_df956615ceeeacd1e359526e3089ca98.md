The issue of degrees of freedom of the GOF warrants some discussion. Note that Gof test statistic is computed based on the maximum likelihood estimates, which are obtained from the raw data. On the other hand, the distributional result that the degrees of freedom of the GOF is $\nu=R-3$ is based on the assumption that the statistic is computed using the estimates from the grouped data. As illustrated in Chernoff and Lehmann (1954) and as discussed in Chapter 30 of Kendall, Stuart, and Ord (1991) and in Satradhar et al. (2008), there seems to be a recovery of degrees of freedom due to using the more efficient maximum likelihood estimator. Recommended degrees of freedom of our proposed GOF test lies between $\nu_l=(R-1)-(k+h)$ and $
u_u=(R-1)$. The lower bound $
u_l$ corresponds to the case where the parameters are estimated, whereas the upper $
u_u$ corresponds to the case when all the parameters are known. The results of the Simulation 5.4 support the use of $\nu=(R-1)-2$ degrees of freedom when the probability of success $\pi$ and the overdispersion parameter $\rho$ are common among the clusters.

We illustrate the use of GOF test when using the present data from the epidemiological study conducted by Awa et al. (1971, 1978) on chromosome aberrations in survivors of the atomic bombs exploded in Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The study was conducted from January 1968 to November 1969 in Hiroshima and from January 1968 to March 1971 in Nagasaki. The cases were selected from the A-bomb survivors who were resident in Hiroshima and Nagasaki for whom tentative radiation dose estimates were available. The controls consisted of two groups: those who were not in the cities at the time of bombing and those who were exposed to the bombs but estimated doses were less than 1 rad. Blood samples were taken and cytogenetic examinations were made on n=668 subjects from Hiroshima and n=403 from Nagasaki. We will focus on the Hiroshima data only; we would like to thank Prof. D. Moore for providing the data for this example. A total of 30 to 100 lymphocytes were examined from each subject and the number of chromosome aberrations (j-th subject) were recorded. Let $d$, denote, in rad, the radiation dose received by the unit of exposure in this study is believed to require more than one hit to cause an aberration, whereas a single hit is sufficiently to detect it. The total radiation dose considered here is the sum of the gamma and neutron components. Dose estimates exceeding 600 rad were reduced to 600. Prentice (1986) analyzed the chromosome aberrations data using an extended Beta-binomial model that allows for some degree of overdispersion. Moore (1987) extended the maximum likelihood estimation method for the Beta-binomial and a method based on the mean and variance to allow the modeling of the extra-binomial component as a function of the mean. In Prentice' estimates of the radiation dose received were grouped, whereas in Moore' these estimates were used without doing any grouping of the data.
