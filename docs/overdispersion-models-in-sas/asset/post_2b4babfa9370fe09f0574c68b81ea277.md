Morel and Koehler (1995) proposed a methodology that allows for different levels of overdispersion (or underdispersion) in the components of multinomial responses. This approach can be viewed as a generalization of the single scale inflation based on the Pearson's Chi-square statistic. They introduce category-specific scaling factors by replacing (7.16)

$$Var ((\mathbf{T}_j) = m_j \mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_j) \mathbf{C}^T(\boldsymbol{\pi}_j), \quad (7.20)$$

where $\mathbf{\Phi}$ is a diagonal matrix $\mathbf{\Phi} = \text{Diag}(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_k)$ and $\Delta(\boldsymbol{\pi}_j) = \mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_j)\mathbf{C}^T(\boldsymbol{\pi}_j)$. The case when $\Phi$ is an unknown $d \times d$ covariance could also be considered. However, we will focus only on the case where $\mathbf{\Phi}$ is a diagonal matrix and $\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_j)$ is the Cholesky decomposition of $\Delta(\boldsymbol{\pi}_j)$. This has a straightforward interpretation, as we will see here. In the context of quasi-likelihoods, variance structure (7.20) is a meaningful extension of the single scale inflation based on the Pearson's Chi-square statistic to multinomial responses. In particular, different degrees of overdispersion can be accommodated by different categories. ## 7.6 A Birth Defects Study

Hartsfield et al. (1992) studied the effects of prenatal exposure to cadmium on death and malformation of hamster fetuses. Data were obtained from a completely randomized experiment with pregnant female hamsters (litters) as the primary units. Nine days after the hamsters became pregnant they intravenously received a solution containing one of the following substances: 2 mg kg⁻¹ of zinc, 2 mg kg⁻¹ of cadmium, 3 mg kg⁻¹ of cadmium, 2 mg kg⁻¹ of zinc and 3 mg kg⁻¹ of cadmium. In addition to these groups, there was a control group consisting of 10 pregnant females and 10 pregnant females injected a saline solution (injected controls). Each female was sacrificed five days later and each fetus into one of three categories: “dead,” “alive with malformation,” and “alive without any malformation.” The data are reported in Table 7.2. Morel and Koehler (1995) analyzed these data using a one-step Gauss-Newton estimator that accounts for overdispersion in the model. One-step Gauss-Newton estimators have a long history among econometricians, for example, Chapter 5.5 of Fuller, 1976). For illustrative purposes we will use the customary cumulative link and the variance structure (7.20) with $\Phi = \text{Diag}(\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots, \phi_4)$. Following Morel and Koehler (1995), the untreated and injected controls were combined into a single control group.
