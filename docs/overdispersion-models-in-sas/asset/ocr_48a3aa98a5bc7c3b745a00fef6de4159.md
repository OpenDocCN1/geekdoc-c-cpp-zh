This is a consistent estimator of the true underlying variance-covariance matrix, even when the correlation of the repeated measurements is mismeasured, and hence the name "robust." The use of "sandwich" estimators is well-known to sampling practitioners. Binder (1983) and Liang and Zeger (1986) discuss the estimation of the variance-covariance matrix of the parameters with non-linear link functions, such as the log function, to model the mean of a categorical response variable. The "sandwich" estimator is justified by a Taylor series expansion (or the Delta method) as discussed by Binder (1983). Liang and Zeger obtain the "sandwich" estimator under the GEE set. Their approach justifies applying the GEE to the exponential family of response variables and expands the robustness argument to include a wide range of models. Hence, the estimator is called the "Delta method estimator" in the sampling literature; the estimator is denoted as "GEE methodology" in the biostatistical literature.

As originally presented, GEE methodology is derived under a very general set up for the exponential family of distributions. Explicit distributional assumptions do not play a direct role in the implementation of GEE other than in the computation of the first moments. Since overdispersion data are characterized by the specification of the first moments, and the overdispersion parameter captures the within-cluster correlation, GEE may be used naturally to obtain estimates of the mean, variance and the overdispersion parameters. Of course, it also opens up further research questions for incorporating higher moments to obtain a better fit, as explored in Newcomer (2007). Thus, we devote a chapter in this book for explaining GEE and its application to overdispersion data.
