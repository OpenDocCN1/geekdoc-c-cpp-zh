It is important that the GENMOD procedure does not handle random effects of any type. Terms as G-side or R-side are not present in the theoretical discussion of the GENMOD procedure ("Details: GENMOD Procedure" of the SAS Documentation). Readers may recall that the GENMOD procedure is essentially devoted to regression in the exponential family. It can also accommodate the Zero-inflated Poisson and the Zero-inflated Negative-binomial models, neither of which is a member of the exponential family. In GENMOD, repeated measurements can be analyzed in conjunction with the GEE methodology following the original guidelines in Liang and Zeger (1986), and Zeger and Liang (1986). The distinction, as we will see later, between GENMOD and GLIMMIX is more evident in the two procedures practically implement using the GEE methodology. The procedure relies on Quasi-likelihood and moment estimation, where the latter relies upon the Pseudo-Likelihood estimation based on linearization. However from some minor variations, both procedures basically incurred in the computation of the "sandwich" estimator.

The GEE methodology is a two-step estimation process. To begin, with a type of Quasi-likelihood model is stated when one assumes correlation or variance structure on the repeated measurements. The parameters of this initial model (working model) are estimated. Once the working model is fit, the "robust," "empirical," or "sandwich" variance-covariance matrix is then estimated. To understand how the "sandwich" variance arises, consider for a moment the set-up of Generalized Linear Models discussed in Chapter 2. We know the beta estimates are estimated iteratively around $\beta$ in equation (2.22), that is,

$$\hat{\beta}_{(s+1)} = \hat{\beta}_{(s)} + (\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})$$

Then, the Taylor expansion of $\hat{\beta}$ around $\beta$ yields

$$\hat{\beta}-\beta \triangleq (\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})$$

The variance-covariance matrix of $\hat{\beta}$ from the Taylor's expansion, would be

$$Var(\hat{\beta}-\beta) \triangleq Var(\hat{\beta}) \triangleq (\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D})^{-1}.$$

If we define $\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}$ by $\mathbf{H}$, and $\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{D}$ by $\mathbf{G}$, the above variance-covariance matrix can be written as

$$Var(\hat{\beta}) \triangleq \mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{H}^{-1}.$$

Now we turn to the Pseudo-likelihood Estimation by Linearization, used by the GLIMMIX procedure. This method is best motivated using the basic setup of a linear mixed model (see section "Mixed Models Theory" in the documentation of the MIXED procedure). Therefore, we start with a brief introduction. In general, a linear mixed model is written as

$$y = X\beta + Z\gamma + \varepsilon, \quad (9.5)$$

where, in general linear models, $X$ represents the design matrix (or the matrix of known covariates), and $\beta$ is an unknown vector of parameters. The vector $\beta$ represents the parameters of the fixed part of the model. The parameters associated with the random part of the model are represented by the unknown vector $\gamma$. The $Z$ contains either continuous or dummy variables, as also seen with $X$. See McCulloch and Searle (2001) for an account of the historical developments of the mixed model. It is assumed that the random vectors $\gamma$ and $\varepsilon$ are jointly and normally distributed with mean

$$\begin{pmatrix} \gamma \\ \varepsilon \end{pmatrix}$$

and variance-covariance matrix

$$Var\begin{pmatrix} \gamma \\ \varepsilon \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} G & 0 \\ 0 & R \end{pmatrix}$$

Notations used in the previous expression are the effects of the G-side and R-side, used here to describe the two different ways of incorporating random effects. If $y$ in equation (9.5) represents a realization of the random vector $Y$, it can be shown that

$$Var(Y) = GZ^TG + R.$$

$Var(Y)$ can be modeled by properly defining the variance-covariance structures of $G$ and $R$. Because of the additive nature of the relationship among $G$, $R$, and $Var(Y)$, many mixed models will have equivalent G-side and R-side specifications, as illustrated in the hair growth example of 9.2.

The general set-up of a Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) is

$$E(y|\gamma) = g^{-1}(X\beta + Z\gamma) = g^{-1}(\eta) = \mu, \quad (9.6)$$

where $\gamma \sim \text{Normal}(0, G)$, $g(\cdot)$ is a link function, $g^{-1}(\cdot)$ its inverse, and $\eta = X\beta + Z\gamma$ the linear predictor. The variance of $Y$ on the random effect $\gamma$

$$\text{Var}(Y|\gamma) = A^{1/2}RA^{1/2}, \quad (9.7)$$

where $A$ is a diagonal matrix that contains the variance functions of the model. As GLM, the variance function expresses the variance of a response as a function of the mean. namely, from the DIST= option in the MODEL statement, or from the user-supplied variance function the matrix $R$ is specified by the RANDOM statement. This matrix must not be confused with the R-side previously described. If the conditional distribution of the data contains an additional scale parameter, it is either part of the variance functions or part of the R matrix. This will not be the case in the models discussed here since they all have $\phi=1$. The method of Pseudo-likelihood estimation based on linearization to estimate the parameters of GLMM in (9.6) was proposed by Wolfgang and O’Connell (1993) based on a first-order Taylor expansion of $\mu$ around some initial estimators $\hat{\beta}$ and $\hat{\gamma}$. This boils down to

$$P = X\beta + \varepsilon^*, \quad (9.8)$$

where $P = \Delta^{-1}(\mu-g^{-1}(\eta)) + X\beta$, $\Delta = \frac{\partial g^{-1}(\eta)}{\partial \eta} = \hat{\Delta}$, $\varepsilon^* \sim \text{Normal}(0, \Sigma)$ and

$$\text{Var}(P) = \Sigma^* = \hat{\Delta}^{-1}A^{1/2}RA^{1/2}\hat{\Delta}^{-1}$$ corresponds to a mixed model. The reader can see this mixed with the G-side $Z\gamma$. As a result of Wolfgang and O’Connell’s (1993) terminology, the variance-covariance matrix $\Sigma^*$ represents the R-side of (9.8). As previously stated, the $R$ in $\Sigma^*$ is specified by the user through the RANDOM statement.

Common covariance matrix structures available in GLIMMIX include:
- VC (Variance components)
- CHOL (Cholesky parameterization)
- UN (Unstructured)
- AR (Autoregressive)
- CS (Exchangeable)
