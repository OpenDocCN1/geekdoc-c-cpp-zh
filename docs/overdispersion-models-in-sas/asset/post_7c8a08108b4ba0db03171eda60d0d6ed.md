Lawless (1987) found evidence against the Poisson model and overdispersion was present in the model. Lawless then fitted a Binomial model with random effects. The type of model, which belongs to the family of Generalized Linear Mixed Models, will be discussed later in the book. For illustrative purposes we now fit a Quasi-like Poisson model. The programming statements are found in Program 3.4. This time, we make use of the GENMOD procedure and request LS-Means for the Control and Retinoid groups. An estimate of the Retinoid effect is made as well. The main results are provided in Output 3.4.

# Program 3.4: Fitting an Overdispersed Poisson Model--Tumor Data of Table 3.4

```
/*
Number of Tumors in Rats
Example from Lawless (1987) and Gail et al. (1980)
*/
data number_of_tumors;
  input group $ n 88;
  do retid=1 to n;
    input y 88;
    output;
  end;
  drop n;
datalines;
  Retinoid 23 1 0 2 1 4 3 6 1 1 5 2 1 5 2 3 4 5 4 5 1 2
  Control 25 7 11 9 2 9 4 6 7 6 1 13 2 1 10 4 5 4 5 11 11 9 12 1 3 3 3
;
ods html;
proc genmod data=number_of_tumors;
  class group;
  model y = group / dist=poisson link=log scale=pearson;
  lsmeans group;
    estimate "Logarithm Ratio Control Vs Retinoid" group 1 -1;
run;
ods html close;
```

# 3.6 Patients at High Risk for Vertebral Fractures

We now turn to Quasi-likelihood estimation in models using offsets. An offset is technically defined as a linear predictor with coefficient one. Offsets are useful in Poisson models since they normalize the mean. A typical example of offset is "duration of exposure". Occurrences of counts of a certain event depend on duration of exposure: the longer the exposure, the more likely we observe larger counts. In this example we assume the form of overdispersion as $E(Y)=\mu$ and $Var(Y)=\mu\phi, \phi > 1$. Note that this variance structure can be written as $Var(Y)=\mu(1+\kappa), \kappa>0$, which is one form of overdispersion for the Poisson model discussed in Dean (1992).

In a clinical study of osteoporotic patients at risk for vertebral fractures, subjects were randomly assigned to either a group receiving bisphosphonate therapy (n=28) or to a placebo group (n=25). The number of vertebral fractures (new or worsening) was measured during a period of time that lasted approximately 14 to 39 months. The data are presented in 3.6 which has been discussed in Morel and Nelich (2010). Let $Y_j$ and $Z_j$ represent the number of vertebral fractures and the years of follow-up for the $j$-th subject in the $i$-th treatment group, $j=1, 2, \dots, n$. Conditioning on $Z_i$, $E(Y_j)=\mu_j/Z_j$, $Var(Y_j)=\mu_j/Z_j^2, \phi > 1$, and $ln(\mu_j/Z_j) = \alpha + \tau$, where $\alpha$ is an overall mean and $\tau$ the effect of the $i$-th treatment group. (duration of follow-up) is an offset, $Z_i > 0$. The link function can be written as $ln(\mu_j) = \alpha + \tau + ln(Z_i)$. Including the offset variable in the model leads to estimation of rate of vertebral fractures normalized by the number of years follow up. In this example we will use the GENMOD. The estimate of the scale parameter will be based on Pearson’s Chi-square statistic. The programming statements are provided in Program 3.5. There are three estimates. They provide the logarithm of the rates of the two groups and their difference, along with the associated ratios and rate ratios. The results are reported in Program 3.5. The estimate of the scale parameter turns out to be 1.7311, indicating the presence of dispersion.
