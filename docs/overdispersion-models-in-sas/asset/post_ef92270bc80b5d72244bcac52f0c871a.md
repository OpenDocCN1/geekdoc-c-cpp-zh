While comparing the presentation here with Cohen's original work, it is important to note a crucial change in notation. Note that from equation (6.9) the probability of $y=0$ is $\omega(1-\omega)f(0)$, whereas in Cohen's notation, the same probability is given by $(1-\omega)+\omega f(0)$. We follow the parameterization in Section 2.2, Chapter 8 of Johnson et al. (1992), which is consistent with the notations used in GENMOD and COUNTREG. In Cohen's paper, $\omega$ represented "the proportion of the infected or susceptible group in the population," which was associated with the probability function $f(\cdot)$. Our parameterization permits us to interpret $\omega$ as the Zero-inflation Probability measure. This family of distributions allows one to model an excess of zeros and is readily applicable to both the Poisson and the Negative-binomial distributions. It is possible to take $\omega$ negative, provided that

$$\omega \ge \frac{-f(0)}{1-f(0)} \quad (6.10)$$

In such a case the proportion of zeros associated with $f(y;alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_s)$ are reduced.

If $f(y;\mu)$ corresponds to the probability function of a Poisson distribution with parameter $\mu$, it follows from (6.9) that $Y$ is a Zero-inflated Poisson distribution (ZIP) with parameters $(\mu, \omega)$, its probability function is

$$Pr(Y=y) = \omega I(y=0)+(1-\omega)\frac{e^{-\mu y}}{\Gamma(y+1)}, y=0,1,\dots \quad (6.11)$$

$I(\cdot)$ is an indicator function. The reader should note that there are two sources of zeros in (6.10), arising from a zero-degeneration distribution with probability $\omega$, and the other one from a Poisson($\mu$) with probability $(1-\omega)$. Note that, $Pr(Y=0)=\omega+(1-\omega)e^{-\mu}$, which is always greater than or equal to $e^{-\mu}$ for $\omega>0$. Similarly, for $\mu<0$, as in (6.10), with probability of observing zero under the ZIP model will be smaller than the probability of zero under the Poisson distribution. The mean and variance of the ZIP distribution can be shown to be

$$E(Y) = (1-\omega)(\mu+\mu^2) \quad (6.12)$$

and

$$Var(Y) = (1-\omega)(\mu+\mu^2) \quad (6.13)$$

Recall that the standard deviation for count data is the Poisson distribution, and that overdispersion is present when the variance of the Poisson-type random variable is larger than that. From $Var(Y)=(1-\omega)(\mu+\mu^2) > (1-\omega)E(Y)$, there is an excess of zeros in the Poisson

Similarly, the probability function (6.9) can be also extended to the Negative-binomial distribution. If $f(\mu,\kappa)$ corresponds to the probability function of a NB distribution with parameters $(\mu,\kappa)$, then the probability function of a Zero-inflated Negative-binomial (ZINB) with parameters $(\mu,\kappa)$ is given

$$Pr(Y=y)=\omega I(y=0)+(1-\omega)\frac{\Gamma(y+\frac{1}{\kappa})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{\kappa})}\left(\frac{\mu}{1+\kappa}\right)^y,y=0,1,\dots \quad (14)$$

In the case of the ZIP distribution, zeros in the ZINB distribution arise from different sources, namely a zero-degenerate distribution with probability $\omega$, and from a Negative-binomial($\mu,\kappa$) with probability $(1-\omega)$. Observe that distributions (6.10) and (6.13) are both finite mixture distributions. Show that the mean and variance of the ZINB distribution are

$$E(Y)=(1-\omega)\mu \quad (15)$$

and

$$Var(Y)=(1-\omega)(\mu(1+\kappa)+\omega^2\} \quad (16)$$

In the case of the ZIP distribution, the fact that $Var(Y)=(1-\omega)(\mu+\omega^2)$ indicates that an excess of zeros in the NB distribution implies overdispersion. Again, overdispersion refers to comparing the mean with the variance. As in the case of Poisson, there is no direct comparison for variance of ZINB and NB, which exhibits more variability than ZIP while retaining the same proportion of excess zeros. This is analogous to the fact NB exhibits overdispersion relative to Poisson.

## 6.4 Example of Number of Eggs of the Intestinal Schistosoma Mansoni

Trematodes are a class of flatworms which includes both external and internal parasites of animal hosts that have a thick outer cuticle and one or more suckers or hooks for attaching to tissue. They are also called fluke. Among the trematodes Schistosoma Mansoni causes a disease known as Schistosomiasis (also known as bilhariasis or snail fever). This disease is most commonly found in Asia, Africa, and South America, especially in areas where the freshwater waters may carry the parasite. Cohen (1966) analyzed a sample counts of the number of eggs of the trematode Schistosoma Mansoni, on single slides, collected from 926 inhabitants of an Egyptian village. The population sampled consists of a mixture of an infected group with a non-infected group.

We will make use of the COUNTREG procedure in order to get the maximum likelihood estimates under both the NB and the ZINB. COUNTREG assumes the logarithm link on the parameter $\mu$ and identity link on the dispersion parameter $\kappa$; it allows the user to select either a log or logit link on the parameter $\omega$. output out=exp_nb prob=prob_nb;
