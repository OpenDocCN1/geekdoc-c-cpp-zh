## 4.1 Introduction

In this chapter we will discuss several overdispersion models using the proper likelihood functions for binomial data. These models, applicable to binomial responses (i.e., number of successes “T” out of “m trials”), are obtained by postulating different sets of assumptions corresponding to different mechanisms of underlying overdispersion. One family of the models is obtained by assuming that the probabilities of success of responses from one to another, according to a certain distribution with domain in the zero-one interval. The unconditional distribution of T is then an overdispersed distribution with a likelihood function that allows for the joint estimation of probability of success and the overdispersion parameter. Another approach is modeling meaningfully the random mechanism that induces correlation among the Bernoulli outcomes which add up to the binomial-response type, the lack of independence among the Bernoulli outcomes results in overdispersion. While the likelihood functions of these two families look substantially different from one another, they can both be viewed as mixtures, either finite or infinite, of Binomial distributions.

An important aspect of these models is the clear connections with GLMs. The likelihood models discussed in this chapter allow for link functions (logit, probit, etc) in the probability of success and the overdispersion parameter. In principle, two linear predictors are possible: the customary one with the mean of the model, and the one associated with the overdispersion parameter. We will first convert the main concepts by using simple examples to show the functions. The estimation methods employed here are highly similar to those used in GLMs. The maximum likelihood equations of these models are highly non-linear in the parameters; therefore, we will have to rely on iterative methods for computing the MLEs. Although the simplifications discussed in equations (2.23) and (2.24) of Chapter 2 for distributions in the exponential family with canonical links is not known to hold for the overdispersed models discussed in this chapter, both estimation methods, the Newton-Raphson and Fisher Scoring methods, can be implemented for the MLE of the parameters of the model.

## 4.2 The Beta-binomial distribution

Modeling overdispersion with binomial responses can be approached hierarchically by setting a prior on the probability of success of the Binomial, and then examining the variability in the count marginally. Let T represent the total of successes in a cluster of size m. It is assumed that T |P ~ Binomial(P,m). The probability of success P, 0 < P < 1, be distributed according to an arbitrary distribution with density $f (P)$ (or probability function if the distribution of P is discrete) in the (0,1) interval such that

> E_f(P) = \pi \ (4.1)

and

> V_f(P) = \sigma^2 \ (4.2)

Since $P^2 < P$ for any P between zero and one, it follows that $E_f(P^2) < E_f(P)$. Then $\sigma^2 + \pi^2 < \pi$, which implies $\sigma^2 < \pi(1-\pi)$. Thus, there exists a real number $\rho^2$, $0 < \rho^2 < 1$ such that $\sigma^2 = \rho^2\pi(1-\pi)$. Then, (4.2) can be written as

> V_f(P) = \rho^2\pi(1-\pi). (4.3)

Using standard based on conditional expectations, E(T) = E_f{E(T|P)} and Var(T) = E_f\{V(T|P)\} + V_f\{E(T|P)\}$, we can show that the unconditional mean and variance of T are

> E(T) = m\pi \ (4.4)
