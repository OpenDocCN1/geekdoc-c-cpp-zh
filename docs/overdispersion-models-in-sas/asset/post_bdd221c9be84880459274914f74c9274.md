%Macro DM_RCM_Exact_Approximate(data, label);
title &label;

Proc IML;

use &data;
read all into T;

n = nrow(T);
k = ncol(T);
m = sum(T(1));

Parameter = shape(('PI1') ('PI2') ('PI3') ('PI4') ('PI5')
('PI6') ('PI8') ('PI9'),k-1,1) // ('Rho) // ('Log Likelihood);
FVALUE = j(k,1,0);

mattrib Estimate format=12.5;
mattrib StdErr format=12.6;
mattrib AppStdErr format=12.6;
mattrib Pic_Rel_Error format=12.5;

**************************************/
/* MLE from DM Distribution */
**************************************/
Load Module=MLEM;

thetaMLE = MLEM(m, T);
pIMLE1 = thetaMLE[1:(k-1)];
rhoMLE1 = thetaMLE[k];

INVIM = Inv(*nFIDM(m, pIMLE1, rhoMLE1));
SE1 = j(k,1,0);
Do i 1 to k;
SE1[i] = sqrt(INVIM[i,i]);
End;

ll = 0;
Do j 1 to n;
tj = T[j:(k-1)];
l11 = ll + log(ProbDM(m, pIMLE1, rhoMLE1, tj));
End;

Estimate = pIMLE1 // rhoMLE1 // ll11
StdErr = SE1;
Pct_Rel_Error = StdErr;

**************************************/
/* MLE from DM Distribution using approximate FIM */
**************************************/
Load Module=Stage2DM;

thetaMLE = Stage2DM(m, T);
pIMLE1 = thetaMLE[1:(k-1)];
rhoMLE1 = thetaMLE[k];

INVIM = Inv(*nFIDM(m, pIMLE1, rhoMLE1)); // ---- Updates FIM;
SE1 = j(k,1,0);
Do i 1 to k;
SE1[i,] = sqrt(INVVFIM[i,i]);

ll1 = 0;
Do j = 1 to n;
tj = T[j,1:(k-1)];
ll1 = ll1 + log(ProbRCM(m, pIMEL, rhoME1, tj));

AppStdErr = SEI;
Pct_Rel_Error = 100*abs(Pct_Rel_Error - AppStdErr)/Pct_Rel_Error;
print "Maximum Likelihood Estimates using FIM and Approximate FIM -- Dirichlet-Multinomial Distribution";
print Parameter Estimate StdErr AppStdErr Pct_Rel_Error;

### 9.1 Introduction

"Generalized Estimating Equations," better known simply by its acronym GEE, is a methodology adopted by Liang and Zeger (1986), and Zeger and Liang (1988) for Generalized Linear Models in a longitudinal setting. The methodology is related to Quasi-likelihood models Woodburn (1974) and McCulloch (1983) and Generalized Linear Models [McCullagh and Nelder (1983)]. Although the GEE methodology was originally proposed for longitudinal data analysis, it has been applied to a wide variety of models for cluster data. There are three essential elements of GEE: correct specification of the mean of the model via a link function, the proposed structure of the correlation that encompass the clusters (subjects in

![](img/fa69b64c17ad451eb7eb4ad8d92f5fb_295_0.png) longitudinal studies) referred to as the working correlation matrix, and an application of the Taylor series expansion to obtain the variance estimation which provides the variance-covariance matrix of the estimates, usually known as "robust," "empirical," or "sandwich". This is a consistent estimator of the true underlying variance-covariance matrix, even when the correlation of the repeated measurements is mismeasured, and hence the name "robust." The use of "sandwich" estimators is well-known to sampling practitioners. Binder (1983) and Liang and Zeger (1986) discuss the estimation of the variance-covariance matrix of the parameters with non-linear link functions, such as the log function, to model the mean of a categorical response variable. The "sandwich" estimator is justified by a Taylor series expansion (or the Delta method) as discussed by Binder (1983). Liang and Zeger obtain the "sandwich" estimator under the GEE set. Their approach justifies applying the GEE to the exponential family of response variables and expands the robustness argument to include a wide range of models. Hence, the estimator is called the "Delta method estimator" in the sampling literature; the estimator is denoted as "GEE methodology" in the biostatistical literature.

As originally presented, GEE methodology is derived under a very general set for the exponential family of distributions. Explicit distributional assumptions do not play a direct role in the implementation of GEE other than in the computation of the first moments. Since overdispersion data are characterized by the specification of the first moments, and the overdispersion parameter captures the within-cluster correlation, GEE may be used naturally to obtain estimates of the mean, variance and the overdispersion parameters. Of course, it also opens up further research questions for incorporating higher moments to obtain a better fit, as explored in Newcomer (2007). Thus, we devote a chapter in this book for explaining GEE and its application to overdispersion data.
