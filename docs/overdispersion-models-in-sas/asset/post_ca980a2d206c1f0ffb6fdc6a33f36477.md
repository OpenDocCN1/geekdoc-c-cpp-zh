# 10.1 Generalities and Fundamental Concepts of GLMMs

In this chapter, we pick up the discussion of Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) again and provide a more comprehensive view of how overdispersion models interact with GLMM. Several advanced examples given here illustrate how likelihood-based overdispersion models presented in this book can augment the estimation methodology provided in SAS procedures GLMIX and GENMOD. The diagram below depicts one of the models discussed in this chapter. We will provide Binomial, Poisson, and Multinomial GLMMs as examples. These are conditional models. We will also include four examples of Generalized Linear Overdispersion Mixed Models (GLOMM). They correspond to the Binomial, Random-clumped Binomial, Zero-inflated Poisson, and Zero-inflated Negative models with random effects. By studying these models, we complete all the hierarchical models we promised to cover in this book (see Appendix 1.1 of Chapter 1). We first start with a discussion of few fundamental concepts.

The Generalized Linear Models (GLM) framework provided by McCull and Nelder (1989) unifies the analysis of continuous and discrete response variables when the responses can be assumed to be independent. The GLM framework was extended by Zeger, Liang, and Albert (1998) to include random effects. The resulting models, Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM), can accommodate the analysis of longitudinal data where subjects are monitored over a period of time. GLMM can also be used to analyze observations obtained on different subjects from a cluster, like those usually in cluster sampling. As indicated earlier, overdispersed Binomial (or Multinomial) data are usually a result of natural association among observations from the same cluster. Thus we provide a brief review of GLM in this chapter.

# Chapter 10 Generalized Linear Overdispersion Mixed Models

The extension of GLM to GLMM is straightforward and is analogous to the extension of linear models to linear mixed models. A random effect is introduced, corresponding to each subject to capture the deviation of the subject's response from the group average. We consider only the simplified case of subjects treated as random. Real-life examples might be more complex. In instance, a k-dimensional multi-center study where subjects within centers provide repeated measurements over time, it would be reasonable to treat “centers” and “subjects within centers” as random.

We will first present the GLMM in a very general set for multivariate observations from elemental units clusters. Clusters are here used as a generic term, and in practice represent a subject, a center, or investigator. Let $\mathbf{y}_j$ denote the $d$-dimensional response vector from the $j$-th elemental cluster, $j=1,2,\dots,n$, $j=1,2,\dots,m$; suppose mean vectors and variance-covariance matrices of these response vectors are represented as $E(\mathbf{y}_j) = \mu_j$ and $Var(\mathbf{y}_j) = \Phi(\mu_j)$, where the $i$-th component $\mu_{ij}$ of the mean vector $\mu_j$ depends on a $k$-dimensional row of known covariates $\mathbf{x}_{ij}$ and an unknown $k$-dimensional column vector of parameters $\boldsymbol{\beta}_j$, through a link function $g(\mu_{ij}) = \mathbf{x}_{ij}^T \boldsymbol{\beta}_j + \eta_{ij}$, where $\eta_{ij}$ is the linear predictor. If $\eta_{ij}$ includes random effects, the model becomes a GLMM. In the case of overdispersion, the variance is often modeled as $Var(y_{ij}) = \phi V(\mu_{ij})$, where $\phi$ is the overdispersion parameter. We will now explore how these models are implemented in SAS.
