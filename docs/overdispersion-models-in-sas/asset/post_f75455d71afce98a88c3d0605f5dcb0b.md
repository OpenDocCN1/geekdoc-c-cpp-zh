
# Chapter 4 Likelihood Models for Overdispersed Binomial Responses

## Program 4.1: Maximum Likelihood Estimation of Chromosome Data under Five Distributions

> * Maximum Likelihood Estimation Skellam's (1948) Chromosome Data Example under the following distributions: Binomial, Beta-binomial, Random-clumped Binomial, Zero-inflated Binomial and a Generalized-Binomial Distribution with Additive Interaction

```sas
data chromosome;
m = 3;
input freq;
n+ freq;
if n = 4 then
 call symput('n', n); /* "n" is total number of clusters;
drop n;

title 'Random-clumped Binomial Distribution';
proc nlmixed data=chromosome;
parms Pi=0.4 0.5 0.6 Rho=0.2 0.3 0.4;
bounds 0 < rho < 1;
c = 1 / rho;
z = lgamma(m-t);
pic = 1 - pi;
ll = freq * (z + lambda(c) + lambda(t*c) + lambda(m-t+c)) - lambda(c) - lambda(pi) + lambda(c);
model t = general(ll);
predict n * exp(z + lambda(c) + lambda(t*c) + lambda(m-t+c)) - lambda(c) - lambda(pi) + lambda(c) out=pred;
run;

title 'Zero-Inflated Binomial';
proc nlmixed data=chromosome;
parms Pi=0.4 0.5 0.6 Rho=0.2 0.3 0.4;
bounds 0 < Rho < 1;
theta = rho;
z = lgamma(m-1) - lgamma(t+1);
p = pi + theta * (1-pi);
w = p;
pc = pi / p;
wc = 1 / w;
ll = freq * exp(z + log(w) + t*log(p) + (m-t)*log(pc)) + wc*(t=0);
model t = general(ll);
predict n * (exp(z + log(w) + t*log(p) + (m-t)*log(pc)) + wc*(t=0)) out=pred;
run;

title 'Generalized-Binomial Distribution -- Additive Interaction';
proc nlmixed data=chromosome;
parms Pi=0.4 0.5 0.6 Rho=0.2 0.3 0.4;
alpha = rho * rho;
z = lgamma(m-1) - lgamma(t+1);
pic = 1-pi;
ll = freq * (z + t*log(pi) + (m-t)*log(pic)) + log(alpha/2*(t-1)/pi*(m-t)/pic) - alpha*(m-1)/2 + 2);
model t = general(ll);
predict n * exp(z + t*log(pi) + (m-t)*log(pic) + log(alpha/2*(t-1)/pi*(m-t)/pic) - alpha*(m-1)/2 + 1) out=pred;
run;
ods html close;
```

The maximum likelihood results are reported in Table 4.2. The five models provide similar estimates of the probability $\pi$. In fact, after rounding up three decimals, the estimates are essentially the same, 0.581. It is common to get similar estimates for the mean parameter under both Binomial and overdispersion models. In this example, the Beta-Binomial, Random-clumped Binomial, and Generalized Binomial provide basically identical results for estimates of $\hat{\pi}$ and $\rho$ with corresponding standard errors. The variance estimates of $\hat{\pi}$ under these three models are approximately 1.7 times the estimate obtained by using the Binomial distribution. This factor turns out to be 1.1 under the ZIB model. The Binomial distribution fails to recognize the intrinsic clustering of the data. It is also interesting to note that the maximum value of the likelihood under are almost identical. Note that none of these distributions are nested in the other, and their mathematical formulas do not lend themselves to any specific comparison of this type. The estimates under the ZIB seem to be slightly different. To test for the presence of overdispersion under a specific overdispersion model, we need to test the hypothesis $H_0: \rho = 0$ versus the alternative hypothesis $H_1: \rho > 0$, assuming one of these models to be true. Since all the overdisp considered here reduce to the Binomial distribution under the null hypothesis, the likelihood ratio statistic can be computed by subtracting the maximum value of the likelihood under an overdispersed model from the maximum likelihood value under the Binomial distribution. While computing this test statistic poses no difficulty at all, properties of the likelihood ratio test cannot be on the boundary of the parameter space. Self and Liang (1987) have shown in this case (Case 5 in their paper), the asymptotic distribution of the likelihood ratio test is a 5:50 mixture of a point mass at zero and a Chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom. The likelihood ratio test statistic is 880.8–873.6–7.2 under the Beta-binomial, 7.0 under the Random-clumped Binomial, 3.2 under the ZIB, and 7.3 under the Generalized Binomial with Additive Interaction. Their appropriate P-values are 0.004, 0.004, 0.037, and 0.003, and strongly support the hypothesis of overdispersion under either model.
