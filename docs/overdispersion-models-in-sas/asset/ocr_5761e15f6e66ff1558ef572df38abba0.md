$f(\epsilon_i; 0) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(\frac{(y_i - x_i\mathbf{eta})^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$

The MLE $\hat{\theta} = (\hat{\beta}, \hat{\sigma}^2)$ is obtained by maximizing the logarithm of the likelihood function, that is, first taking partial derivatives of the log-likelihood with respect to $\beta$ and with respect to $\sigma^2$, and then equating those derivatives to zero to obtain solutions for $\beta$ and $\sigma^2$, respectively. The derivative of the log-likelihood is known as the **score function**. It can be shown that the MLE of $\hat{\beta}$ is

```
$$\hat{\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$ (2.8)
```

which is precisely the OLS estimator $\hat{\beta}$ in equation (2.6). The MLE of $\sigma^2$ turns out to be

```
$$\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i\mathbf{eta})^2}{n}$ (2.9)
```

Note that $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{n-k}{n}\sigma^2$, so $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is asymptotically unbiased (consistent). That is, $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is biased, but its bias tends to zero as $n$ increases.

One way to assess the model quality of a linear model is by examining the estimated residuals $\hat{\epsilon}_i = y_i - \sum_{j=1}^k x_{ij}\hat{\beta}_j$. A nice property of the residuals is that they are uncorrelated with the predicted value (linear predictor) $\hat{\y}_i = \sum_{j=1}^k x_{ij}\hat{\beta}_j$. So, a plot (diagnostic plot) of the estimated residuals $\hat{\epsilon}_i$ versus the linear predictors $\hat{\y}_i$ should be randomly scattered without exhibiting any pattern. Persistent patterns in the diagnostic plot suggest an inadequacy or **Lack-of-Fit (LOF)** of the model. Normality of residuals can be visually evaluated via a **Normal Probability Plot**.
