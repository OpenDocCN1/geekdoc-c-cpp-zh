## 1.6 The Exponential Family

The main tasks to be covered in this book, namely Quasi-likelihood Functions, Generalized Linear Models (GLM), Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE), and Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM), are motivated by the generality of the many statistical methods applicable to all members of the exponential family. Therefore, we begin with a brief introduction to the exponential family of densities by outlining some of their properties. The exponential family is a class of density functions sharing certain characteristics and properties. Among the members of this family are the Normal, Bernoulli, Binomial, Multinomial, Poisson, Negative-binomial, Beta, and Gamma distributions. This family of distributions plays a very important role in the classical statistical inference techniques. Following the literature, we use the canonical parameter and canonical link set up for our analysis, but all examples will be in the family notation, which may be or not be canonical.

A density function in this family can be written

```
$f(y) = \exp\left(\frac{y\theta - b(\theta)}{\phi} + c(y, \phi)\right\},\quad (1.8)$
```

$b(\cdot)$ and $c(\cdot)$ are some specific functions. The parameter $\theta$ is a real-valued **location parameter** and is known as the **natural** (or canonical) parameter. The function $b(\theta)$ is referred to as the **cumulant** and the parameter $\phi$ as the **dispersion (or scale) parameter**. We assume that $b(\theta)$ is continuously twice differentiable. In the case of the Normal distribution, the parameter $\phi$ corresponds to the variance $\sigma^2$ and is functionally unrelated with the mean. On the other

```
$Var(Y) = \phi v(\mu) \quad (1.11)$
```

Table 1.3 shows the main features of the Bernoulli and Poisson distributions as members of the exponential family. Note that for the Bernoulli distribution, the mean and canonical link functions are the logistic and log functions. They are also the same for the Binomial distribution (not depicted in Table 1.3). For the Poisson, the exponential and logarithmic functions are, respectively, the associated mean and link functions.

|---|---|---|

## Generalized Linear Models

### 2.1 Introduction

Standard linear models are composed of two components: systematic effects and random errors. Generalized Linear Models (GLM) is an extension of the usual linear regression to the Binomial, Poisson, Negative-binomial, Gamma, and distributions. Originally proposed by Nelder and Wedderburn (1972), it preceded Quasi-likelihood, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE), and Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM). In this chapter we will review the basic concepts of GLM, emphasizing overdispersion.

### 2.2 Linear Regression Models

Let $Y_j, j=1,2,...,n$, independent random variables, and let $y_j$ represent a realization of $Y_j$. Associated with each $y_j$ are a $k$-dimensional row vector of known covariates $\mathbf{x}_j = (x_{1j},x_{2j},x_{kj})$ and a $k$-dimensional column vector of unknown parameters $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\beta_1,\beta_2,...,\beta_k)^T$ such that

```
$y_j = \sum_{i=1}^k x_{ij}\beta_i + \varepsilon_j \quad (2.1)$
```

where the $\varepsilon_i$'s are uncorrelated random variables such that $E(\varepsilon_i)=0$ and $Var(\varepsilon_i)=\sigma^2$. This is the classical linear model. The linear model (2.1) in matrix form is

```
$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \quad (2.2)$
```

where $y$ is an $n$1 vector, $y=(y_1,y_2,...,y_n)^T$, $X$ is an $n \times k$ matrix of known covariates, $\mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2,...,\mathbf{x}_n)^T$, and $\varepsilon$ is an $n \times 1$ vector of disturbances, $\varepsilon = (\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,...,\varepsilon_n)^T$. It is assumed that $n > k$.
