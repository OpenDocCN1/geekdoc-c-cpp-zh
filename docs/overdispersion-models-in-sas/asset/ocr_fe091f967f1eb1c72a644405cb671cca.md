Note that the unconditional variance of T is written as sum of the expected value of the conditional variance and variance of the conditional expectation. Consequently, one can expect the unconditional variance to be larger than the conditional variance of T. Thus, appropriate prior on P would lead to a model with overdispersion. In particular, when the Beta prior is chosen, the Beta-binomial distribution will be used. Therefore, one may consider the Beta-binomial to be a general way (not tied to a specific mechanism) of modeling overdispersion. As stated in Chapter 1, the last two equations represent a common characterization of overdispersion relative to the Binomial distribution. This formulation clearly allows a Bayesian framework. The distribution f() can be viewed as a prior distribution on the success probability P of the Binomial random variable T.

Under the prior distribution f(P), for any t = 0,1,...,m, it follows that

```
$Pr(T=t) = E_f\Pr(T=t)\}\\n=\int \binom{m}{t}\rho'(1-P)^{m-1}f(P)dP$$
```

A distribution of the form (4.6) is said to be a mixture, with the mixing distribution. If f(P) is continuous, the unconditional distribution of T is an infinite mixture. If the prior f(P) is discrete and finite, the integral symbol in (4.6) changes to a finite sum, and the resulting distribution is a finite mixture. Each of the overdispersion models discussed in this chapter can be realized by choosing mixing distributions. We will first present the well-known Beta-binomial distribution, originally used by Skellam (1948), which corresponds to a choice of Beta as the mixing prior. The second model, called the Random-clumped Binomial distribution, proposed by Morel and Nagaraj (1993), is a mixture of two Binomials and corresponds to choosing a discrete distribution as the prior. Two additional distributions will be considered in this chapter. We first discuss the Beta-binomial distribution.
