An analysis of variance was performed to examine how sample size affected the likelihood of errors of inference regarding factor structure of this scale. The results are presented in Table 5.2 on page 88. As expected, larger samples tended to produce solutions that were more accurate. Only 10% of samples in the smallest (2:1) sample produced correct solutions (identical to the population parameters), but 70% in the largest (20:1) produced correct solutions. Further, the number of misclassified items was also significantly affected by sample size. Almost two of thirteen items on average were misclassified on the wrong factor in the smallest samples, whereas just over one item in every two analyses was misclassified in the largest samples. Finally, two indicators of trouble—the presence of factor loadings greater than 1.0, or failure to converge, or both, were both exclusively observed in the smaller samples, with almost one-third of analyses in the smallest sample size category failing to produce a solution.

As we discussed above, Osborne et al. (2008) found that the majority of recent papers have subject to item ratios in the lower ranges. (See Table 5.1 on page 86.) Costello & Osborne's analyses demonstrate that the error rates for these ranges are extraordinarily high. Specifically, approximately two-thirds of published EFA studies have subject to item ratios of less than 10:1; at the same time, this ratio is associated with an error rate of approximately 40%. Even the samples with ratios of 20:1 have error rates well above the field standard alpha = .05 level!

## Application: Impact of Sample Size on Interpretation

As we have demonstrated above, both sample size and subject to item ratio have an impact on the quality of your results. The larger the sample and subject to item ratio, the better. But if you have a smaller than desirable sample, your analysis might identify an incorrect factor structure, inaccurate classification of items, or unrepresentative factor loadings. Costello & Osborne (2005) examined the effect of sample size among the SDQ data. Now let’s review how differences in sample size could affect interpretation of the engineering and GDS data.

We will start by running an EFA on the entire sample of data. We will use the extraction methods, the number of factors, and the rotation methods considered to be appropriate for this data based on the preceding chapters. Thus, we will use iterated PAF extraction and oblimin rotation for both data sets, and we will extract two factors from the engineering data and five from the GDS data.

Next, we will use the SURVEYSELECT procedure to select a subset of the data to rerun our analysis on. This procedure offers a variety of methods for selecting random samples. We will use the simple random sampling without replacement (METHOD=SRS) for our purposes. In addition to the sampling method, we must also specify the number of observations to select (N=), the input data set (DATA=), and the output data set that will contain just our selected observations (OUT=). We will select reduced samples that represent subject to item ratios of 10:1 and 5:1. We will then rerun our EFA analysis, with all of the same settings as the previous run, on each reduced sample.

The syntax to do this for the engineering data is presented below. Note we also use the FUZZ and REORDER options to order items by magnitude and display only loadings above a certain magnitude.

```
**Run initial model with all data;
proc factor data = engdata nfactors = 2 method = printr priors = SMC
rotate = OBLIMIN fuzz=.3 reorder;
VAR EngProb: INTERESTeng : ;
run;
```

```
**Select subsample from engineering data that meets 10:1 ratio and run;
proc surveyselect data=engdata method=srs n=140 out=engdata_red1;
run;
proc factor data = engdata_red1 nfactors = 2 method = printr
priors = SMC rotate = OBLIMIN fuzz=.3 reorder;
VAR EngProb: INTERESTeng : ;
run;
```

```
**Select subsample from engineering data that meets 5:1 ratio and run;
proc surveyselect data=engdata method=srs n=70 out=engdata_red2;
run;
proc factor data = engdata_red2 nfactors = 2 method = printr
priors = smc rotate = OBLIMIN fuzz=.3 reorder;
VAR EngProb: INTERESTeng: ;
run;
```

Table 5.3 on page 90 summarizes the pattern matrix results for each sample of the engineering data. We can see that the three pattern matrices for the engineering data are all relatively similar. The loadings vary slightly between the different samples, but the overall structure remains intact. Each of the samples would lead to similar conclusions.

Table 5.4 on page 91 summarizes the pattern matrix results for the GDS data, which shows us very different results. The items in the table are organized according to the magnitude of the factor loadings in the full sample (using the REORDER option). If we look at the two reduced samples, we notice that items no longer load on the same factors. In addition, the relative weight, or unique variance extracted from each item, differs more dramatically between the solutions.

Similar to the findings of Guadagnoli & Velicer (1988), we see that data with higher loadings and stronger factor structure is less affected by sample size. Unfortunately, it is often difficult to know before analysis if an instrument will exhibit such traits. EFA is exploratory and often the first foray into a set of data. Thus, it is wise to approach an EFA with the mindset that your structure might not be clear, and sample size could play an important role in your results.
