The halo effect is when we give over-complimentary feedback because we do not want to hurt the feelings of the recipient or we do not want to rock the boat. I often term this as 'bullshit feedback'! Often whole organisations collude with this system because it keeps everything running smoothly. I've known old friends giving each other top marks on annual appraisal forms which had nothing to do with their performance.

Why do we behave in this way? Management expert Jerry B. Harvey argues that we are tempted to say nothing or dress up feedback because of the following:

- Anxiety: giving honest feedback creates intense anxiety in the giver as they think about acting in accordance with what they know to be right. As a result, they act in a manner incongruent with their beliefs.
- Negative fantasies: the giver builds up in their minds the negative consequences of speaking the truth until they are paralyzed by the thought of it.
- Fear of separation: probably the biggest reason for bullshit as the giver fears separation from the norm or if they take the risk of speaking out. If we think back to our friends, one was terrified of her feedback changing the relationship she valued.

This gives us the reasons why bullshit feedback is so prevalent in our society. NLP assists us to change our view about feedback – both the delivery of it and the receipt. We know that the presupposition of NLP is that: > There is no failure. In other words, there is only learning. If you truly believe this, you will seek the learning that comes from all experiences in order to develop yourself. Feedback then becomes something to be welcomed and not something to be feared. Feedback is fundamental to development.

There are other beliefs that also support our ability to give and receive feedback.

### Everyone's perception is truth

We may not agree with the feedback we have been given but it's true for the giver in that situation. Each person's perception is unique to them and represents their own model of the world. That does not make it right or wrong, it is just true for them. By accepting the feedback from someone, we are accepting part of ourselves. If everyone in a group gives the individual the same feedback then there is something for them to think about. If you can spot it, you got it. NLP trainer Sue Knight says this at NLP: other words, what we recognise in others is true about ourselves. The characteristics in others that touch us emotionally are pointers towards things we don't like. Yet, these are often 'blind spots' in us. To see something in others, we have to have that structure in our thinking too. For example, I recently gave feedback to my daughter about not paying attention on time and recognised behaviour in myself. That was an example of a blind spot for me.

Taking on these beliefs helps us to change our mindset around feedback and to see it as something precious. It also starts to become clear that feedback is all around us if we take the time to seek it out. It's not just what others tell us; for example, if everybody stays quiet it means they don't like.

## How to give and receive feedback

In NLP, a popular approach for giving feedback is the ‘feedback sandwich’. It isn't the only approach but it's one I personally find very useful. Change happens at the level where the mind needs to hear feedback quickly after the event. Feedback that is stored up until the day of performance appraisal is a waste of time. One of the most common complaints about feedback is that it takes place too long after the event. Fast feedback is built into the daily routine, ideally at the moment the event happens, or at the end of a meeting.

![](img/28d67ab57dd9834451e192_0.png)

The feedback sandwich process is made up of three stages, while maintaining eye contact at all times. First, check that you are in rapport with the person and then tell them what they did well – this gets the mind open and interested in listening. Next, give the person specific suggestions as to what they can do better – this is often called the ‘meat’ in the sandwich. Finally, you end with an overall positive statement to confirm the learning by asking the person what they will do differently. The important thing is to say the feedback the way it is. Be as direct as possible – do not leave opportunity for misinterpretation, and give as many specific examples as you can. Vague feedback is meaningless. Areas for improvement: Again, it’s important to focus on what the person should do differently and not on what they shouldn't do. As we learnt earlier, the mind cannot process a negative. Remember: don't think of the blind spot. To not think of a blind spot, you have to first think of it. If we focus on what we shouldn't do, we attract that thing to us.

### Steve – experiencing the importance of the feedback sandwich

Steve is known as ‘Meat Man’, meaning that he gives very direct feedback in the middle of the sandwich. He tells a story of when his boss used the feedback sandwich. Steve had spent years in the army and did not consider the good stuff – he wanted the boss to be straight with him. His boss asked him three times if that was what he truly wanted and said ‘yes’. The boss then began to give him just the meat. Several weeks went by and he realised he was avoiding his boss. In fact, if he heard him coming along the corridor, he would slip into another office to hide. He couldn’t understand this behaviour until one day the penny dropped. Receiving feedback was nothing about being ‘tough’ and it was all about balance. If you give compliments, you will receive compliments and that helps build confidence. He asked his boss to return to the feedback sandwich and he soon began to notice he was much happier to bump into him in the corridor again.

Rece feedback is a fundamental precursor for being able to give it. Your ability to receive feedback is central to continuous learning and growth as well as key to healthy relationships both in business and in life. Receiving feedback is also a skill to be learnt and practised. To receive feedback you need to hold the beliefs discussed above in your mind. If you see everything as feedback then the potential of failure does not exist. That makes it easier to listen to and consider the feedback you are given.

Before you receive feedback get yourself into a resourceful state. Remember how we learnt to anchor a positive state from the past (see Chapter 8). The best states may be openness, humility, self-confidence or curiosity. Always anticipate feedback and always invite it when appropriate so that feedback is in constant supply to you.

The specific skills for receiving feedback are as follows:

- Listen carefully and keep an open mind. You cannot make judgements on the validity if you have a closed mind.
- Suspend judgement. Do not put up your defences; rather take your time and make some mental notes to check later.
- Let the giver finish. Wait until they have got to the end and you have a full picture.
- Paraphrase to check you have heard and to clarify your perception.
- Ask for specific examples if they are not given.
- Avoid arguing, denying, justifying and minimising. It’s their point of view. Decide what you want to do with the information. Look for patterns with other feedback you received before.
- Respond in a way that presupposes acceptance, saying things like ‘In what ways do I do this?’ and ‘What effect does this have on you?’.
- Gather information from other sources.
- Decide what you want to do. Think about how you can use the information to improve the results. Imagine yourself having taken on board the feedback and now behaving in a way that demonstrates you have done so.
- Thank the person for giving you feedback.
