
In this section, we compare the performance of CNS with existing techniques as discussed below.

We first compare the performance of CNS with those of CtS [9] and VarBaTaC [10] in terms of the transmission length duration. We assume ideal channel conditions for this comparison. Because both CtS and VarBaTaC utilize complete silence for information communication, their performance degradation in the presence of noise will be much more than that suffered by CNS.

The biggest drawback of the CtS scheme is that the transmission time is exponential in the number of bits to be sent. VarBaTaC uses a variable coding base and controls the communication time by suitably tuning the base value. Suppose we want to transmit a value \(M\), where \(0 \leq M < 2^k\), \(k \geq 0\). The representation of \(M\) in base \(r\) will have \(m = \left\lceil \frac{k}{\log_2 r} \right\rceil\) digits. Each of these \(m\) digits is then transmitted using CtS with a pulse separating each digit. For \(m\) digits, the number of pulses transmitted would be \(m+1\) and the total time taken in the worst case would be \((m+1)+m\cdot(r-1)\). For example, the decimal value \((245)_{10}\) in base 16 is \(F5\). Assuming base 16 is used for transmission, the number of pulses required by the VarBaTaC scheme would be 3 (i.e., \(3e_b\) units of energy expenditure) and the total duration of transmission would be \(3+(15+5)=23\) time slots. To compare VarBaTaC with CNS, we see that \((245)_{10}\) in binary is \(11110101\). The frequency of both the bit pairs \(01\) and \(11\) is 2. We arbitrarily choose \(01\) to be the \(N\) symbol and map \(11\) to the symbol \(A\). Thus the sequence to be transmitted in CNS is \(AANN\) and the total duration of transmission would be four time slots, with only \(2e_b\) units of energy expenditure by the transmitter for the two \(A\) symbols.

Let \(t_{avg}\) be the average number of time slots required in the VarBaTaC scheme.

Whatever the value of the message to be transmitted in the range \(0\) to \(M\) (both inclusive), we need to transmit \(m+1\) pulses for the \(m\) digits to be transmitted. In addition, we need on an average, \(\frac{m(r-1)}{2}\) time slots for the digits (assuming equal likelihood for all possible values of the message). Hence, \(t_{avg}\) is given by

$$t_{avg}=(m+1)+\frac{m(r-1)}{2}=\frac{m(r+1)}{2}+1. \tag{8.36}$$

Let \(r=2^p\). Hence, \(m=\left\lceil\frac{k}{p}\right\rceil\), and from Equation (8.36) we have

$$\frac{k}{p}\left(\frac{2^p+1}{2}\right)+1\leq t_{avg}<\left(\frac{k}{p}+1\right)\left(\frac{2^p+1}{2}\right)+1.$$

As we can see from Tables 8.14 and 8.15, on average, CNS will require fewer time slots than VarBaTaC. In general, the transmission time of CNS will be less than that of CtS and VarBaTaC, while generating significant savings in energy at both the transmitter and the receiver. Note that neither CtS nor VarBaTaC generates energy savings at the receiver. Thus, in CtS and VarBaTaC, the energy savings generated during transmissions would be substantially outweighed by the excessive receiver energy due to longer duration of communication than binary data transmission or CNS. For single hop networks this is not a problem but for multi-hop WSNs, this may become an issue.

### 8.5.3.2 Performance Comparison in a Noisy Channel

We now turn to the realistic situation where a channel is noisy and compare the performance of CNS with binary FSK and similar techniques that employ silent symbol-based strategies like the RBNSiZeComm [39], TSS [3] and RZE [5]. We first need to find the dependency of the bit error rate (BER) on the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for the CNS scheme. We assume that the channel noise is *additive white Gaussian noise* (AWGN). Let us also assume that the transmitter uses FSK modulation with frequencies \(f_c\), \((f_c - \Delta f)\) and \((f_c + \Delta f)\) to represent \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) in any order. Effectively this will be a hybrid modulation scheme involving FSK and ASK. As a representative example for showing the energy savings, we use a non-coherent detection-based receiver similar to the one used for RBNSiZeComm or TSS protocol, with a structure as shown in Figure 8.19.

In Figure 8.19, \(r\) is the signal received at the input of the receiver which consists of the transmitted signal \(s\) as well as the channel noise \(\eta\), i.e., \(r = s + \eta\). The received signal \(r\) is first passed through three bandpass filters (BPFs) as shown in the figure, with center frequencies at \(f_c + \Delta f\), \(f_c\) and \(f_c - \Delta f\) (corresponding to \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\), respectively). The outputs of these bandpass filters are applied to the inputs of three envelope detectors (ED) to derive the values \(e_A\), \(e_B\) and \(e_C\) at their outputs, corresponding to the symbols \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) respectively. In the ideal situation when there is no noise (\(\eta = 0\)), only one of these \(e_A\), \(e_B\) and \(e_C\) will assume a non-zero value if the transmitted symbol is \(A\) or \(B\) or \(C\), and all of \(e_A\), \(e_B\) and \(e_C\) will be zero if the transmitted symbol is \(N\). However, due to noise, even if the transmitted symbol is, for example, \(A\), \(e_B\) and \(e_C\) may have non-zero values, although we may assume in this situation that \(e_A > e_B\) and \(e_A > e_C\). Similarly, if the transmitted symbol is \(B\) (or \(C\)), not only \(e_B\) (or \(e_C\)) will have a non-zero value, but \(e_A\) and \(e_C\) (or \(e_A\) and \(e_B\)) may also assume small non-zero values. If the transmitted symbol is \(N\), all of \(e_A\), \(e_B\) and \(e_C\) may assume small non-zero values.

To detect the transmitted symbol correctly in the presence of channel noise, we first pass \(e_A\), \(e_B\) and \(e_C\) through a comparator to find which one is the largest, and also we use three threshold detectors to see whether \(e_A\), \(e_B\) or \(e_C\) is above a certain threshold.

$$P\{q_A=0|A\}=\int_0^{e_{th}}\frac{e_A}{\sigma^2}e^{-\frac{e_A^2+s_A^2}{2\sigma^2}}I_0\left(\frac{s_Ae_A}{\sigma^2}\right)de_A,$$

$$X_1=1-\left(1-e^{-e_{th}^2/2\sigma^2}\right)^2\int_0^{e_{th}}\frac{e_A}{\sigma^2}e^{-\frac{e_A^2+s_A^2}{2\sigma^2}}I_0\left(\frac{s_Ae_A}{\sigma^2}\right)de_A. \tag{8.41}$$

$$P\{e_A>e_B\mid A\}=P\{e_A>e_C\mid A\}=\int_0^{e_A}\frac{e_B}{\sigma^2}e^{-e_B^2/2\sigma^2}\,de_B=1-e^{-e_A^2/2\sigma^2}.$$
