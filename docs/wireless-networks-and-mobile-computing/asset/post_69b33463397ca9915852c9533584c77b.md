
## 6.1.2.3 Max-Min Fair (MMF) Allocation

One well known association policy [2, 3] is known as the *max-min fair (MMF)* allocation. As stated earlier, the *STAs* are associated to the *APs* in their range by obtaining physical bit rates. A rate vector is *MMF* if there is no other vector for which the rate of some node, say *i*, is higher without the rate of some other node, say *j*, that has a smaller than or equal to rate than *i*, being made lower. *STAs* associated on the basis of *MMF* allocation of their individual data rates may obtain uniform or fair throughput allocation. However, such a policy may lead to a reduced network throughput [3]. Furthermore, instead of the obtained data rate, max-min fair allocation can be formulated with the obtained bandwidth. A system provides a fair service if all users have the same allocated bandwidths. Unfortunately, such a degree of fairness may cause significant reduction of the network throughput, since all users have the same bandwidth allocation as bottleneck users. As described in [3], a bandwidth allocation of a weighted system is called max-min fair if there is no way to increase the bandwidth of a user without decreasing the bandwidth of another user with the same or less normalized bandwidth.

## 6.1.2.4 Least Load First (LLF)

Another association policy known as the *least load first (LLF)* association is a widely used load-balancing heuristic defined in terms of load of an *AP* [3]. When an *STA* associates to an *AP*, it imposes a load on that *AP*. According to this association policy, an *STA* checks the load incorporated to an *AP* before associating it to the *AP*. A user chooses the least loaded *AP*, where an *AP* load is inversely proportional to the current bandwidth that its associated users receive. This policy may lead to unfair bandwidth allocation and poor load balancing.

## 6.1.2.5 Min-Max Load Balancing Allocation

An association policy aiming at providing fair load balancing across the APs is *min-max load balancing* [3]. The load of an *AP* needs to reflect its inability to satisfy the requirements of its associated users and it should be inversely proportional to the average bandwidth that they enjoy. A feasible association is min-max load balanced if its corresponding load vector has the same or lower lexicographical value than any other load vector of any other feasible assignment. According to such a policy, *STAs* are associated to the *APs* with the objective of min-max load balancing [3]. Load balancing along with mean *RSSI* can be used as the association metric [4]. According to such a metric, users select the *AP* on the basis of the number of users already associated with that AP and the mean *RSSI* value.

## 6.1.2.6 Link Rate and Throughput-Based Association

Link rate and realized throughput together can be considered to formulate an association policy [13] for a multi-channel multiple AP network. The objective is to maximize the capacity region of the set of *APs* under a dynamic traffic model. Users arrive at a point within the network coverage area, associate with an access point, realize a download and leave. Network capacity is defined by the limiting load beyond which the number of active users would grow indefinitely as the arrival rate in some region is greater than the rate at which downloads complete. The limitation of such an approach is that using network capacity as a criterion for comparison does not necessarily consider how well policies perform at normal loads.

According to this association policy [1], users associating to an AP providing the maximum throughput may change to another AP with maximum RSSI depending on the available bandwidth. The drawback is that the change may also lead to an imbalance in the load distribution.

In [8], a numerical approach based on stochastic dynamic programming proposes to find the optimal association for a network having a small topology consisting of two APs only. Using the insights from the association rule for the two-AP problem, a near-optimal heuristic named RAT, has been proposed. An objective function is defined based on both rate and throughput and then an STA associates with that AP for which its objective function is minimized.

The AP selection and channel allocation are two fundamental access control problems. For users in the coverage area of one AP, this involves only channel allocation. However, for users that belong in the intersection of coverage areas of more than one AP, the selection of an appropriate AP to establish the connection is also important. Iordanis et al. [16] proposed a joint approach of AP selection and channel assignment with a view to satisfying a given user load vector with a minimum number of channels. The authors formulated this joint problem as a clique load balancing problem and using this as a guideline, a heuristic algorithm has been proposed.

Traditionally, AP placement and channel assignment are considered sequentially. The *AP* placement is often modeled as a facility location problem and the channel assignment problem is mapped to a graph coloring problem. Authors in [11] argued that treating these two key issues separately may lead to suboptimal designs.

Accordingly, they proposed an integrated model based on mathematical programming that addresses both the aspects simultaneously to maximize the net throughput that an *STA* can enjoy.

In [17], *AP* placement and channel assignment were considered jointly to maximize the system throughput and fair resource sharing among mobile terminals. However, they allowed co-channel overlapping between cells.
