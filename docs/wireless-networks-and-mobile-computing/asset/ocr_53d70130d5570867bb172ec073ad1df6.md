In contrast, the Brownian mobility model [31] allows non-intentional movements of the constituent nodes in which the position of a node at the next time step is dependent only on its position at the current time step. To be more specific, three parameters are used in this model: $prob_{stat}$, $prob_{move}$ and $m$. The $prob_{stat}$ parameter represents the probability that a node remains stationary for the entire time of interest. The $prob_{move}$ parameter represents the probability that a node moves at a given time step and finally $m$ defines a square having each side of length $2m$ centered at the current position. If a node is at its current position at time step $k$, at the $(k+1)^{th}$ time step its position will be at some randomly chosen point within this square. In effect, the parameter $m$ models the maximum velocity of the mobile node, for a given duration of each time step. Because of the short step movements of a node at each time step, the movement pattern appears like the movement of a drunken person and hence it appears that the nodes following this model make non-intentional movements.

# 2.4.4  Map-based Mobility Model

In the above three models, nodes are assumed to be free to move around within the deployment region $R$. In this model, we assume that the nodes move only along specified paths within the region $R$. Examples of such constrained movements include driving along roads or highways and people moving along sidewalks on the streets. Thus, a road map of the region $R$ is needed in this model. The nodes will be randomly deployed on these roads and the movements of the nodes will be confined to these defined paths only. An example of a map-based model is the *freeway mobility model* proposed by Bai et al. [32] that assumes several freeways with varying numbers of lanes in both directions.
