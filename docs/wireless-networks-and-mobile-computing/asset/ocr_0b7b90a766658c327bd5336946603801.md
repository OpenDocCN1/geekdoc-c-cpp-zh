These data are assumed to be transmitted as short ASCII text messages [25, 26, 24], usually a few bytes long at the most. In a typical field deployment with EbT-based transmissions, considering a mesh network deployment for a field of size 60m × 60m, radio range of 0 to 80m with multi-hop communication, data rate of 300 packets/min = 0.5 packets/s and duty cycle of 15% at 20mA with a battery of 7.2Ah capacity, a radio battery would last for only 100 days [25, 26, 24].

From the simulation results, it follows that $P_N$ is around 0.4751 for ECG signals, 0.2884 for JPEG data from blood samples and 0.388 for text data, as shown in Table 8.19. Using these values of $P_N$ in Equation (8.44) and noting that the transmitter is in *active* or *idle* state during the transmission period of the $N$ symbols, the required SNR values for given BERs were computed for each of the above types of data. From these computations, we find that for ECG signals at the same BER, the energy saving from using CNS for ECG signals as compared to binary FSK will be approximately 50%.

Similarly, the energy savings for transmission of JPEG image files of blood and tissue samples was 33% for CNS, when compared to binary FSK. For text data, the energy saving was 42.6%. All these results are summarized in Table 8.19.

The amount of transmitter energy savings is between 33% and 50% for CNS, depending on the nature of the data to be transmitted. For the same data set, the average transmitter energy savings generated by RBNSiZeComm varied from 33% to 61% [39]. Figure 8.22 shows the comparison of transmitter energy savings generated by CNS and RBNSiZeComm for the different data types. CNS also simultaneously generates a saving in energy of about 50% compared to binary FSK at the receiver, regardless of the type of data. However, no such saving was generated at the receiver by RBNSiZeComm [39].
