In [9], the AP selection is modeled as a many-to-one matching problem in a bipartite graph to maximize the total load among all the APs.

Another association policy [10] taken into account the throughput degradation of an STA before it joins an AP. The metric is a weighted sum of the throughput provided to the new STA and the average transmission time of STAs already associated with the concerned AP.

Below we describe a few more association policies under various MAC scheduling strategies.

# 6.1.2.1 Strongest Signal First (SSF) Association

Strongest signal first(SSF) is one of the simplest association policies based on the metric of strongest signal-to-noise ratio measured by a received signal strength indicator (RSSI). According to this policy, an STA is associated to the AP from which it receives the strongest signal. As described in [4], the procedure for finding this metric is simple. An STA scans the available channels of each AP in the region and listens to the beacon or probe response frames. Depending on the country, one to fourteen channels per AP may exist. The STA stores the RSSI of the beacon or the probe response frames and other relevant information (e.g., whether encryption is on or off). After finishing the scanning procedure, the STA selects that AP with the maximum RSSI if the selected AP covers other requirements (usually the extended service set identification (ESSID), wired equivalent privacy (WEP) encryption) as well. The STA will leave the AP when the RSSI falls under a predefined threshold. This procedure is based on the premise that the quality of service of the selected AP is the best. However, the result of this procedure is that many stations are connected to a few APs, while neighboring APs remain idle. This overloading of the APs due to non-uniform load distribution will lead to performance degradation [3, 6, 5].
