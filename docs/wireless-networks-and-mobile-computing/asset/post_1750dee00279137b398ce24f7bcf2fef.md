### 6.1.2 Different Association Policies

When several *APs* are available, the *AP* selection of an *STA* heavily influences its performance and those of others. An association policy is an algorithm on the basis of which an *STA* is associated to an *AP* within its range. Intensive studies have been performed by various researchers to find a feasible association between *STAs* and *APs* based on different metrics and objectives. Kumar et al. [2] address an optimal association policy between *STAs* and *APs* with the objective of maximizing the throughput of the association, but ignored the co-channel interference by assuming the proper frequency planning of the *APs*. Many authors advocate load balancing to optimize performance. It has been shown [3, 6, 5] that the traditional association based on the received signal strength indicator (*RSSI*) measurement may lead to poor user experience due to non-uniform load distribution across different *APs*. To address this issue, some *AP* access control mechanisms are proposed [5, 6]. Bejerano et al. [3] proposed an association model considering both load balancing and fairness that includes a complicated central *AP* association mechanism.

Unlike the traditional *RSSI*, a heuristic has been proposed that helps the users to select the *AP* to be associated on the basis of the number of users already associated with that *AP* and the mean *RSSI* [4].

Vasudevan et al. [7] proposed an *AP-STA* association policy based on the bandwidth that an *STA* is likely to receive if it is associated with that *AP*. Before a user is associated to an *AP*, it estimates the potential bandwidth of neighboring *APs* by passive measurement and selects the *AP* with the highest potential bandwidth for connection.

In [12], an *AP* association strategy is proposed. Before an *STA* associates to an *AP*, it calculates the minimal throughput for all the *STAs*. The *STA* finds its associating *AP* such that the minimal throughput for all the *STAs* is maximized.

In [9], the AP selection is modeled as a many-to-one matching problem in a bipartite graph to maximize the total load among all the APs.

Another association policy [10] takes into account the throughput degradation of an STA before it joins an AP. The metric is a weighted sum of the throughput provided to the new STA and the average transmission time of STAs already associated with the concerned AP.

Below we describe a few more association policies under various MAC scheduling strategies.

#### 6.1.2.1 Strongest Signal First (SSF) Association

Strongest signal first (SSF) is one of the simplest association policies based on the metric of strongest signal-to-noise ratio measured by a received signal strength indicator (RSSI). According to this policy, an STA is associated to the AP from which it receives the strongest signal. As described in [4], the procedure for finding this metric is simple. An STA scans the available channels of each AP in the region and listens to the beacon or probe response frames. Depending on the country, one to fourteen channels per AP may exist. The STA stores the RSSI of the beacon or the probe response frames and other relevant information (e.g., whether encryption is on or off). After finishing the scanning procedure, the STA selects that AP with the maximum RSSI if the selected AP covers other requirements (usually the extended service set identification (ESSID), wired equivalent privacy (WEP) encryption) as well. The STA will leave the AP when the RSSI falls under a predefined threshold. This procedure is based on the premise that the quality of service of the selected AP is the best. However, the result of this procedure is that many stations are connected to a few APs, while neighboring APs remain idle. This overloading of the APs due to non-uniform load distribution will lead to performance degradation [3, 6, 5].

#### 6.1.2.2 Maximum Utility-Based Association

Kumar et al. [2] address an optimal association policy between the $STAs$ and the $APs$ in their ranges in which finding an optimal association policy is considered the maximum utility-based formulation of the problem. The authors note that an $STA$ associates to an $AP$ with a physical bit rate and eventually such an association will yield an $STA$ throughput dependent on the specific $MAC$ scheduling used. They used two $MAC$ scheduling techniques, namely random polling and proportional fair access, to calculate the throughput of an individual association. As per random polling access, the set of $STAs$ associated to a common $AP$ achieves equal individual throughput. If there are $m_j$ $STAs$ $i_1,i_2,\ldots,i_{m_j}$ associated with AP $j$ and these are associated with rate $r_{i_pj}$ where $1 \leq p \leq m_j$, the aggregate uplink throughput obtained by all the $STAs$ served by AP $j$ is of the form:

$$\frac{m_j}{\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{m_j}\frac{1}{r_{i_pj}}}.$$

The individual throughput obtained by the $STA$ from AP $j$ can be expressed as

$$t_j=\frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{m_j}\frac{1}{r_{i_pj}}}.$$

For fair allocation of user throughput, they used log utility to formulate the aggregate throughput of the network as given by

$$\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_j\ln(t_j).$$
