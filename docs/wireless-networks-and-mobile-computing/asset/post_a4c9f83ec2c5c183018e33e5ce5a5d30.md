We now make the following observations:

**Observation 1.** *The application of the reduction rules 1 and 2 on the binary data during transmission ensures that the digit patterns $\overline{1}\overline{1}$, $1\overline{1}$ and $\overline{1}1$ do not occur in the transmitted data.*

**Observation 2.** *If the original data was an $n$-bit binary data frame, RBNS encoding can result in a frame of size $n+1$ RBNS digits.*

**Observation 3.** *The encoding and decoding processes need to scan from the least significant to the most significant digit position, and these can be conveniently overlapped with a pipelined serial transmission and reception of the digits.*

It is thus clear that the logic complexities for hardware conversion from binary to RBNS and vice versa are minimal. Thus, the increase in delay and energy consumption due to the encoding and decoding logic would be too small to considerably affect the energy savings offered by this scheme.

# 8.3.2  RBNSiZeComm Communication Protocol

The RBNSiZeComm communication protocol involves the execution of only two steps:

1. Recode the binary data frame in RBNS using reduction rules 1 and 2.
2. Send the RBNS data frame, transmitting a non-zero voltage level only for the 1 and \(\bar{1}\) bits, while remaining silent for the 0 bits.

To distinguish between a channel with no signal and a zero bit inside an ongoing transmission, a *start of transmission* signal is sent at the beginning of transmission of the data and an *end of transmission* signal at the end of the data frame.

# 8.3.3  Analysis of Energy Savings

Let us first compute the average number of non-zero digits after applying reduction rule 1 on a binary string of length \(n\). We first find the total number of occurrences of runs of 1’s of length \(k\), \(1 \leq k \leq n\) in all possible binary strings of 0’s and 1’s of length \(n\).

We denote a run of 1’s of length \(k\) by \(R_k\). Clearly, two consecutive runs of 1’s of length \(k_1\) and \(k_2\), \(1 \leq k_1, k_2 \leq n\) in a bit string will be separated by at least one zero. Let us append a zero on left of each such \(R_k\), \(1 \leq k \leq n\) and denote the symbol 0\(R_k\) by \(y_k\). We also denote a single zero by the symbol \(y_0\). Each such \(y_k\), \(0 \leq k \leq n\), will be a string of length \(k+1\). We describe two techniques for solving this counting problem: one using generating functions and the other using recurrence relations.
