If the paging delay is unconstrained, the message traffic arising due to polling can be reduced to some extent by sequentially searching the cells in decreasing order of probability that the MT is currently residing in that cell [8]. However, this requires some prior knowledge about the recent history of movements of the MT within that LA. Location updates and paging subject to delay constraints have been considered by various researchers [9, 10] and are essentially based on having an upper bound on the time required for paging. In [9], a distance-based location update mechanism has been assumed and the residing area of the called MT is partitioned into a number of smaller areas. When an incoming call for the MT arrives, all these subareas are polled sequentially. The number of polling areas is limited to some maximum value, say N, so that the total paging delay is limited to the time required for N polling operations. Depending on the mobility pattern of the MT (which may be decided by a mobility model), call arrival rate and maximum delay N, the optimal distance may be computed for distance-based location update. Thus, an optimal scheme for a constrained paging delay scheme with distance-based location update is complex to implement. However, as in [10], if a movement-based location update is used along with the maximum number N of polling operations to limit the paging delay, the implementation of the overall scheme for location update and paging becomes relatively simple.

## 2.1.4 Database Architectures

As discussed above, location registration involves updating the location databases with the most recent location information of an MT that is currently available, while call delivery requires querying these databases to find the current location of a called MT first to the level of the MSC that stores the current information of the called MT and then to the cell level, i.e., the level of the base station (BS) of the cell where the called MT currently resides. If the called MT has moved far away from its home location, a lot of location update messages may be generated from its current VLR to the HLR and also to all the intermediate VLRs on the path from its current VLR to the HLR. Also, the acknowledgement and registration cancellation messages will be communicated among its HLR and the respective VLRs (see steps 4, 5 and 6 of the location registration process described above). The total overhead in terms of the time needed for the two basic operations of updating the databases and terminal paging as well as the message traffic in the network for the required signalling operations may be significant, as explained below, unless the location databases are properly managed.

For example, consider a situation that the called MT is originally registered in Kolkata, and has currently moved to Bangalore. Then a location update message is transmitted from the VLR in Bangalore to its HLR in Kolkata whenever the MT moves to a new LA that belongs to a different VLR. This may involve message communication through a number of intermediate MSCs. Further, when a call is generated from another MT in Bangalore to this roaming MT (which is also in Bangalore), the MSC of the calling MT must first query the HLR of the called MT in Kolkata, to discover that the called MT is currently residing in Bangalore. Due to an ever-increasing number of mobile users, this signalling traffic through the network can be very high, causing a significant reduction in the network’s performance in handling the calls.

There are two basic approaches to design the architecture of the location databases to mitigate the above problem. The first is the *centralized database architecture* and the other is the *distributed database architecture*.

### Centralized Database Architecture

One possible scheme under this category is a dynamic hierarchical database architecture which uses an additional level of databases called *directory registers (DRs)* along with the two-tier IS-41 database structure involving HLR and VLR. The basic idea is to use a DR that covers the service area of a number of MSCs so that it stores the location pointer configuration for the MTs roaming in its service area. There are three basic types of location pointers used by a DR [1]:

- A *local pointer* is stored at an MT’s serving DR pointing to the currently serving MSC of the MT.
- A *direct remote pointer* is stored at a remote DR which points to the currently serving MSC of the MT.
- An *indirect remote pointer* is stored at a remote DR which indirectly points to the currently serving MSC of the MT.

Along with this scheme of using the DRs, the HLR of the MT may now be configured to store a pointer to the serving DR and/or the currently serving MSC. In some cases, however, no such change in HLR may be made for a cost-effective operation and the original IS-41 scheme may be used in HLR. In view of these DRs, let us again consider the above example of an MT having its HLR in Kolkata, and currently roaming in Bangalore. Suppose it moves to Chennai and receives calls from the Bangalore area. If a significant number of incoming calls to this MT are detected as originating from Bangalore, a direct or indirect remote pointer can be set up for this MT in the DR at Chennai. When the next call is initiated for this MT from Chennai, the calling MSC first queries the DR and the call can be immediately forwarded to Bangalore, without the need for querying its HLR in Kolkata. This significantly reduces the signaling traffic in the network for call delivery. Likewise, signaling traffic in location registration can also be greatly reduced if its HLR records the ID of the serving DR (instead of the serving MSC) of the MT. If the MT moves to a new LA under a different MSC (but under the same DR) in the Bangalore area, then only the local pointer at the serving DR of the MT has to be updated, without the need for accessing the HLR in Kolkata. Thus, such a scheme using DRs can be used for significant overhead reduction in both location registration and call delivery processes.

Another possible scheme for reducing the overhead in location registration and call delivery is through *per-user location caching* [11] which stores the current location information of an MT in a cache at a nearby signal transfer point (STP). Whenever an MT is accessed through the STP, an entry is added to the cache which refers to the mapping from the ID of the MT to that of its serving VLR. When a call is initiated to an MT, the STP first checks whether a cache entry exists for this MT. If no cache entry exists, the usual (e.g., IS-41) call delivery scheme as described above is used to locate the MT. If, however, a cache entry is found for this MT, the STP will query the VLR as specified by the cache entry. If the MT still resides under this VLR, a cache hit occurs and the MT is found easily without the need for querying its HLR. If, however, the MT has already moved to another location not associated with this VLR, a miss occurs and the usual IS-41 call delivery scheme is followed in locating the MT. Figure 2.7 illustrates this scheme in which MT1 is trying to call MT2 and there is a cache entry at the signal transfer point STP1 corresponding to this MT2 that it is now mapped to VLR2. If the MT2 still resides under VLR2, the call is delivered to it immediately through VLR2 without querying the HLR of MT2. However, if MT2 moves to a new location under a VLR other than VLR2, there will be a cache miss and the usual IS-41 process will be applied to locate MT2. Thus, the overhead under this cache miss situation is more than that in the usual IS-41 scheme.
