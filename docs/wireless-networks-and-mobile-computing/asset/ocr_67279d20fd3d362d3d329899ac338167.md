Message complexity (Figure 5.5.2.8) refers to the number of transmissions of the broadcast message \( M \), while time complexity (Figure 5.5.2.8) is the number of rounds required to complete the broadcast. It can be recalled that each round consists of eight time slots. Figure 5.5.2.8 shows the number of retransmissions of the broadcast message \( M \) that occurred on average. Retransmissions can occur due to two reasons: i) collision detected by the receiving nodes and, ii) a node in transmit state receives an \( N \) message. Simulation results showed that the number of actual message collisions suffered was slightly less than the total number of retransmissions of \( M \). Figure 5.5.2.8 depicts the total number of transmission attempts on average including the transmission of the control signals and the broadcast message.

The graphs in Figures 5.28 to 5.32 show the performance of the above algorithm for mobile scenarios. Sinha et al. [13] selected two mobility models: the widely used random way point model and the city section model, also known as the Manhattan mobility model (as introduced in Chapter 2) for the purpose of studying the performance of the bf\_broadcast algorithm. The city section mobility model provides realistic movements for a section of a city as the mobile nodes must follow predefined paths and behavior guidelines such as traffic laws. The mobility trace files were regenerated using the bonnmotion tool [19] and then ns-2 was used to obtain the results. We considered graphs of 100, 200 and 300 nodes for comparison of results. As in the case of the static scenario, each node was in turn considered as the source node and the averages of 100 different simulation trials for each graph size presented as the performance results.
