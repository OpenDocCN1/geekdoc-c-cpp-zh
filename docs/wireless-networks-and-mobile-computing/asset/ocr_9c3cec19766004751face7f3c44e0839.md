On the other hand, the total bandwidth for signal communication in cellular mobile networks is very limited. Apart from the 100 MHz band from 860 MHz to 960 MHz used by the GSM (Global System for Mobile Communications) system, the 1992 World Administrative Radio Conference in Geneva allocated the 1885 to 2025 MHz and 2100 to 2200 MHz bands — a total of only 230 MHz — for terrestrial and satellite communications. Within these two bands, the satellite band covers 1980 to 2010 MHz and 2170 to 2200 MHz for a total of 60 MHz, while the remaining 170 MHz is for terrestrial mobile communication. The frequency gaps between 2025 to 2110 MHz and beyond 2200 MHz are reserved for other services such as remote sensing, cable TV relay service, space research, etc. In 1995, the World Radio Conference changed the above frequency allocation a bit. The satellite band allocation for Region 2 (the Americas and the Caribbean) was modified to 1990 to 2025 MHz and 2160 to 2200 MHz for a total of 75 MHz, leaving only the other 155 MHz for terrestrial services. This has made the situation for cellular mobile communication in the countries of this region more difficult. Even with some later minor modifications, the end result is that the total bandwidth available for terrestrical uses is very small compared to the huge ever-increasing demand due to a large number of mobile users along with the increasing demand for multimedia communication.

One possible way to overcome this problem of bandwidth crisis is by intelligent re-use of the frequencies at sufficiently distant cells of the cellular network so that these two frequencies do not interfere with each other. Over the past two decades, research results on this topic have appeared in the literature, and yet a number of problems need to be solved to develop an effective algorithm for channel allocation in cellular mobile networks.
