The RSA algorithm is applicable for encryption of a block of data or message bits. In many real-life applications, message bits are transmitted as streams, for example, in a wireless communication channel. For such applications, a different class of encryption algorithms, commonly known as **stream ciphers** are needed which differ from the so-called **block ciphers** like the RSA algorithm.

Stream ciphers are broadly classified into two categories depending on the platform most suited to their implementation: software stream ciphers and hardware stream ciphers. RC4 is one of the widely used stream ciphers implemented in software. This cipher is used in network protocols such as SSL, TLS, WEP and WPA. The cipher also finds applications in Microsoft Windows, Lotus Notes, Apple AOCE and Oracle Secure SQL. Though several other efficient and secure stream ciphers were devised after RC4, it is still the most popular stream cipher algorithm due to its simplicity, ease of implementation, and speed.

## 9.2.4 RC4 Stream Cipher

The RC4 stream cipher was designed by Ron Rivest for RSA Data Security in 1987. It uses *S*-box *S*, an array of length *N*, where each location of *S* stores one byte (typically, *N* = 256). A secret key *k* of size *l* bytes is used to scramble this permutation (typically, 5 ≤ *l* ≤ 16). Array *K* of length *N* holds the main key, with secret key *k* repeated as *K[y] = k[y mod l]*, for 0 ≤ *y* ≤ *N* − 1.

RC4 has two components, namely the key scheduling algorithm (KSA) and the pseudo-random generation algorithm (PRGA). The KSA uses the key *K* to generate a pseudo-random permutation *S* of {0, 1, . . . , *N* − 1} and PRGA uses this pseudo-random permutation to generate arbitrary number of pseudo-random keystream bytes. The procedures are in Algorithms 1 and 2, respectively.

```
**Algorithm 1** Key Scheduling Algorithm

1: **procedure** KSA(Secret Key *K*)
2: Initialize *S* ← {0, 1, . . . , *N* − 1} and *j* ← 0
3: **for** *i* = 0, . . . , *N* − 1 **do**
4:       Increment: *j* ← *j* + *S*[*i*] + *K*[*i*]
5:       Swap: *S*[*i*] ↔ *S*[*j*]
6: **end for**
7: **return** S-box *S*
8: **end procedure**
```

```
**Algorithm 2** Pseudo-Random Generation Algorithm

1: **procedure** PRGA(*S*-box *S*)
2: Initialize indices: *i* ← 0, *j* ← 0
3: **while** TRUE **do**
4:       Increment: *i* ← *i* + 1, *j* ← *j* + *S*[*i*]
5:       Swap: *S*[*i*] ↔ *S*[*j*]
6:       **output** *Z* ← *S*[*S*[*i*] + *S*[*j*]]
7: **end while**
8: **end procedure**
```

Any arithmetic addition used in context of RC4 is in general addition modulo *N*, unless specified otherwise. The output keystream *Z* is XOR-ed with the plaintext (byte per byte) to generate the ciphertext at the sender end (*C* = *M* ⊕ *Z*), and is XOR-ed with the ciphertext to get back the plaintext at the receiver end (*M* = *C* ⊕ *Z*).

Efficiency of keystream throughput is used as a benchmarking parameter for stream ciphers. The efficiency of the RC4 obviously depends on the efficiency of KSA and PRGA. While the KSA invokes a fixed cost for generating the initial pseudo-random state *S*, the PRGA incurs a variable cost based on the number of keystream bytes to be generated. An efficient implementation of RC4 would minimize the cost per round of KSA and PRGA to provide better throughput. The software implementation of RC4 is simple, and a detailed comparison of the software performance of eSTREAM and RC4 is given in [2]. It is rumored in the cryptographic community that an efficient RC4 software implementation can produce keystream bytes at a rate of 3 cycles per byte. However, there is no documented evidence to prove this claim. The best software performance of RC4 stated in the literature [2] provides an account of 7 to 15 cycles per byte of RC4 keystream, depending on the processor and the clock speed on which the cipher was tested. This rate is slow for typical wireless communication applications.

In contrast to the software implementation, a hardware implementation of the RC4 stream cipher may possibly be faster and find potential applications in encrypting and decrypting message streams in a wireless communication network. A number of attempts for efficient hardware implementation of RC4 have already been reported in the literature.

A 3-cycle-per-byte efficient implementation of RC4 on custom pipelined hardware was proposed by Kitsos et al. [11] in 2003. Later, a patent by Matthews [17] was disclosed and provided a similar 3-cycles-per-byte architecture using hardware pipelining. Five years later, Matthews disclosed another patent [18] that included a design for RC4 hardware based on pipeline architecture that could increase the efficiency of the cipher to obtain 1-byte-per-cycle in RC4 PRGA. In 2013, Sen Gupta et al. [1] introduced a hardware implementation of RC4 that also produced 1 byte per cycle as in the Matthews’ patent [18]. We describe below a hardware implementation of RC4 [1] which is based on loop unfolding and instruction rescheduling with a three-stage pipelining, resulting in less hardware cost and complexity than in [18]. Note that wireless communication devices are often constrained by the computing resources and optimization of resources and speed are always beneficial.

The approach followed in [1] is based on twofold unrolling of the loop in the PRGA algorithm, rescheduling the instructions in the two consecutive iterations of the loop, and generating one cipher byte per cycle in a three-stage pipelined fashion.

### Step 1: Calculation of $i_1$ and $i_2$

Incrementing $i_0$ by 1 and 2 can be done by the same clock pulse applied to two synchronous 8-bit counters. The counter for $i_1$ is initially loaded with 00000001 and the counter for $i_2$ is loaded with 00000010, the initial states of these two indices. This serves the first two rounds of RC4, in both KSA and PRGA.

Thereafter, in every other cycle, the clock pulse is applied to all the flip-flops except the ones at the LSB position for both the counters, as shown in Figure 9.1. This will result in proper increments of $i_1$ that assume only the odd values 1, 3, 5, . . ., and for $i_2$ assuming only the even values 2, 4, 6, . . ., as required in RC4. This is assured as the LSB of $i_1$ will always be 1 and that of $i_2$ will always be 0 as shown in Figure 9.1.

### Step 2: Calculation of $j_1$ and $j_2$

The values of $j_1$ and $j_2$ will be computed and stored in two 8-bit registers. To compute $j_1$, we need a 2-input parallel adder unit. It may use a carry lookahead adder or use *scan* operation as proposed by Sinha and Srimani [24], or use *carry-lookahead-tree* as proposed by Lynch and Swartzlander [13]. For computing $j_2$, there are two special cases:

$$j_2=j_0+S_0[i_1]+S_1[i_2]=\begin{cases} j_0+S_0[i_1]+S_0[i_2] & \text{if } i_2\ne j_1 \\ j_0+S_0[i_1]+S_0[i_1] & \text{if } i_2=j_1. \end{cases}$$

The only change from $S_0$ to $S_1$ is the swap $S_0[i_1]\leftrightarrow S_0[j_1]$, and hence we need to check whether $i_2$ is equal to either of $i_1$ or $j_1$. Now, $i_2$ cannot be equal to $i_1$ as they differ only by 1 modulo 256. Therefore, $S_1[i_2] = S_1[j_1] = S_0[i_1]$ if $i_2 = j_1$, and $S_1[i_2] = S_0[i_2]$ otherwise. In both cases, three binary numbers are to be added.

Let us denote the $k^{th}$ bit of $j_0$, $S_0[i_1]$ and $S_1[i_2]$ (either $S_0[i_2]$ or $S_0[i_1]$) by $a_k$, $b_k$ and $c_k$, respectively, where $0 \leq k \leq 7$. We first construct two 9-bit vectors $R$ and $C$, where the $k^{th}$ bits ($0 \leq k \leq 8$) of $R$ and $C$ are given by:

$$R_k = \operatorname{XOR}(a_k,b_k,c_k)\ \text{for }0 \leq k \leq 7,\quad R_8=0,\quad C_0=0,$$

$$C_k = a_{k-1}b_{k-1}+b_{k-1}c_{k-1}+c_{k-1}a_{k-1}\ \text{for }1\leq k\leq 8.$$

In RC4, all additions are done modulo 256. Hence, we can discard the ninth bit ($k=8$) of the vectors $R,C$ while adding them together, and carrying out normal 8-bit parallel addition considering $0\leq k\leq 7$. Therefore, one may add $R$ and $C$ by a parallel full adder as used for $j_1$. The circuit to compute $j_1$ and $j_2$ is shown in Figure 9.2.
