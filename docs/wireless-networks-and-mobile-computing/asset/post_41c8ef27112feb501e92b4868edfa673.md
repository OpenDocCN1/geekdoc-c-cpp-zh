## 1.1.8 Wireless Mesh Networks

As an alternative to the cellular and ad hoc network architectures, there has been a great deal of interest in recent times in wireless mesh networks (WMNs). The operational characteristics of WMNs may be considered to be somewhere in between infrastructure-based networks such as wireless local area networks (WLANs) and ad hoc networks with no pre-installed infrastructure support. In WMNs, the network consists of a set of *fixed* gateway nodes and a set of non-gateway nodes (either stationary or mobile). The non-gateway nodes must access a gateway node (similar to WLANs) to establish communication to the outside world. These non-gateway nodes can act as hosts or as wireless routers by forwarding packets from other users (similar to ad hoc networks), enabling other non-gateway nodes to establish links with the gateway nodes in a multi-hop fashion, if required.

This fusion of ad hoc network and infrastructure-based network properties allows significant extension of the coverage area of the network and enables nodes in areas with poor signal propagation properties to contact the outside world with higher probability of success. The overall effect of such a deployment is thus a substantial reduction in the number of deployed gateway nodes needed to cover a zone and consequently the overhead for installation, operational, and maintenance expenses.

Furthermore, such a network structure also allows for scalable operations simply by adding new gateway nodes when and where required. The reliability also improves as multiple paths to gateway nodes are more common and re-routing of packets in case of node failures is handled as in ad hoc networks.

Because of the above mentioned advantages, even traditional infrastructure-based networks such as cellular networks are beginning to look at possibilities of migrating to either WMN-based or WMN-like topologies [21, 22, 24, 28]. The promises are several: reducing infrastructure setup and maintenance cost, improving fault tolerance and robustness in the face of unnatural events and natural calamities and enhancing network coverage, among others. However, with the use of a mesh architecture, the importance of the channel assignment problem becomes even more accentuated due to the increased probability of interference between the links of a WMN, as most applications of WMNs (e.g., dense sensor networks) tend to contain more links than traditional cellular and ad hoc networks in an attempt to improve reliability through path diversity [28, 27, 26, 25]. As an example, Lan and Trang [23] proposed a technique for channel assignment in WMNs with multiple channels and multiple radios at each node of the network for increasing the network throughput.

## 1.1.9 Vehicular Ad Hoc Networks (VANETs)

A vehicular ad hoc network (VANET) is a mobile ad hoc network (MANET) consisting of moving cars. Each participating car acts as a wireless router or node of the network, with a range of about 100 to 300 metres. If cars move out of the signal range, they will drop out of the network, while other cars may also join the network. Design of such a network was first intended to enable police and fire vehicles to communicate with each other for security and safety purposes on highways.

## 1.1.10 Wireless Sensor Networks

A wireless sensor network (WSN) or simply a sensor network, is a network of spatially distributed autonomous sensors to monitor physical or environmental conditions, such as temperature, sound, pressure, etc. and cooperatively pass their data through the network to a sink node at some location, as shown in Figure 1.3. The more modern networks are bi-directional so as to enable control of sensor activity.

Each such sensor network node has several parts: a radio transceiver with an internal antenna or connection to an external antenna, a microcontroller, an electronic circuit for interfacing with the sensors and an energy source, usually a battery or embedded form of energy harvesting. A sensor node may vary in size from that of a shoe box down to the size of a grain of dust, although functioning *motes* of genuine microscopic dimensions have yet to be created. The cost of sensor nodes is similarly variable, ranging from a few to hundreds of dollars, depending on the complexity of the individual nodes. Size and cost constraints on sensor nodes result in corresponding constraints on resources such as energy, memory, computational speed and communications bandwidth.

A sensor network may be thought to be an ad hoc network. Just like the ad hoc networks, a sensor network may also be static or mobile. For example, a surveillance system with large static camera sensors deployed in a mall or supermarket is a static sensor network, while very light and small-sized sensors deployed over a terrain where the sensors can float around or may change their positions over time constitute a mobile sensor network. Communication protocols in wireless sensor networks may be developed in a similar way as those in ad hoc networks. Data propagation among the sensor nodes may be through point-to-point communication or through flooding using broadcast or multicast.

Sensor networks find applications in a large number of real-life situations including environmental monitoring, inventory control in industries, process control, machine health monitoring, smart home building, monitoring of structures of large buildings and bridges, military and surveillance control, and so on. Processing of data streams received continuously from the sensor nodes by the sensor nodes even with low computational resources for specific application purposes constitutes a challenging research problem.

## 1.1.11 Body Area Networks

A body area network (BAN) (also referred to as a wireless body area network (WBAN) or a body sensor network (BSN)) is a wireless network consisting of a set of wearable computing devices. In particular, the network consists of several miniaturized body sensor units (BSUs) together with a single body central unit (BCU). The technology for developing body area networks started around 1995 based on the idea of using wireless personal area network (WPAN) technologies for implementing communications on, near, and around the human body.

## 1.2 Effects of Mobility of Devices

A wireless mobile network, whether cellular, ad hoc or sensor, consists of mobile devices (telephones, sensors or other types of devices) which are invariably operated by batteries of limited energy because of the mobility of such devices. When the battery of a device is exhausted, the device leaves the communication network. This phenomenon may be effectively abstracted by a node/link failure in the network.

Apart from this energy constraint, such mobile devices, because of their small size for portability, usually have small computing resources in the form of memory, computational power and speed of computation. They are also usually constrained by low communication bandwidth.

Mobility of component nodes in a network also gives rise to additional problems in network connectivity, e.g., when a node moves out of the range of communication from all other nodes in the network, it is dropped from the network. On the other hand, new nodes are also usually allowed to join a network at any time and may request additional communication channels for sending their messages. Such an allocation of new channels to newly arrived nodes must avoid all types of channel interferences with the existing communicating nodes in the nearby region. The communication protocols of the network must take proper account of such a dynamic network topology arising from all these situations.

Further, when a mobile communicating node changes its geographical position, it is possible that the channels assigned to this node may interfere with the channels allocated to other communicating nodes in the vicinity of its new location. Accordingly, some new non-interfering channel needs to be assigned to this node very quickly so as not to disrupt communication which is already in progress.

Quality of Services (QoS) is an important consideration for developing the communication protocols in any network. The quality of service is usually expressed or demanded by the users in several different forms like delay (time required for communicating packets of message), delay jitter (i.e., variation in delay for communicating different packets of the same message), reliability (e.g., bit error rate (BER)), availability of channels, and the bandwidth of communication under different application environments.
