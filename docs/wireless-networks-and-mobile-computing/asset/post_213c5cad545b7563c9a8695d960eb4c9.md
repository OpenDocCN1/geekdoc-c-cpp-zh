In this method, in a two-dimensional geometry, the distances of the mobile terminal in question are measured from three reference points which are not collinear. By drawing three circles centered at these three reference points with these measured distances of the reference points from the terminal as the radii of these circles, we can estimate the coordinates of the mobile terminal. The distance of the mobile terminal from a reference point can be determined by sending a signal (at ultrasonic or radio frequency) from the reference point to the mobile terminal and then measuring the time of flight of the signal. This is also called the time of arrival (TOA) technique for finding the location of the mobile terminal. Such an estimate of the distance is possible if there is a direct path or a line of sight (LOS) communication between the transmitter and the receiver. Non-line of sight or NLOS signals occur under multi-path conditions when the received signals come from reflected, diffracted or scattered paths, thus introducing excess path lengths into the Euclidean distance between the transmitter and the receiver. The NLOS error is defined as the excess distance traversed compared to the direct path and is always positive. The corruption of LOS signals by NLOS signals and Gaussian measurement noise are the major sources of error in all location estimation systems; the former is the dominant factor [2].

Another possible way of locating a mobile terminal is by measuring the angle of arrival (AOA) in which the transmitter sends a signal which is received by the receiver with the help of a highly directional antenna so that the receiver can estimate the angle of arrival of the received signal with respect to a reference direction. By noting the directions of receipt of the LOS signals by the nodes at two reference points, the position of the mobile terminal can be found from the point of intersection of two straight lines.

The first and arguably archetypal indoor location discovery system was the Active Badge Location System [31]. The Active Bat system used time difference of arrival technique [32]. Complementing the Active Bat was the Cricket system [5] which implemented both lateration and proximity techniques. It had less precision than the Active Bat, but had the advantages of privacy support and a decentralized scalability. RADAR [4] was a building-wide tracking system developed by Microsoft. It used a building signal characteristic model for location estimation. Unfortunately, movements of people or objects may necessitate reconstruction of the pre-defined signal-strength database or creation of an entirely new database.

The Global Positioning System (GPS) [3, 36] is perhaps the most widely publicized location-sensing system. Unfortunately, GPS does not scale well in dense urban areas or in indoor locations. Modeling of the radio propagation environment provides a more accurate location estimate by mitigating the effect of NLOS errors. While reasonably accurate radio propagation models exist for outdoor conditions [37, 38, 39], unfortunately there are no such unanimously accepted models for indoor environments.

Authors in [6] modeled distance errors for a sample office environment, measured using TOA techniques. Their simulation results show that LOS signals can be modeled as a Gaussian distribution while the obstructed LOS signals may be treated as a combination of Gaussian and exponential distributions for the environment considered. Authors in [6, 41] demonstrated that the bandwidth of the channel plays an important role in the magnitude of errors introduced and that a minimum bandwidth of 100 MHz is required for effective location estimation.

Several authors have attempted to mitigate the effect of NLOS errors [6, 42, 43, 1, 45, 4, 46, 47, 48, 49]. The least squares (LS) algorithm [50, 51], uses the Davidon algorithm [52] to find a location estimate that would minimize the residual of the estimate. The residual weighting algorithm (Rwgh) was proposed in [42] as another way to mitigate the effects of errors in distance measurements caused by NLOS propagations. Authors in [50] compare different geolocation algorithms for indoor environments, assuming the channel characteristics proposed in [6]. Authors in [43] proposed two algorithms for identification and correction of NLOS errors, based on _a priori_ knowledge of the NLOS errors. An NLOS error mitigation algorithm based on maximum likelihood detection has been presented in [53]. Another approach to location estimation was presented in [1], where the authors attempted to distinguish between LOS and NLOS measurements by assuming a statistical distribution of thermal receiver noise and signal characteristics.

Authors in [54] considered the problem of position estimation in large area networks, particularly sensor networks. They based their algorithm on measurement of signal strengths or time difference of arrival of signals and then the use of statistical methods to characterize the separation distances between two nodes. APS [55] is another location estimation algorithm that does not use GPS. However, the use of a hop-by-hop propagation process, such as DV hop or DV distance to propagate location estimates makes it unsuitable for mobile and random graphs. Authors in [29] presented a location estimation algorithm based on triangulation technique of signals in a cellular mobile network that is suitable for outdoor environments. Authors in [56] presented another GPS-free location estimation technique for mobile ad hoc networks. A common characteristic of all of the above algorithms is that they compute a single location point as an estimate. However, any estimation is a probabilistic measure and hence may be considerably different from the true scenario in some cases, especially for dense urban or indoor environments where NLOS errors are functions of location and time.

## 1.6 Issues in Sensor Networks

The important issues to be considered in sensor networks include the following:

- Placement of sensors
- Communication protocols
- Computation with streaming data
- Energy
- Security

The first issue of deploying the sensors at strategic locations is important for several reasons depending on the application requirements. For example, for a video surveillance system using cameras as sensors in a shopping mall or a library, the visibility of all points of the mall or library by the set of camera sensors is essential. In another application of weather sensors, the placement should be such that all the sensors are able to communicate their sensed data to the sink location through other sensors using minimum energy for transmission and reception of all messages. We consider this aspect of placement in more details in Chapter 7.

The issues of communication protocols, energy and security are similar to those in ad hoc networks. However, depending on the typical application requirement of the sensed data, some specific energy-efficient algorithms can be developed for the application layer of the communication protocol stack in some special types of sensor networks by exploiting the impact of the computation process on the sensed data.
