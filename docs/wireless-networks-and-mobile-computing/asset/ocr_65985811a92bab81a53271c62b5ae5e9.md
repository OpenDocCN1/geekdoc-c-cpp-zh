The seven-node critical block fails to give the optimal number of slots as it is possible for one of the nodes adjacent to a node of the critical block (but not a part of it), to have a demand that exceeds the sum of the demands of its Distance 3 neighbors in the critical block. From the cellular graph we see that for every peripheral node of the critical block, there are three neighbors at Distance 3 from some other peripheral node of the critical block. Consider, for example, node f in Figure 5.7 with neighbors c, g and j. The slots assigned to node d can be re-used to satisfy the demands of all three nodes while the slots assigned to node a can be re-used to satisfy the demands of only j and g, and the slots assigned to node h can only be re-used to satisfy the demands of only c and g. Hence, each of these three neighbors is a potential source of excess demand over that of the \(\mathcal{D}_7^{(2)}^*\), either individually or in combination with the others. This suggests that it is necessary to include these three nodes in computing the optimal number of slots.
