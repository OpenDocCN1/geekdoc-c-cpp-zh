Due considerations need to be given for developing robust communication protocols for maintaining the desired QoS under all dynamic network conditions as discussed above that may arise from mobility of the component nodes.

## 1.3 Issues in Cellular Mobile Networks

As evident from the requirements of the cellular mobile networks described above, we essentially need to i) assign a channel to a new call, avoiding all sorts of channel interferences from other calls and ii) maintain the communication between any two nodes even when one or both of them move from one place to another.

Apart from these two essential factors, the next important concerns are the energy-efficiency in implementing the communication protocols and security of messages to be communicated. Thus, broadly speaking, we need the following four issues to be resolved when implementing a cellular mobile network.

- Mobility management
- Bandwidth management
- Energy management
- Security

### 1.3.1 Mobility Management

In order to achieve the major goal of mobile computing in a cellular mobile network, i.e., the ability of computing at any place at any instant of time even when people are on the move, the important concern is, of course, to allow a user to make a call from any place (cell) to any other person in the network and to continue the call even when one or both of them are moving. The following four activities are involved in implementing this requirement.

- Call setup
- Handoff management
- Internetworking and interoperability

We give a brief introduction to these activities as follows:

### 1.3.1.1 Call Setup

In order to set up a call from a mobile terminal (MT) to another MT, two distinct activities are involved: i) location registration and ii) call delivery. These two activities are known as location management functions. Each mobile terminal is required to update its location information periodically or at least when it changes its location. For this purpose, usually a number of cells are grouped together to form a location area (LA) and a mobile switching center (MSC) controls several such LAs. Whenever an MT moves from one LA to another LA, the MT initiates a request to its current MSC for updating the location databases. On the other hand, call delivery involves querying the location databases to find the current location of the called MT. We discuss these two activities in detail in Chapter 2.

### 1.3.1.2 Handoff Management

When an MT changes its position from one cell to another cell, the strength of the signal received by it from the base station (BS) of the old cell becomes less than that from the base station of the new cell. Accordingly, the control of this MT is to be handed over from its old BS to the new BS. A smooth handoff of control of the MT from the old BS to the new one without hampering the ongoing communication with this MT, if any, is known as handoff management. In other words, handoff or handover management enables the network to maintain a user’s connection when the user (MT) continues to move and changes its access point from one BS to another BS. There are three distinct stages for handoff: i) handoff initiation, ii) new connection generation and iii) data flow control. In the first stage, handoff may be initiated by a user or MT when it receives the signal from its BS at a very low strength (lower than some threshold value) or it may be initiated by network software (either due to weak signals received from an MT or due to some changing network conditions, e.g., the communication channel used by the MT may be changed by executing a channel allocation algorithm at the base station to satisfy the channel demands of other MTs in the network). The second stage of handoff management, new connection generation, may be performed in three different ways. In case of network-controlled handoff (NCHO) or mobile-assisted handoff (MAHO), the network generates a new connection and finds the resources and the additional routing operations. In mobile-controlled handoff (MCHO), the MT finds the new resources and the network approves them. The third stage, i.e., data flow control, involves the delivery of data from the old connection path to the new connection path according to the agreed-upon services to be provided to the MT. In Chapter 2, we address handoff management in somewhat more detail.

### 1.3.1.3 Internetworking and Interoperability

There are different backbone networks which can support cellular mobile communication. Examples of such backbone networks include public land mobile networks (PLMNs), mobile IP networks, wireless asynchronous transfer mode (WATM) networks, low earth orbit (LEO) satellite networks, and so on. Each such backbone network has its own kind of signal propagation techniques and communication protocols to provide services to cellular mobile users. While roaming, it is quite possible that an MT moves from one place to another where the supporting backbone network is changed. The future generation mobile communication system aims at global roaming capability for mobile users. In order to implement this idea, the associated problem of internetworking must be properly addressed. The mobile devices need to be equipped with the appropriate hardware (physical layer and MAC layer issues) and software (network layer issues) to operate in different backbone networks during roaming. Also, this switching from one backbone network to another should be smooth and transparent to the users without disrupting the ongoing calls of the moving MTs. The location registration, call delivery and handoff management techniques must also be appropriately handled for a smooth switching from one backbone network to another.

Likewise, an MT may also move from one area to another where the backbone network remains unaltered, but the service provider is changed causing corresponding changes in communication techniques, protocols and service level agreements (e.g., tariffs) between the user and the service provider. For example, different service providers may operate over different frequency bands for message communication, and use different channel access mechanisms. One may use frequency division multiple access (FDMA) to access the channel, while the other may use code division multiple access (CDMA). The issues of compatibility of the mobile devices and the associated communication protocols, and designing the appropriate location registration, call delivery, handoff management techniques for such interoperability of the mobile services need also to be addressed to provide roaming facilities across different service providers. All these issues related to internetworking and interoperability constitute an important area of future research and development for providing the effective global roaming facilities to the mobile terminals.

### 1.3.2 Bandwidth Management

Because of the extreme popularity of mobile telephone systems, the number of cellular mobile users is increasing day by day almost exponentially. With about 200 million listed mobile users in 1997, the number has gone up to more than 6.8 billion in early 2015 and continues to increase at a high rate. Moreover, the users of mobile telephone systems no longer confine their use to voice messages and texts; multimedia signals including audio, still image and video are also communicated through cellular mobile networks.
