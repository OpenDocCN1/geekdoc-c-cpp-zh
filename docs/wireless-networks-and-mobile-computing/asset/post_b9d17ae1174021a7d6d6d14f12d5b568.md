During recent years, wireless communication has emerged as the most widely used technology in various application domains involving almost all spheres of our daily activities. Most notable of these applications is, of course, the mobile telephone system which has proved extremely useful and popular worldwide. Starting with about 200 million registered mobile users in 1997 [1], the number of users of the mobile telephone system increased to more than five billion by early 2014.

However, prior to the introduction of mobile telephone systems, wireless communication technology was also used extensively in other household and commercial devices like cordless telephones, remote controls for TV sets, cordless microphones, and so on. Over the past two decades, the world has experienced a phenomenal growth in communication science and technology, particularly in optical communication and wireless communication. Many types of communication networks like ZigBee networks, Wi‑Fi networks, cellular mobile networks, ad‑hoc networks, sensor networks and many other such networks based on wireless communication technology have been introduced for real‑life applications ranging from personal communication, entertainment, to rural and urban healthcare, smart home building, inventory control, surveillance, police and security control, military and defense systems, and

so on. A brief review of various application areas of wireless communication technology is given in the following section.

# 1.1 Applications Involving Wireless Communication

# 1.1.1 Cordless Telephones

A cordless telephone is a replacement of the handset cord of a landline telephone receiver set by means of a radio link so that the handset can be physically detached from the remaining part of the receiver set (may be considered the _base station_ of the cordless telephone) [2]. This modified handset communicates with the base station which, in turn, is connected to a fixed telephone line. The communication range of the handset is usually very small, just sufficient for covering short distances within a room or building. Unlike a corded telephone, a cordless telephone needs supply of power from the mains for its base station to work. The cordless handset is powered by a rechargeable battery, which is charged when the handset is placed on its cradle.

In 1994, digital cordless telephones operating around 900 MHz frequency range were introduced. Use of digital signals enabled the phones to be more secure (decreased eavesdropping) than those using analog signals. In 1995, the digital spread spectrum (DSS) technique was introduced for cordless phones. This technology enabled the digital information between the receiver and the base station of the cordless telephone to be spread in pieces over several frequencies, thereby making it very difficult for anyone to eavesdrop on the cordless conversations.

A cordless microphone has no physical cable connecting it directly to the sound recording or amplifying equipment with which it is associated [3]. This is also alternatively known as a radio microphone or a wireless microphone. Its body contains a small, battery‑powered radio transmitter which transmits the audio signal from the microphone by modulated radio waves to a nearby radio receiver unit, which recovers the audio from the received radio signal by appropriate demodulation technique. The rest of the audio equipment is connected to the receiver unit by a cable. Cordless microphones are widely used in the entertainment industry, television broadcasting, seminars and interviews to allow the audience to speak and also the interviewers or performers or entertainers to move about freely while using a microphone to amplify their voices.

There are many different standards, frequencies and transmission technologies used to replace the microphone’s cable connection and make it into a cordless or wireless device. Users may transmit, for example, radio waves over the UHF (300 MHz to 3 GHz) or VHF (30 MHz to 300 MHz) frequency bands and use FM, AM, or various digital modulation schemes. Some low‑cost models may, however, use infrared light. Infrared microphones require a direct line of sight communication between the microphone and the receiver, while the costlier radio‑frequency models do not have such constraints. Some models operate on a single fixed frequency, but the more advanced models operate on a user‑selectable frequency to avoid interference and allow the use of several microphones in nearby places at the same time.

Wi‑Fi, also spelled Wifi or WiFi [4], is a technology that allows electronic devices to exchange data or connect to the Internet through wireless communication using 2.4 GHz signals (in the UHF band) or 5 GHz signals (in the super high frequency or SHF band of 3 GHz to 30 GHz). Wi‑Fi is also used synonymously to indicate a wireless local area network (WLAN) based on the IEEE 802.11 protocol.

Many devices can use Wi‑Fi, e.g., personal computers, laptops, video‑game consoles, smartphones, some digital cameras, tablet computers and digital audio players. These can connect to a network resource such as the Internet via a wireless network access point. Such an access point (or hotspot) has a range of about 20 meters (66 feet) indoors and a greater range outdoors. Hotspot coverage can cover an area as small as a single room with walls that block radio waves or as large as many square kilometres achieved by using multiple overlapping access points.

Wi‑Fi can be less secure than wired connections (e.g., Ethernet) because an intruder does not need a physical connection to such a network. Web pages that use the secure sockets layer (SSL) – a protocol used above the transport layer for encrypting information over the Internet – are secure but unencrypted Internet access can easily be detected by intruders. Because of this, Wi‑Fi has adopted various encryption technologies. The early WEP (wired equivalent privacy) encryption, introduced in 1999 for IEEE 802.11 wireless networks has been proven easy to break. Higher quality protocols such as WPA (Wi‑Fi protected access) and WPA2 were introduced in 2003 and 2004, respectively.

# 1.1.4 ZigBee Networks

ZigBee [5] is a specification for a suite of high‑level communication protocols used to create personal area networks built from small, low‑power digital radios. It was conceived in 1998, standardized in 2003, and revised in 2006.

ZigBee is used in applications that require a low data rate, long battery life, and secure networking. It has a defined rate of 250 Kbit/s, best suited for periodic or intermittent data or a single signal transmission from a sensor or input device. Typical applications include wireless light switches, electrical meters with in‑home displays, traffic management systems, and other consumer and industrial devices that require short‑range wireless transfer of data at relatively low rates.

ZigBee is based on the IEEE 802.15 standard. Though low‑powered, ZigBee devices can transmit data over long distances by passing data through intermediate nodes (devices) to reach more distant ones in the network. The ZigBee network layer supports star, tree and generic mesh networks. Every network has a coordinator node. Within star networks, the coordinator must be the central node. Trees and meshes allow the use of ZigBee routers to extend communication at the network layer.

ZigBee networks are secured by 128‑bit symmetric encryption keys. In home automation applications, transmission distances range from 10 to 100 meters line‑of‑sight (LOS), depending on power output and environmental characteristics.

The technology defined by the ZigBee specification is intended to be simpler and less expensive than other wireless personal area networks (WPANs) using Wi‑Fi or Bluetooth standards (Bluetooth is a wireless technology standard for exchanging data over short distances using short wavelength UHF radio waves in the ISM (industrial, scientific and medical) band from 2.4 to 2.485 GHz introduced by telecom vendor Ericsson in 1994 as a wireless alternative to RS‑232 data cables).

ZigBee operates in the ISM radio bands: 868 MHz in Europe, 915 MHz in the USA and Australia and 2.4 GHz in other countries. Data transmission rates vary from 20 Kbps in the 868 MHz frequency band to 250 Kbps in the 2.4 GHz frequency band.

ZigBee builds upon the physical layer and media access control defined in IEEE Standard 802.15.4 (2003 version) for low data rate WPANs. The specification goes on to complete the standard by adding four main components: network layer, application layer, ZigBee device objects (ZDOs) and manufacturer‑defined application objects that allow for customization and favor total integration.
