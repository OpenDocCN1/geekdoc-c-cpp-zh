## 8.5.2.4 Collision Avoidance Issues

For contention-based medium access protocols, an issue that arises with the use of *N* symbols in the transmitted data is how the neighboring nodes determine whether a channel is free for transmission or whether there is an ongoing transmission containing a run of silent *N* symbols. An erroneous interpretation of the absence of any signal (carrier and/or baseband) can lead to corruption of an ongoing transmission (thus leading to retransmission and an increase in energy consumption by the communicating node pair) or unnecessary increase in communication delay due to waiting, when the channel is actually free.

To avoid the above mentioned issue, we can use one of the three schemes described in Section 8.3.4.3. Following the third scheme described there, a node attempts a transmission if it finds the channel silent for, say, eight consecutive symbol periods. In *CNS*, eight consecutive *N* symbols correspond to sixteen consecutive zero bits in the original message. Thus, occurrence of eight successive *N* symbols in the message implies that there is a run of zeros of length 16 in the message. Depending on the symbols before and after these consecutive eight *N* symbols, the original message may actually have a run of zeros of length 16, 17 or 18 corresponding to this occurrence of eight consecutive *N* symbols. Let $V_{cc}$ be the supply voltage and $I_{high}$ and $I_{low}$ denote currents drawn in the *transmit* (TX) and *idle* states, respectively. Let $I_0 = I_{high} - I_{low}$. We assume that the device moves from idle to TX state in time $t_{rise}$ and during this transient period draws a current $I_{tran}$. Also, let $t_p$ denote the duration of a symbol ($A$, $B$, $C$ or $N$) which is sufficiently larger than the switching time $t_{rise}$. Typically, for the CC1100 chip, the value of $I_{tran}$ is 8.7 mA. Similarly, when the device moves from the TX state to the idle state, it takes a finite time, say, $t_{fall}$. Usually $t_{fall}$ is very small. For example, for the CC1100 or CC2420 chips, the device moves from TX state to idle state very quickly drawing insignificant extra energy over that in the idle state. We therefore neglect the energy consumed by the device during this $t_{fall}$ time.

The energy expenditure for transmitting the equivalent radix 4 data frame using the CNS protocol can be represented as the sum of the following three terms:

+   1. A base energy value $E_{base}$ that is needed throughout the duration of the transmission, and is equal to the energy required when the device is in the *idle* state. Considering that the original binary data is of length $n$ bits, this energy required for transmitting all possible $4^{n/2}(=2^n)$ strings of length $n/2$ symbols each of the CNS data is given by

$$E_{base}=\frac{n}{2}2^n t_p I_{low}V_{cc}$$

+   2. Extra energy $E_{tx}$ in addition to the base energy required to transmit an $A$, $B$ or $C$ symbol in TX state. For an $n$-digit binary data frame, considering all

possible strings, we have $\frac{3n}{8}$ $A$, $B$ or $C$ symbols in the TX state. Thus,

$$E_{tx}=\frac{3n}{8}2^nt_pI_0V_{cc}$$

+   3. Extra energy $E_{rise}$ in addition to the base energy required during the edge transitions from an $N$ symbol to $A$, $B$ or $C$. Considering that the probability of occurrence of the $N$ symbols is $\frac{1}{4}$ in an $\frac{n}{2}$-symbol data stream, we get
+   
+   $$E_{rise}=\frac{n}{8}2^nt_{rise}(I_{tran}-I_{low})V_{cc}$$

Hence, the total energy required to transmit an $n/2$-digit CNS data frame is

$$\begin{aligned} E_{cns} &= E_{base}+E_{tx}+E_{rise} \\ &= V_{cc}2^n\left[\frac{n}{2}t_pI_{low}+\frac{3n}{8}I_0t_p+\frac{n}{8}t_{rise}(I_{tran}-I_{low})\right]. \end{aligned} \tag{8.34}$$

In contrast, the total energy consumed by using the EbT scheme for transmitting an $n$-bit binary data frame, is $E'=n2^nt_p(I_{low}+I_0)V_{cc}$.
