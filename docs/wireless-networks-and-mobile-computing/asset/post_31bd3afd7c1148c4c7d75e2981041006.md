### 3.3 Lower Bounds on Bandwidth

As mentioned in the earlier subsection, because of the NP-completeness nature of the CAP, researchers attempted to solve the problem based on heuristic approaches or approximation algorithms. Many of these algorithms do not assess how far their results are away from optimality and some of them over-estimate the frequencies by more than 100% [41, 42]. As a result, even if by some lucky coincidence, an optimal assignment is found, it may not be recognized as optimal unless one has a clear idea about the lower bound on the required bandwidth. In heuristic approaches like the genetic algorithm (GA), the process usually terminates after a certain number of iterations. Hence, a prior idea about the lower bound on the required bandwidth will be very useful in deciding the termination conditions of these algorithms. Furthermore, in the approaches based on neural networks, simulated annealing, and tabu search, the techniques typically start from some known lower bounds and then gradually improve the results through each iteration.

The problem of determining the lower bounds on bandwidth has been extensively studied by several authors [20, 1, 29, 28, 42, 43, 7, 45, 46]. In [43], a lower bound on bandwidth developed for CAP considered only the co-channel interference constraints. In [42], Gamst et al. presented some lower bounds on bandwidth for CAP that took the additional constraints for adjacent channel interferences and co-site interferences into account. Improving these results, Tcha et al. [7] presented some results on the lower bound which, however, did not explicitly include the co-site constraints. In [20], the authors derived a lower bound which, in some cases, was tighter than those presented in [42] considering all the co-channel, adjacent channel, and co-site constraints. In [44], new lower bounds were generalizations of the bounds presented in [20, 42]. All these lower bounds are defined on a general network of arbitrary cell structure.

For the special case of hexagonal cellular networks, the problem of determining the lower bounds has been studied by several authors [29, 28]. In this special type of network, a *k*-band buffering exists in the network if the channels assigned to any two calls in two cells which are more than distance *k* apart do not interfere with each other. In other words, for a *k*-band buffering network we need to define *k* + 1 constants $s_0, s_1, s_2, \cdots, s_k$ to specify the constraints for avoiding channel interferences where $s_i$, $0 \leq i \leq k$ denotes the minimum gap between two channels assigned to two calls in two cells which are distance *i* apart. Clearly, $s_0 \geq s_1 \geq s_2 \geq \cdots \geq s_k$. Authors in [29, 28] studied the problem of determining the lower bounds for a two-band buffering situation in hexagonal cellular networks using only the three parameters $s_0$, $s_1$, and $s_2$, where $s_0$, $s_1$, and $s_2$ are the minimum frequency separations required to avoid interference for calls in the same cell, cells at distances 1 and 2, respectively. They derived expressions for the lower bounds on bandwidths for different relative values of $s_0$, $s_1$, and $s_2$ for both uniform and non-uniform demand situations. These lower bounds have been computed based on the minimum bandwidth requirement for assigning frequencies to a seven-node subgraph as shown in Figure 3.4.
