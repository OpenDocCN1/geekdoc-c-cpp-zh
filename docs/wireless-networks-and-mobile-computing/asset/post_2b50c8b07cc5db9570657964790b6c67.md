The seven-node critical block fails to give the optimal number of slots as it is possible for one of the nodes adjacent to a node of the critical block (but not a part of it), to have a demand that exceeds the sum of the demands of its Distance 3 neighbors in the critical block. From the cellular graph we see that for every peripheral node of the critical block, there are three neighbors at Distance 3 from some other peripheral node of the critical block. Consider, for example, node f in Figure 5.7 with neighbors c, g and j. The slots assigned to node d can be re-used to satisfy the demands of all three nodes while the slots assigned to node a can be re-used to satisfy the demands of only j and g, and the slots assigned to node h can only be re-used to satisfy the demands of only c and g. Hence, each of these three neighbors is a potential source of excess demand over that of the \(\mathcal{D}_7^{(2)}^*\), either individually or in combination with the others. This suggests that it is necessary to include these three nodes in computing the optimal number of slots. Using an 8- or 9-node critical block would also fail to obtain a lower bound on the number of slots for the same reasons as for a seven-node critical block. So, we consider a ten-node block consisting of a seven-node Distance 2 clique and three other nodes outside this Distance 2 clique which are neighbors of a peripheral node of this Distance 2 clique. We thus get the following result.

**FIGURE 5.9:** Ten-node critical block

**Lemma 22.** *For a cellular network with a heterogeneous demand vector, to find the optimal bandwidth requirement of the network, it is necessary to consider a ten-node critical block, as using a critical block with fewer than ten nodes would not be sufficient to compute the minimum slot requirement of the network.*

To compute the demand of the ten-node critical block for which the number of slots will be maximum among all such ten-node blocks, let \(C=(V,E)\) be a cellular Distance 2 clique. Let $free_u$ denote the number of slots of node $u \in C$ that can be used by a node which is at a Distance 3 from $u$ and not a part of $C$, and $used_u(j)$ be the number of slots assigned to $u \in C$ that are re-used by node $j \notin C$ at a Distance 3 from $u$. Noting that $N_3(u)$ is the set of all Distance 3 neighbors of node $u$, we define residual demand $res_j$ of node $j \in N_1(i)$, $i \in C, j \notin C$ as

$$res_j = \max(0, w_j - \sum_{u \in N_3(j)\cap C} used_u(j)).$$

For $i \in C$, the sum of residual demands of $N_1(i)$ which are not in $C$ will be designated the residual sum of neighbors of $i$ and defined as

$$Res_i = \sum_{j \in N_1(i),\ j \notin C} res_j.$$

We demonstrate the procedure for computing the ten-node critical block with the help of the following example.

**Example 25.** *Consider the cellular graph shown in Figure 5.9. Let abcdefg be a candidate critical block. Without any loss of generality, we consider the three neighbors $x$, $y$ and $z$ of node $c$. Initially, $free_a = w_a$, $free_f = w_f$ and $free_e = w_e$. The computation of $Res_c$ would be as follows,*

- **Step 1:** Assign slots to node $x$ using maximum number of slots from node $e$, and the rest, if any, from the node $f$.

$$used_e(x) = \min(w_x, free_e); \quad free_e = free_e - used_e(x).$$

$$used_f(x) = \min(w_x - used_e(x), free_f); \quad free_f = free_f - used_f(x).$$

$$res_x = \max(0, w_x - (used_e(x) + used_f(x))).$$

- **Step 2:** Assign slots to node $z$ using maximum number of slots from node $a$, and the rest, if any, from the node $f$.

$$used_a(z) = \min(w_z, free_a); \quad free_a = free_a - used_a(z).$$

$$used_f(z) = \min(w_z - used_a(z), free_f); \quad free_f = free_f - used_f(z).$$

$$res_z = \max(0, w_z - (used_a(z) + used_f(z))).$$

- **Step 3:** Assign slots to *y* using available number of slots from nodes *e*, *a* and *f*.

\[ res_y = \max(0, w_y - (free_a + free_e + free_f)). \]

- **Step 4:** Sum the residual demands of *x*, *y* and *z*, i.e., \(Res_C = res_x + res_y + res_z\).

Let \(Res_{max}(C) = \max_{i \in C}[Res_i]\). Referring to Figure 5.9, let \(D_{10}^{(2)}(\mathcal{G})\) represent the demand of the ten-node subgraph, \(\mathcal{G} \equiv abcdefgxyz\), where \(D_{10}^{(2)}(\mathcal{G}) = D_7^{(2)}(C) + Res_{max}(C)\). The demand of the ten-node critical block, \(D_{10}^{(2)*}\), is then defined as the demand of a ten-node subgraph that has the maximal \(D_{10}^{(2)}(\mathcal{G})\) in the network, i.e.,

\[ D_{10}^{(2)*} = \max_{\mathcal{G}}[D_{10}^{(2)}(\mathcal{G})]. \]

Let \(\mathcal{R}_C\) represent the set of nodes outside \(C\) but adjacent to some peripheral node of \(C\), corresponding to \(Res_{max}(C)\). We call \(\mathcal{R}_C\) the *maximum residual set* of \(C\).

**Theorem 21.** *The demand sum* \(D_{10}^{(2)*}\) *is the optimal bandwidth requirement of a hexagonal cellular network having a heterogeneous demand vector.*

*Proof.* We established from Lemma 22 that it is necessary to consider at least a ten-node critical block to compute the minimum slot requirement of a cellular network. We now prove that the demand of a ten-node critical block gives the minimal bandwidth requirement of a hexagonal cellular network with a heterogeneous demand vector.

The nodes adjacent to the critical block have the least number of contributing nodes in the critical block to satisfy their demands. The further away a node is from the critical block, the higher is the number of contributing nodes in the critical block to satisfy its demand. Hence, proving that the demand of any Distance 2 clique adjacent to the critical block can be satisfied using only the slots assigned to the critical block, implies that the demand of all other Distance 2 cliques in the cellular graph can be satisfied using only the slots assigned to the ten-node critical block.

Suppose the nodes 1 to 10 in Figure 5.10 form the ten-node critical block in a cellular graph. The nodes 8, 9 and 10 form the maximum residual set corresponding to the critical block. Without any loss of generality, let nodes 11 and 12 be adjacent to the critical block whose demands need to be satisfied.

Let $S_i$ denote the set of slots allocated to node $i$.

Let $N_i$ denote the set of extra slots allocated to node $i$ after re-using the slots from the critical block.

The objective is to prove that the cardinality $|N_{11} \cup N_{12}|$ is always 0. Two scenarios can arise when attempting to assign slots to the nodes 11 and 12 while re-using the slots assigned to the critical block:

- **Case 1:** All slots of cells 1, 3 and 6 are utilized to satisfy demands of cells 8, 9 and 10.
- **Case 2:** Some slots of cells 1, 3 and 6 remain unused after satisfying demands of cells 8, 9 and 10.

We now prove that $|N_{11} \cup N_{12}| = 0$ under all assignment conditions. For this we consider the above cases separately.

![](img/6578f6fc95ac71d2c7109e594d9df12f_274_0.png)

**Proof of Case 1**: Assuming all slots of nodes 1, 3 and 6 are re-used to satisfy the demands of nodes 8, 9 and 10, we have:

- $S_8 \subset S_3 \cup S_6 \cup N_8$
- $S_9 \subset S_1 \cup S_3 \cup S_6 \cup N_9$
- $S_{10} \subset S_1 \cup S_3 \cup N_{10}$
- $S_{11} \subset S_1 \cup S_2 \cup S_3 \cup S_8 \cup N_{11}$
- $S_{12} \subset S_1 \cup S_2 \cup S_8 \cup S_9 \cup N_{12}$  (5.1)

The worst case scenario, where the minimum number of slots from the critical block can be re-used for nodes 11 and 12 arises when:

- $S_1$ is fully used in $S_{10}$.
- $S_3$ is fully used in satisfying $S_9$ and $S_{10}$.
