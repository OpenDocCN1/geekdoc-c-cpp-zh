As noted in Chapter 1, security is an important concern in all applications of wireless communication. There are different possible types of security threats in a wireless communication network, whether cellular, ad hoc, cognitive radio network, or wireless sensor network. Such threats threaten a network in multiple ways. An attacker may be a node of the network (called an intruder), or may be a node outside the network. For example, if we are concerned about message communication amongst the nodes of a MANET, an internal attacker may be a misbehaving or adversary node which is a member of the same network, while an external attacker may be a node which is not an integral part of the network.

Internal attacks may be of several types. For example, a node of the network may misbehave in such a way that it receives all messages coming from its neighbors, but never responds to those messages or never forwards them to their destinations. Thus, effectively, this attacker node behaves like an infinite sink for all messages.

Another kind of internal attack may be a reverse activity in the form of sending unnecessary or duplicate messages to other nodes of the network which would cause undue flooding of the network with useless messages, consuming a lot of communication bandwidth and node energy, thus disrupting or delaying the message communications. Some nodes may become non-operational if they are depleted of their stored battery energy due to undesirable flooding in the network.

In a third kind of more intelligent attack by an internal node, the attacker selectively suppresses received packets that are not forwarded to their destination nodes. The attacker node may also misdirect the packets and prevent them from reaching their true destinations by changing their destination addresses. Packets may be selected randomly or, more intelligently, by critical examination of their contents and limiting selection to packets containing important information.

A fourth kind of attack may be a little different. The attacker node misdirects a packet with changed route information in the message so that the packet moves around a local loop. Thus, the packet never reaches its destination, but the network resources of communication bandwidth, node energy, etc., are continuously wasted. This kind of attack is more serious than the third kind mentioned above.

In a fifth type, an internal node may be *jamming* by continually transmitting high intensity radio signals of the same frequency as that used by all the nodes in the network so that other nodes are unable to transmit their packets (for collision-free transmission) or nodes are unable to receive packets correctly due to the interference created by noise signals from the attacker node (bit-error rate of the receiver may be reduced due to reduced SNR). To measure the performance of the network under noisy situations, we usually define two parameters:

- **Packet Sending Ratio (PSR)** on the transmitter side, which is equal to *s/n*, where *s* is the number of packets actually sent by the transmitter (within a specified time-out period), given a total number of *n* generated packets (which are intended to be sent).
- **Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR)** on the receiver side, which is equal to *r/s*, where *r* is the number of correctly received packets out of the total of *s* packets sent by the transmitter.

Due to such jamming signals, both *PSR* and *PDR* are likely to be reduced. This type is called *constant jamming* to distinguish it from the other types of jamming which arise from the attack of the second type above.
