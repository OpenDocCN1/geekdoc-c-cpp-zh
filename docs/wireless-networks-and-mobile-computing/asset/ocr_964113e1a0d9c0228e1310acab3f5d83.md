Most commonly used proactive routing protocols include *Destination-Sequenced Distance Vector Routing (DSDV)* [21] and *Optimized Link State Routing (OLSR)* [20].

In reactive routing protocols, routes are not computed in advance. Rather, routes are discovered only when needed. If a source node wants to send a message to some destination node, it first examines its local routing table to see whether a route to the desired destination already exists. If no such route exists, it initiates a *route discovery* process to find a routing path to the destination node. Examples of such reactive routing protocols include *Ad Hoc On-Demand Distance Vector Routing (AODV)* [24], *Dynamic Source Routing (DSR)* [23], and *Temporally-Ordered Routing Algorithm (TORA)* [25].

Reactive routing protocols perform better than the proactive routing protocols due to reduced overhead in route discovery and route maintenance for low to moderate traffic loads in the network. However, if the traffic load is heavy, the signaling overhead in the route discovery of reactive protocols tends to become equal or even surpass that in proactive routing protocols. On the other hand, if the nodes in a MANET have a high mobility or network conditions change very frequently due to addition or removal of nodes or changing transmission conditions, the route maintenance overhead in proactive protocols may rise significantly (being triggered by specific events) and in these situations, reactive protocols may be better choices. Reactive protocols suffer from an additional drawback of a *latency* period of acquiring the route from the instant when the source node needs a route to a given destination. In proactive protocols, there is no such latency as the routes to all destinations are computed in advance at each source node.
