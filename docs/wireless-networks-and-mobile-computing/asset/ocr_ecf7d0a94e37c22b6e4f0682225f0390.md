Another possible way of locating a mobile terminal is by measuring the angle of arrival (AOA) in which the transmitter sends a signal which is received by the receiver with the help of a highly directional antenna so that the receiver can estimate the angle of arrival of the received signal with respect to a reference direction. By noting the directions of receipt of the LOS signals by the nodes at two reference points, the position of the mobile terminal can be found from the point of intersection of two straight lines.

The first and arguably archetypal indoor location discovery system was the Active Badge Location System [31]. The Active Bat system used time difference of arrival technique [32]. Complementing the Active Bat was the Cricket system [5] which implemented both lateration and proximity techniques. It had a less precision than the Active Bat, but had the advantages of privacy support and a decentralized scalability. RADAR [4] was a building-wide tracking system developed by Microsoft. It used a building signal characteristic model for location estimation. Unfortunately, movements of people or objects may necessitate reconstruction of the pre-defined signal-strength database or creation of an entirely new database.

The Global Positioning System (GPS) [3, 36] is perhaps the most widely publicized location-sensing system. Unfortunately, GPS does not scale well in dense urban areas or in indoor locations. Modeling of the radio propagation environment provides a more accurate location estimate by mitigating the effect of NLOS errors. While reasonably accurate radio propagation models exist for outdoor conditions [37, 38, 39], unfortunately there are no such unanimously accepted models for indoor environments.
