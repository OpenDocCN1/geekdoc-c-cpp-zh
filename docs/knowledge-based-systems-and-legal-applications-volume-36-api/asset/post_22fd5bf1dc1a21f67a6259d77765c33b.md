## Local mixture of initiative

Taking from. We have just seen how users sometimes took the initiative away from the system in order to resolve some local breakdown in communication. This was done by selecting the ‘next turn initiator’ ‘What?’, or more rarely ‘Why?’.

Other occasions on which users took initiative from the system involved the use of different devices. For example, several users selected the Let Me Speak button to self-select between the parts of a complex system answer. This was used in two ways. In the first, users self-selected to ask a different question as soon as they recognized that the answer to the last question was not the kind they wanted. In the second, users self-selected to volunteer information made by the beginning of the answer. This is shown in the sequence below, in which a student volunteers the fact that Maggie is not receiving any one-parent benefit, after becoming convinced by the first part of the system answer that this is a benefit Maggie should be getting. Her expectation in volunteering this information was that ‘now it should tell me I should be getting some'

Taking the initiative from the system did not always work smoothly. A variety of problems in the switching of initiative had to do with confusion over the ownership of utterances appearing on the right-hand side of the screen. Although users were told clearly at the beginning of the interaction that all utterances on the right represented their talk to the system, there was a tendency for them to hear some of them system talk to them.

Part of the problem was the ambiguous nature of the language used. Both user and system talk was expressed in the first person, so that each party referred to themselves as ‘I’ and to each other as ‘you’. Thus any utterance such as ‘Let me tell you something,’ could be read in two ways. Utterances on the right of the screen were particularly prone to misinterpretation because they could only be selected by the user by the system. This led to a dangerous confusion of ambiguous utterances being presented to users by the system itself. One user expressed the general confusion in saying ‘I didn’t expect there to be two me’s’.

The effect of hearing user talk as system talk was to obscure the appearance of who was currently speaking. This in turn affected the ways user might take the initiative from the system. For example, several users attempting to take the initiative during a system pause, and happen to depress the mouse button during a garbage collection period de-activate. This means that any mouse click to select some screen option within the garbage collection period is not registered by the system and consequently fails to effect the corresponding function.

This can have quite devastating consequences for the switching of initiative if users are attempting to take the initiative by clicking on the 'Let me speak' button during a system pause. The effect is less severe in cases where screen options are displayed continuously, but undesirable consequences may still exist. For example, several users attempting to select punctuation at the ends of their utterances were slow to realize the selection had failed, and waited seconds for the system to respond.

Lastly, a tendency of the system to perform a low-level operation called garbage collection led to a general problem in micro-initiative. Garbage collection is a function carried out by the underlying software to 'clean up' the internal state of the system. It is carried out at irregular intervals and can last for irregular periods up to a second. The effect on the user is to de-activate the mouse and display the cursor in. In general garbage collection acts as a form of system inaction which users must learn to recognize and avoid from the colour of the cursor.

An alternative interpretation of ‘Let me speak’ led users to select it as a method of returning the initiative to the system; in practice, selecting 'Let me speak' has the opposite effect. The reasons for this behaviour are harder to understand, but may be related to a correct belief that utterances on the right-hand side of the screen are not actually spoken until selected.
