## 8.2. THE HISTORY OF THE POLICY APPLICATION

### 8.2.1. The phases of the project

The Alvey-DHSS Large Demonstrator project can be thought of as having two large phases of approximately equal length. The first, from the project's inception in 1984 until about October 1986, is a period of exploration and analysis, marked by the existence of many small teams developing many, small prototypes. Much of what these teams were doing was developing an understanding of the application areas and testing the feasibility of techniques and approaches. There was also a certain amount of work on infrastructure and general-purpose software. At a social level, there was energy spent on team-building and learning in a large and widespread collaboration. For very many individuals there was a great deal of learning to be done, not only about the application areas but also the tools and techniques that would be used on the project. At this time, the application teams, including policy, were each relatively self-contained and did all of the analysis, design and implementation within their areas.

In October 1986, a mid-term review of the project was completed and its results published. In brief, they were:

- 1. To reorganise the project into three ‘applications’ (previously there had been five).
- 2. To create a more formal split between analysis, design and implementation.
- 3. To use the Local Office application as the vehicle for demonstrating both the ‘engineered and measured approach’ and the construction of a ‘large’ knowledge base.

### 8.2.2. The policy application up to July 1987

As well as the overall project objectives, which are listed elsewhere (Chapter 2), the Policy Application was initially intended to produce (in the words of the proposal):

- 1. Policy database covering the principles, intent, explanation, justification and amendment of policy.
- 2. Advanced quantitative model to examine the financial and organisational implications of policy changes.

#### The sequence of events

A project workshop in summer 1984 was the real beginning for the policy team. At that meeting, the planning was done for the first six months of the project which included the initial analysis of the policy function. There followed a series of interviews which led to the production of a data flow diagram of the policy process and the six-stage model of policy decision making outlined above. A further useful outcome of this analysis was a hierarchy of policy concepts, intended to show the relationship between the terms used in the legislation, with those used in the formulation of policy goals, and those used in the legislative terms operational. The first six months of the policy application should be thought of as a probationary period as it was in this time that the feasibility of policy as an application area was assessed and the decision to pursue it further. The technical explorations of inference mechanisms appropriate to this activity, as the support of policy making proceeded parallel with this, the exploration of particular policy problems due to poverty traps for widows and invalids. After the winter project workshop of 1984/85 there was a simulation of the widows' invalid traps and a constraint model for consistency checking/evaluation of effects of changes. The feasibility prototype (P0), based on this simulation and the feasibility study for the planning component was completed by then. The addition of planning facilities to P0 was completed by October 1985. There followed a period of concentration on the interface to the functionality of P0 and a new prototype, P1, emerged in late 1986 followed by a much improved version (P1v2) ready for demonstration in summer, 1987.

### 8.2.3. The effects of the mid-term review

The policy application was, in the long-run, not much affected by the mid-term review as other applications. It had been, until that time, a coherent and technically successful group and was widely considered to be one of the most interesting and challenging areas in the project. It had also been fortunate in having within several of the project's most experienced and technically competent members. What is more, the difficulties of the period up to the production of P1 all came after the mid-term review. It survived the review, therefore, very little changed in terms of its scope and objectives. However, the constitution of the policy application Team did change considerably between October 1986 and July 1987. This period also found the team without any effective management and progress was made. The later (July 1987) reorganization of the project teams rectified this but the mid-term review can be thought of causing a long and wasteful hiatus in the progress of the policy application.

### 8.2.4. The policy application after July 1987

#### The objectives of the mid-term review,

After the mid-term review, the objectives of the policy application remained unchanged. As the local office application was to be the highest priority of the three and to become the focus for work on large knowledge bases, the requirement for the policy application to produce a large knowledge base or even a complete demonstrator was removed. Instead, it was felt that the policy application could take more risks and be more experimental in the approach to providing support. Nevertheless, the policy application team were still required to produce a convincing demonstration of knowledge-based support for DSS policy makers. The new policy application team was headed by Graham Storrs, who had joined the team in October, 1985. Andrew Taylor who had run the team since the beginning of the project left the organisation as Justin Foster who had been a prominent member of the team until that point. Trevor Bench-Capon, another of the team's most prominent members, had reduced involvement from about this time onwards when he took a lecturing appointment at Liverpool University. He did, however, continue to make a significant and important contribution.

#### The sequence of events

From its inception, the post-July 1987 policy application team had three main strands of activity: specifying and overseeing the production of the P1 (version 2) demonstrator, developing the requirements and designing P2, and developing the requirements and the design for P3. The P1v2 specification was produced in September 1987 and was built and demonstrable by the end of the year. It was available for demonstration at the project workshop in February 1988. The P2 functional specification was produced in January 1988 and had its first public demonstration in October 1988. The system had been ready for public demonstration in July 1988 when it was reviewed by a DSS policy maker. The P3 functional specification was produced in July 1988. P3 had its first public demonstration at the end-of-project demonstrations in April 1989. However, an earlier version had been previewed by the DSS in March 1989.

## 8.3. DESCRIPTION OF THE PROTOTYPES

In all, four demonstrators were produced:

#### The Policy System

- P0 (Also known as the feasibility prototype.) This demonstrated the feasibility of using a logical model of the legislation to answer queries in order to identify policy problems. In a later version, a knowledge-based planning component was added to guide the policy maker in deciding on possible changes to the legislation.

- P1 This had essentially the same functionality as P0 (without the planner) but had a considerably more sophisticated interface. The interface was based on a model of the policy task and included notions such as policy folders and specialized stationery. It also introduced the notion of public world and private versions of the actual legislation. Any number of private worlds could exist so that various policy options could be explored within the same policy problem. Queries were put to the system through forms and expression editors. These and rule and attribute editors were used by selecting appropriate components from menus. The results of queries were improved in formalized English. This prototype improved in a further version, P1v2.

- P2 This was a radical departure from the earlier prototypes and demonstrated support for the 'structuring' of policy problems (in a style similar to 'outliners' or 'ideas processors') and the editing of policy argumentation in a specially developed graphical representation. P2 attempted to show how the policy maker could be supported the whole process of policy formulation and was strongly oriented to the Department's working procedures. Problem structuring gave rise to a set of subproblems. Each of these provided the context for detailed argumentation about policy options. This argumentation could be commented on and developed by other policy workers and then reviewed and evaluated by the originating policy maker. A set of selected policy options could then be grouped and reorganized into a coherent set of recommendations in a process we called rationalisation. P2 was widely demonstrated and evaluated by the Department. In all cases it was well received.

- P3 This system attempted to combine the knowledge based P1 functionality and the P2 functionality into a complete demonstration of the kind of support system that was possible for policy-makers. From all stages of the policy formulation process (problem structuring, detailed argumentation, etc.) the policy worker had access to the logical model of legislation for the posing of queries or the 'what if' exploration of potential changes. The policy maker could also retrieve stored arguments, examine legislation and other text and word-process his or her comments and recommendations.
