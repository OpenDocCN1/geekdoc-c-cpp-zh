# 9.4.1. The history of the Advice System application

The proposal suggested that the Advice System would be the second of the claimant information systems to be built and design work on it did not begin until 1986. However, in early 1985, in anticipation of the need for a clear user requirement for the system, we started a study of potential users who continued until 1987. The results of this study were important in formulating the requirements specification [Buckland et al. 1987].

This requirements specification was used as the basis for the construction of an initial prototype which was demonstrated to the rest of the project in July 1987. A period of further research and design followed, culminating in a functional specification published in July 1988 [Robinson et al. 1988]. The demonstrator was built from this specification by the implementation team at ICL and was completed in time for the end of project demonstration in April 1989. However, the system ran slowly, making it difficult to evaluate with users. The therefore embarked on an almost complete reimplementation during the six months to September 1989. It was this third version which was evaluated in the period September to December 1989 and which is described in [Gilbert 1990].

# 9.4.2. The objectives of the Advice System

Most expert systems have been designed for use by professionals who are able to bring both understanding of the domain and some knowledge of computers to their interaction with the system. In particular, they can be assumed to know what the system is designed to do and what its limitations are likely to be. These assumptions about the user have to be relaxed as far as possible in a system designed for public use. The implications of designing for the public include: the concepts in terms of which the knowledge is structured must be eitherhensible or translatable to such concepts; the system should be capable of answering the types of questions which the users will wish to pose, not just those which are easy to answer using conventional techniques; the structure of the interaction with the user ought to be one which feels comfortable and natural. In addition, special methods have to be used to obtain a clear understanding of the requirements of a system designed for public use, since there is no well-defined and small set of users to approach, as would be the case in more conventional systems.
