# Knowledge analysis and representation

Knowledge about benefits used in building the Advice System came primarily from public documents about the regulations and procedures governing the social security system, and information elicited directly from the DSS, namely mainly for details about procedures which could not be found in official sources. The documents were of three kinds: 'official' regulations and manuals (for example, the DSS Adjudication Officers' Guide), published guides on welfare benefits (e.g. the Child Poverty Action Group's National Welfare Benefits Handbook and Ratfelder's How to Claim State benefits) and Little 'knowledge elicitation' of the conventional kind was required for the analysis of eligibility and assessment, because most of the knowledge system required was available in written form (Cordingley 1987, 1989). However, procedural information was less documented. However, procedural information was less documented and therefore interviews with DSS staff and professional workers were also necessary.

The analysis of the procedural information included identifying procedures common to more than one benefit at each stage of the process to see if a pattern could be isolated which would allow generality across benefits. The procedures for one means-tested benefit were represented in a hierarchical procedural network. This network was then customized to each of the other benefits to allow for variations. The decision on how fine-grained to take the analysis of procedures was determined by considering the types of procedural questions claimants might wish to ask and the types of problem claimants most commonly experience.

Knowledge concerning the regulations governing benefits was dealt with separately. Soon after beginning to study the task of analysing social security regulations, it became clear that attempting to encode regulations directly into some formal rule language was a very difficult task. Moreover, direct encoding meant explicit links between the source material and the rules were difficult to construct. In turn meant that the analysis was not available for scrutiny and maintenance. The first alternative to direct representation that we tried was a 'diagrammatic intermediate representation', a semi-formal graphical representation which could be linked back to the source and forwards to the rules which were derived from it [Cordingley 1988].
