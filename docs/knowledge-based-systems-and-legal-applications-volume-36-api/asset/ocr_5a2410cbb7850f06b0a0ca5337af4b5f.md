If we take the function of a legal KBS to provide support for the resolution of a whole case rather than a single point of law, we believe it is contentious to say that such a system must contain, or at least, some representation of the underlying legislation. For example, Rland and Skalak's CABERET system, described in [Rissland and Skalak 1989], combine rule-based reasoning, which directs attention to the points of law to be considered, by HYPO-like case-based reasoning to support the consideration of these points.

If we move from the construction of the knowledge base to its testing, we find again that the legal domain has some special characteristics. It is a significant difference in legal applications that the answer produced by a system is of less importance than the means used to arrive at the answer. Almost all workers in the area have noted this feature with respect to the role played by explanation. Most domains aim at allowing the user to invest some confidence in the answer, or to assist the system builder in debugging the knowledge base when a wrong answer is given. In a legal expert system in contrast, the method of arriving at an answer is of more importance than the answer itself, it needs to correspond to a potentially successful legal argument, and to be available to the user so he can strengthen or counter the points made depending on the side he wishes to argue. The user of a legal KBS wants to know how to win this point is not just that he ought to win it. This force of clause is not, however, connected to explanation, but also affects the knowledge representation: the conclusion must be demonstrably sound on acceptable legal reasoning the knowledge used in the system must also be demonstrably sound. How is such a demonstration to be achieved? Unfortunately it cannot be done by running sets of test cases through the system, but only by an actual scrutiny of the knowledge base itself. Which means that the knowledge base has to be in a form which is accessible to the expert who can assess its correctness, and to be structured in a way which corresponds to the sources from which it derives. Of course, the representation will be a paraphrase legally unacceptable in general: nonetheless the closer the represented knowledge to the source, the more acceptable and capable of validation it will.

Two chief consequences arise from the need for the knowledge base closely to follow the source, and to be available to the expert for audit. Firstly, there needs to be sufficient flexibility of syntax to accommodate the different ways of expressing equivalent statements that might be used in the legislation. Use of a canonical form inhibits intelligibility by forcing rearrangement of the source form. Secondly, the statements in the representation must be truly declarative.
