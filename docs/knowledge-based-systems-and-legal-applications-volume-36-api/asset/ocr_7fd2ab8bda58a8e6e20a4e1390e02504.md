The discussion throughout this section has concentrated on the conceptual issues in the task of the adjudicator officer. It was mentioned in the previous section, however, the practical, personal and contextual elements of the task were, perhaps, more important. The relevance of these aspects, however, was seen to lie in general requirements about the form, rather than the content, of the support given to adjudicator officers. Adjudication is like many other different types of decision-making tasks that it involves is carried out by people with different skills, it has to respond to different degrees of 'completeness' in the information which is available. These factors combine to the extent when if we look at a decision-maker tackling any single case or topic they will:

+   1. Call on a variety of knowledge sources and try a number different approaches to the case before deciding which is the best.

+   2. Do it differently from someone else with different experience and 'memory' may have done it — even though they may end up with precisely the same answer.

+   3. Do it differently next time.

Our conclusion was that the user of any suitable support system must be able to control the sequence in which they addressed issues in the assessment and the use of the system must be efficient in terms of not constraining the user to pursue unnecessary tasks. In addition, however, the system must constrain the user not to make mistakes in terms of contravening the Regulations, working with inconsistent or contradictory conclusions, or attempting to complete a case having failed to consider all the relevant information and conditions. The way in which these requirements were made more specific and reconciled with the objectives of the overall system, are discussed further in Chapter 7.
