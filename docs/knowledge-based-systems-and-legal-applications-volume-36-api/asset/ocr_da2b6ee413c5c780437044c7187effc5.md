Two general problems with ‘What?’ and ‘Why?’ are illustrated in sequence (6). First, users never knew which of the two were most likely to produce the most satisfactory system answer. In fact sometimes neither was chosen or a ‘Don’t Know’ user answer was chosen as a generalised way of initiating system repair. Second, these utterances only apply to the immediately prior utterance. These meant that they could not be used in situations where users wanted to query the whole of a previous segment of dialogue or to apply both questions sequentially to a system utterance. Thus in the example (6), the system applies the user’s ‘What?’ to the system’s previous ‘Sorry I can’t understand that’ utterance, rather than to its original ‘I still do not understand that. Please try again.

Lastly, the problem of interpreting some utterances on the right hand of the screen as system utterances also affected the giving of initiative to the system. Users selected a ‘Let me tell you something’ topic at the top of the screen in order to get the system to tell them something. One user even reported expecting yes/no options to follow this operation; a system question equivalent to ‘Do you want me to tell something?’ In addition, the user continuation marks ‘...’ were sometimes selected for the same purpose.

Again, misinterpretation of the ‘Let me tell you something’ option as a system utterance, led some users to wait for the system seemed to want to tell. Further unnecessary waiting resulted when users failed to recognize that the system had finished generating the next list of topic or phrase options for them to choose from in constructing the next part of their current utterance. This reflects a micro-level at which users can be given the initiative to speak in the interaction. When they receive the floor to construct an utterance, they must be given progressive lists of utterance options in a series of micro-exchanges within their own turn. The problem mentioned above, has to do with knowing when a microexchange has completed.
