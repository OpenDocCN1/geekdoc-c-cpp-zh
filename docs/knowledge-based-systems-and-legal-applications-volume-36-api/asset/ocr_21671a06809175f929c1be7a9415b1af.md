The analysis, design and development of the non-procedural
(domain) knowledge base is the subject of the rest of this section and
the AS portion of section 11.5. The analysis was centered at Surrey. I
worked on it alone in the early stages. Two colleagues seconded
to the D(H)SS for a period to work with me at Surrey on
tailed analysis and representation of Child Benefit knowledge. Later a
colleague from Logic moving between Cambridge and Surrey worked
 me to complete the analysis and develop the AS knowledge base.
We were always a small team and for most of the time working together
the the same place.

The knowledge analysis for the Advice System went through
several phases. During the first phase the benefit system as a whole was
examined and represented in a number of different ways. By July 1986
the process of claiming a benefit had been modelled to show the kinds
of knowledge an advice system in this domain might require; by Sep-
tember 1986 widow’s benefits had been examined and a modification of
systemic network grammar (e.g. Bliss et al. 1983) used to represent
some of its features; and by January 1987 in depth analysis of child
benefit had progressed far enough for intermediate representa-
t of its eligibility conditions to be designed and engineered with
[Cordingley]. The later stages involving a modified
’AND/’OR’ tree representing benefit eligibility conditions in such
detail the ‘leaves’ of the tree related to information the claimant
could be asked for, or would be information stored in the knowledge
base. Lastly, once it had been decided that system would give advice
on cases where the (possible) claimant would be a lone parent and a
range of appropriate benefits identified, analysis at a somewhat less
dailed level was undertaken and they were represented in various forms
including a class hierarchy and rules in the toolkit language. During this
final stage static, dynamic and procedural knowledge were all subject of
ysis and representation.

A brief discussion of four of the early exercises will give a flavour
of exploratory stage. Later development is treated in the section on
KB building below.

It had become clear from studies undertaken by the Surrey team -
the potential user study, the study of advice worker consultations, and
radio phone-in programs — members of the public did not concep-
tualize their problems or benefit provision in terms of administrative
structures (Social Security, National Health Service, Manpower Services
Commission, Local Authority and so on). Were not distinctions related
to funding (allocated by parliament and paid ‘as of right’, paid out of
‘public funds’ the national insurance scheme, industrial injuries scheme
s and so on) particularly pertinent to their view. Several conceptual maps
of U.K. benefit provisions were developed putting Social Security Benefits
the wider context of benefit provision.
