If we take the function of a legal KBS to provide support for the resolution of a whole case rather than a single point of law, we believe it is contentious to say that such a system must contain, or at least, some representation of the underlying legislation. For example, Rissland and Skalak's CABERET system, described in [Rissland and Skalak 1989], combine rule-based reasoning, which directs attention to the points of law to be considered, by HYPO-like case-based reasoning to support the consideration of these points.

If we move from the construction of the knowledge base to its testing, we find again that the legal domain has some special characteristics. It is a significant difference in legal applications that the answer produced by a system is of less importance than the means used to arrive at the answer. Almost all workers in the area have noted this feature with respect to the role played by explanation. Most domains aim at allowing the user to invest some confidence in the answer, or to assist the system builder in debugging the knowledge base when a wrong answer is given. In a legal expert system in contrast, the method of arriving at an answer is of more importance than the answer itself, it needs to correspond to a potentially successful legal argument, and to be available to the user so he can strengthen or counter the points made depending on the side he wishes to argue. The user of a legal KBS wants to know how to win this point is not just that he ought to win it. This force of clause is not, however, connected to explanation, but also affects the knowledge representation: the conclusion must be demonstrably sound on acceptable legal reasoning; the knowledge used in the system must also be demonstrably sound. How is such a demonstration to be achieved? Unfortunately it cannot be done by running sets of test cases through the system, but only by an actual scrutiny of the knowledge base itself. Which means that the knowledge base has to be in a form which is accessible to the expert who can assess its correctness, and to be structured in a way which corresponds to the sources from which it derives. Of course, the representation will be a paraphrase legally unacceptable in general: nonetheless the closer the represented knowledge to the source, the more acceptable and capable of validation it will.

Two chief consequences arise from the need for the knowledge base closely to follow the source, and to be available to the expert for audit. Firstly, there needs to be sufficient flexibility of syntax to accommodate the different ways of expressing equivalent statements that might be used in the legislation. Use of a canonical form inhibits intelligibility by forcing rearrangement of the source form. Secondly, the statements in the representation must be truly declarative. While almost all knowledge representation paradigms have declarativeness as an aspiration, in practice the use of, for example, conflict resolution strategies in production rule systems, means that it is not possible to detach a piece of a knowledge base from its context and consider its correctness in isolation. In many application areas, where we are interested in the effect of the representation, this matters only in so far as it makes the validation of the knowledge base a harder task. But in the legal domain, where the correct decision is only part of what is required, it presents a serious problem.

The same point is reinforced when we consider the maintenance of a legal KBS. Maintenance is a more pressing problem for a legal KBS in many other areas. For, if we are building a medical expert system we can expect the functioning of the human body to remain fairly constant (although knowledge of it may improve, and fashions in diagnosis and treatment change), legislation can be, and is amended, with disturbing frequency, and at any time a new decision on a given point may alter what the law on that particular matter is. Thus a legal knowledge base must be constructed with the certainty that those parts of it discerning legislation and case law will alter. Such alterations in legislation are typically localized: words are added to or deleted from a particular paragraph or section. It is desirable that the changes to the knowledge base be similarly localized, and this can only be so if the structure of the knowledge base reflects that of the legislation.

Lastly we need to mention the use of the system. Because the closer the represented knowledge to the source, the more comprehensible the process will be. From the early days of KBS it seemed that law was a very natural area, because these features, especially declarativeness, mentioned above, were claimed to distinguish KBS from existing systems. In practice, however, these qualities were often overstated for existing paradigms. A certain degree of disillusionment and need to be taken especially seriously when building legal applications.

## Legal Applications of KBS

### 12.3 ISOMPHISM TO LEGISLATION

In the previous section we stressed the need to make the represented knowledge correspond to the source. In this section we some of the implications of this for a knowledge representation is examined.

There should be identifiable items within the knowledge base which correspond to the items found in the legislation. If working within a rule based paradigm, this would ideally mean something like a single rule to express the content of each regulation, definition, etc. The need for isomorphism requires the syntax of rules to be sufficiently expressive to allow this one to correspondence. Furthermore, the internal structure of a rule should map clearly onto its representation; clauses should follow in the same order, linked by the same connectives. Neither of these conditions is fulfilled by PROLOG-style formalisations — for example, a definition of the form:

S1 A person shall be P if, and only if, he is Q or R and S would be represented by the two Horn:

HC1 p(X): q(X).
HC2 p(X): r(X), s(X).

Leaving aside the problem that one part of the biconditional in S1 is represented by the treatment of negation as failure within this paradigm (def by Kowalski (1989)), this has done some violence to the form of the original statement. HC1 and HC2 in fact translate

- S2a A person shall be P if he is Q
- S2b A person shall be P if he is R
- S2c No person shall be P unless he satisfies the conditions in S2a and S2b

Whilst S1 and S2 are logically equivalent, there may well have been a motive in choosing to express the conditions for P-ness in the form of S1 rather than S2. Moreover, neither HC1 nor HC2 can properly be considered in isolation. Several of the motives for isomorphism, particularly considerations deriving from ease of representation and scrutiny for validation are lost by the need to translate into a normal form.
