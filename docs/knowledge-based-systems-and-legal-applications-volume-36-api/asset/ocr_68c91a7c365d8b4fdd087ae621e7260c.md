Labelling the brackets with their logical operators did not eliminate the need to 'teach' people to read the diagrams, but once the principles had been introduced, the label was there as a constant reminder about the easily forgotten feature, the meaning of the brackets.

It became apparent that these diagrams were an effective way of representing the information coming into the consideration of, say, a bereaved woman's entitlement to contributory work's benefit and the 'outcomes' available when considering that information. The former comes from the left, is conceptualized in more detailed until the concepts are appropriate for making decisions. They then are elements of conditions which are grouped using square brackets labelled with the logical operators. The eventual 'outcomes' are given in boxes, a symbol I added to those provided by SGN. I also found it necessary to introduce a disjunctive, the back bracket labelled with an 'or'. This denotes that the path along the arrows would be followed if any one of the nodes 'applies'.

The steps along the path, what would usually be an arc or a link in most graphs, are not explicitly assigned any meaning. In this example, the meaning of the cause of death link (top left, the arrow following that node) is (unusually) given as part of the node label. It would be read in full as 'established being related to one of the following three cases'. The deceased is implicit and would be read as 'must be in terms of the following four characteristics'. Similarly for the claimant link AND for the contributions link (which should have been labelled AND special than EOR as in this version). Links at the next level have different meanings. They can be read 'can be given by one of the following values' or more simply 'is/was'. Links at the next level go into conditionals and have no meaning beyond the meaning of the conditional itself, i.e. 'if... logical operator ... then...'. Noting that the links have such different meaning leading of the links on the child benefit diagrams produced later to be labelled explicitly.

These were richly heterogeneous networks as the links and the nodes were concerned: quite unlike trees, which are designed to be single relation hierarchies. One such simple relation hierarchy is the decomposition tree where the relation connecting nodes is 'is part of' throughout. Another is one where the relation is 'a kind of' or 'is a'.
