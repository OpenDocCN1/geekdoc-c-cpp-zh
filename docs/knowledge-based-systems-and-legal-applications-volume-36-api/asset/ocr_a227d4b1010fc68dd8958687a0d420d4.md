# 13.6. CONCLUSION

This chapter aimed to show the importance of attending to the dynamics of the interaction when designing knowledge-based systems. Small changes in these dynamics can generally alter the way people use systems. We have also presented the dynamics by describing activities the user and system perform at the start, in the body and at the end of the interaction. In particular, we have described the interaction at two levels, the overall level of control and the local, and examined the places where transfer can take place at the local level in terms of giving and taking of turns. This may not be enough. In the Forms Helper interaction, the system had overall control guiding users from one question to the next. At a more local level, when users were given the turn they need not answer the suggested question. They could divert from it and answer one of their choice. Similarly, when a user was answering a question posed by the Advice System, the user could hold onto the turn after answering and volunteer a statement. Both activities occurred during what has been defined as a user's turn, both appear to be taking the initiative from the usual ‘flow of interaction’; ‘taking a turn’ from the system. This may imply that the notion of ‘mixed initiative’ should be further refined, perhaps in terms of multiple levels of initiative.

We have largely concerned in this chapter with the way control can be transferred between users and the two systems. It is possible to see other issues of initiative arising. When problems occur in the interaction, when either the user or system has trouble with what has gone in the other's turn, then it is the responsibility of one of the parties indicate that a problem occurred and each's responsibility to correct it, though they did not have to. Forms Helper II initiated the correction of a user's ‘error’ by putting a message at the bottom of the screen. It was then the user's responsibility to correct it. When users had problems, they could also initiate repair by asking the system for a special sequence when they had problems with the other's turns. We have also looked at the way either the system or the user can initiate the start, the end, and new topics for the interaction. Rather than refining the definition of mixed initiative, it may be more useful to examine the way people use the resources given to them by a computer system to perform these activities. Conversation analysis already has such a terminology for looking at the way people interact with each other in conversations. The turns are taken in conversations have been examined [Sacks et al. 1974]. So has the repair of trouble in conversations [Schegloff et al. 1977], the openings of conversations [Schegloff 1968], the closings of conversations and the negotiation of new topics [Schegloff and Sacks 1973].
