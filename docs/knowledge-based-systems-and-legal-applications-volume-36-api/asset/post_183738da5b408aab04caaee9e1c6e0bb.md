
## 16.2.1. A model of legal source knowledge

With this element of evaluation we have full circle, and are back with the prospect of revising legislation to more closely align effects with intent, or even with modifying the original intent itself. The relationships described in the foregoing discussion can be summarised in diagrammatic form to give a model of the types of knowledge involved in the formulation and application of law (see Figure 16.1).

This model captures all the elements we found when looking at sickness benefits. In other cases some of the levels will contain less knowledge than others: for example a new benefit will require time for a body of case law to be established. Also different fields of law may tend to place more or less importance on the different sorts of knowledge, but I would contend that these elements are to be found everywhere. Lastly, one should notice a key difference between the nodes, and the arcs which procedural knowledge enabling this knowledge to be applied.

In the next section we consider how such a model can be used to focus a discussion of legal knowledge based systems.

## 16.2.2. Applying the model to legal KBS

Given the model we can firstly divide tasks into those which operate at a single level and those which affect a transformation across levels. Then one task is to discover what a piece of legislation really means. In [Allen and Saxon 1987] there is a good discussion of the various ways in which a particular section of a statute may be interpreted, accounting how we interpret words as ‘except’, ‘if’ and ‘unless’ which give rise to differing logical structures for the statute. The tool they propose to assist in this interpretive process, which they call ‘normalization’, thus operates wholly within the legislation level. Similarly, tools designed to support the conceptual retrieval of case law are operating within that level. Other systems support the transformation from one level to another; many of the consultative expert systems in the domain support the mapping of legal concepts into the facts available to the user. Moreover, given the possibility of alternative mappings appropriate to different classes of user, there may potentially be several systems at a given level.

Applying this to the systems described in this book, we can see that the policy system was intended to support the transformation from intentions to legislation, and that the advice and local office systems were designed to support the transformation from legal concepts to ascertainable facts, each appropriate to a different user group.

The model shows that there are many possible types of task which are carried out in the legal domain, and we can characterize a KBS by reference to which task it supports. More importantly, given the correspondence between the levels at which the tasks are carried out and the knowledge used to carry them out, we can begin to see what sorts of knowledge should be incorporated within them. For typically different KBS will draw their knowledge from different levels; thus the knowledge represented in the British Nationality Act system of Sergot et al. [1986] is drawn almost exclusively from the level of legislation, whereas that represented in the Latent Damage Advisor of Capper and Susskind [1988] is to be found at the level of operationalisation, and mapping to the facts of a case. From the model we can see that a system intended to operate within a given level needs to represent the knowledge of that level, but need not represent knowledge from any other level. Thus the normalization system of Allen and Saxon make no reference to knowledge beyond the statute itself, although the user of the system will need to supply certain elements of judgement to clarify the logical structure of the statute when using the system. In contrast any system intended to support a transformation between levels, such as the Latent Damage Advisor, will need to represent knowledge at the two levels between which the transformation is to take place. The British Nationality Act representation, which was designed without any clear notion of a task to support is a different case.

The Social Security Act 1948 provides that a claim for a state pension can only be made by a person who has arrived at the age of retirement and who is a resident of the United Kingdom. The age of retirement for these purposes is defined as follows:

(a) in the case of a man, the age 65;
(b) in the case of a woman, the age 60.

The state pension is payable to any person who has met the contribution requirements. The contribution requirements are based on the number of years of contributions that the person has paid into the National Insurance scheme. A person will be entitled to a state pension if they have paid at least 10 years of contributions, and will be entitled to a full state pension if they have paid at least 35 years of contributions.

The state pension is calculated based on the number of contributions that the person has paid. The amount of the state pension is set by the government and is adjusted annually to account for inflation.

The state pension can be reduced in certain circumstances, for example, if the person has other income or if they are not living in the United Kingdom. The state pension can also be suspended if the person fails to make the required contributions.

The state pension is a vital part of the retirement system for many people. It provides a basic level of income in their old age. However, it is often not sufficient to provide a comfortable retirement, and many people need to rely on other savings or investments.
