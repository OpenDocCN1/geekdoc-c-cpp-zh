Part of the difference lies in the fact that the definition gives necessary and sufficient conditions, whereas the universal generalisation gives only a sufficient condition; but this is not all: for while finding that a person was not an unmarried man would lead us inevitably to conclude that he was not a bachelor, whereas this finding would lead us to suppose that he was not a Catholic priest, further evidence might lead us to reject indeed the universal generalisation. It might be recognized that Catholic priests could marry, and only one day may be women.


In [Brachman 1985], which offers an admirable critique of some of the shortcomings of frame representations as widely used, Brachman discusses definitions. Having established that frame systems which permit the cancellation of default values cannot be used to express necessary conditions he suggests that they are inadequate when dealing with sufficient conditions and so cannot express the difference between a definition and a universal generalisation. He then cites two arguments to suggest that this is important: that no lexical items have complete definitions case law give (e.g. [Bobrow and Winograd 1979]), or that critical definition is impossible for the majority of terms in which this is not good enough to rebut Brachman's criticisms in general, and this is even clearer if our domain is law. For the law is a field in which definitions, AI systems are interested (e.g. [Reiter 1978]). These arguments are clearly not enough to rebut our domain is where definitions, in the quasi-mathematical sense, are available, and where the need to give different treatment to definitions is evident. This latter point is clearly shown in [Schildt 1889] where, because of the nature of the domain, definitions give rise to conjunctions, which are closed, and the empirical observations of case law rise to disjunctions, which are open-ended. In his JURIS system (P & Q) is not equivalent to ~(P v Q), the first would be false and the second would be unknown (to allow the possibility of further clarification of the concept under consideration). This makes a sharp distinction between definitions and of disjunctions, but it restricts the use of conjunctions to generalizations whereas we believe that such a distinction needs to be pervasive in an area such as law where definitions are of such prime importance.
