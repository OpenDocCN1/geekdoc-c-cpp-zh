# 1.15.3. Meldman and Gardner chose very difficult areas of law as their

experimental domains. Assault and battery and offer and acceptance are
both areas of law that rely less on a detailed knowledge of technicalities
than they do on the need to apply common-sense. As McCarty points out
(section 1.12.1), it is the representation of the required common sense
that make these apparently 'simple' areas of law among the most difficult
to address computationally, and P partly with hindsight P he cites
battery and offer and acceptance as specific examples of law that
could be expected to cause most representational problems.

Gardner's project placed a great deal of emphasis on the treatment of
common-sense. The CSK rules in her system are an example of this

concern, but the whole work is largely dominated by consideration of the
common-sense required to understand the notion of offer and acceptance.
For example, a central concern in offer and acceptance is whether a-
icular form of words or does not constitute an offer, and whether
t form of the reply signals acceptance. Does a letter which mentions
t price of an item constitute an offer to at this price? Is there
difference if the letter arrives in direct reply to an inquiry? Does
matter whether the reply is sent in telegram rather than a letter?
These are some of the questions Gardner addressed in her project.
She devoted a great deal of effort to devising a method of representation
base Searle's theories of speech acts. In my opinion, all this-
tracted attention from the fundamental aims of her project which were to
address aspects of legal reasoning in terms of very difficult issues
directly concerned with the comprehension of natural language.

Others have also argued for the generation of alternative, conditional
solutions to account for the presence of undecided open-textured
concepts (see for example section 1.10.1 on logic programming techniques
and [Sergot 1985a]), but no one else has stressed this as much as
Gner. Neither has anyone else addressed directly to distinguish
'hard' questions from 'easy' ones. Gardner's motivation was essentially
simulate aspects of legal reasoning in an artificial intelligence approach,
but the question has practical considerations too. Without some
dinction all open textured concepts are 'hard' and the trees of
conditional analyses unusually bushy. I think this is the most
interesting part of Gardner's work and I think it is a great pity that she
did devote more attention to it in her account of the work.
