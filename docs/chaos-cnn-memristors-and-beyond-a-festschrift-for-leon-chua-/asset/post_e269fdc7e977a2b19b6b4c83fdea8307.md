## 3.1. Discrete circuit realization

In the discrete circuit realization, there is a one-to-one mapping between the differential equations and circuit elements. Each state variable requires a dynamic element (an inductor or capacitor) and the algebraic relations between state variables are realized by resistive elements. Thus, the circuit has two capacitors (C1 and C2), an inductor (L), a linear resistor R and a nonlinear resistor N_R.

The linear capacitors and the resistor are implemented with physical capacitors and a resistor, respectively. The inductor can be implemented as a physical inductor or using a gyrator terminated in a capacitor; we will consider the inductor in detail in Sec. 3.5.

The nonlinearity in Chua's circuit is required to have at least two unstable equilibrium points [4]. Thus, the V-I characteristic must intersect the load line defined by the linear resistor R at least two points. The nonlinearity can have any number of different shapes. If one also requires symmetry then one can consider at least two classes of nonlinear function: piecewise-linear and cubic. Symmetry will yield three equilibrium points. Several subcircuits have been proposed to realize the required characteristic; these are discussed in Secs. 3.2 and 3.3.

## 3.2. Piecewise-linear Chua diode

The simplest symmetric piecewise-linear Chua diode is one which has a large negative slope G_a in its middle region and a smaller negative slope G_b in the outer regions, as shown in Fig. 2.

Practical resistors are eventually passive, meaning that they must lie in the first and third quadrants of the V-I plane for large values of current and voltage. In the case of a piecewise-linear resistor, this means that the characteristic must have two additional segments which return the characteristic to the first and third quadrants. Hence the characteristic has five segments and four breakpoints, two of which are required in order to produce the three equilibrium points, and two which satisfy the eventual passivity condition. Thus, a physical realization of the V-I characteristic shown in Fig. 2 has two additional breakpoints at ±E' and slopes G_c in the outermost regions, as shown in Fig. 8. We will call the outer breakpoints parasitic in the sense that they are not necessary for the operation of the circuit, but result from the implementation. The simplest eventually passive symmetric piecewise-linear nonlinearity has a slope G_a in its middle region, slope G_b in the outer regions, and slope G_c in the outermost regions, as shown in Fig. 8.

If the segments associated with eventual passivity are so far away from the inner breakpoints that the trajectory never reaches them, then the practical implementation is correctly modeled by the original Chua's circuit equations with a symmetric three-segment nonlinearity. If the attractor is too large, it may enter the outermost regions associated with eventual passivity; this distorts the attractor [28]. A further non-ideality due to implementation results from asymmetry in the breakpoints which causes the attractor to be asymmetric. This effect is noticeable in the early experimental realizations of Chua's circuit [27, 28] (see Fig. 6) but the problem was solved by Kennedy [14]. Negative resistance may be produced by using a Negative Impedance Converter (NIC) terminated in a resistor [5, 13]. This turns a positive resistance into a negative resistance over the linear region of operation of the NIC. The NIC itself is eventually passive and therefore exhibits breakpoints associated with the extremities of the linear region of operation of the active element. The breakpoints in the Chua diode characteristic can be implemented in a number of ways including exploiting the eventual passivity of the NIC itself or by using resistor-diode networks.

### 3.2.1. Op amp-based negative impedance converters

In the first experimental implementation of Chua's circuit [27], the piecewise-linear nonlinear resistor was realized by connecting two NICs in parallel. One NIC provides the inner pair of breakpoints ±E, while the other provides the outer pair ±E'. The two-NIC implementation of Chua's circuit is shown in Fig. 9.

Op amp A1 and resistors R1-R3 implement one negative resistor, op amp A2 and resistor R4-R6 implement the other one. The slopes and breakpoints of the Chua diode are defined by:

```
G_a = - R2/(R1 R3) - R5/(R4 R6)   (5)
G_b = 1/R3 - R5/(R4 R6)   (6)
G_c = 1/R3 + 1/R6   (7)
E = R1/(R1+R2) E_sat   (8)
E' = R4/(R4+R5) E_sat   (9)
```

Figure 6 shows experimentally-observed projections of a chaotic attractor. Note that this attractor should be symmetric but it is not; the bottom lobe of the double scroll attractor is smaller than the upper lobe. The reason for this is that the breakpoints are determined by the positive and negative saturation levels (E_sat^+ and E_sat^-) of the op amps. In a 741 op amp with power rails that are symmetrically placed about zero, the saturation levels are not symmetric. In particular, E_sat^- < E_sat^+; this makes the "bottom" lobe of the attractor smaller than the top.

The breakpoints in the op amp-based two-NIC Chua diode are determined by the saturation levels the op amps, which in turn depend on the internal structure of the op amp and the power supplies. If the saturation levels are asymmetric about zero, so are the breakpoints and the resulting attractors, as we have seen. Matsumoto et al. proposed an alternative implementation of the Chua diode [22] where an op amp-based NIC provides the underlying nonlinear resistance Ga and the outer breakpoints required by eventual passivity.
