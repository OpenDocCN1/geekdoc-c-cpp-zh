## 5. Conclusions

From the date of invention and realization of Chua’s circuit, it took two years to publish a rigorous proof of chaos. In this chapter, we covered all the main ideas from this time period. This chapter has thus provided the reader with all the tools necessary for further study of chaos in piecewise-linear dynamical systems. Ever since Chua’s seminal work, a variety of other chaotic circuits have been discovered. Sprott’s “jerky dynamics” based chaotic circuits [17] are especially elegant. Other techniques for rigorous analysis of chaos also exist, such as ones involving topological horseshoe theory [21].

For future work, a particularly interesting concept to explore is the minimal number of circuit elements required for chaos. ## 1. Introduction

The work of Leon Chua opened new horizons in the fields of nonlinear dynamics and chaos, establishing connections between abstract dynamical systems and key real-world complex phenomena of concern, including the onset of spatial patterns, signal transmission, and information processing. As a tribute to his brilliant contributions I would like to convey in this short note some thoughts on the mathematical and physical foundations of emergence, one of the key concepts underlying the science of complex systems [1].

The essence of emergence is that complexity follows rules of its own transcending those governing the subunits constituting the system. On the other hand since the laws of nature are deterministic these rules are bound to follow in one way or the other

from the interactions between these subunits, even though in most cases the precise relations linking them might be very difficult to determine and computationally very costly to implement. When dealing with emergence one is thus confronted at the outset with an apparent paradox, at the origin of its perception by part of the scientific community as an expression of ignorance or, at best, as a metaphor. A central task of complexity research is to show that emergence is in fact a well-defined, quantifiable observable property providing new ways to address and characterize systems composed of interacting subunits.

## 2. Quantifying Emergence

There are several instances, where switching from “elementary” to “higher” levels of description is
accompanied by the appearance of novel, global scale features that one could be tempted to associate to emergence. These features are often brought out by averagings of some sort, in the spirit of statistical mechanics. A diffusion coefficient, a rate constant, or density and temperature for that matter, are novel concepts accompanying the passage from the microscopic to the macroscopic level of description. Likewise the concepts of entropy, dissipation, irreversibility, arise when the macroscopic level of description is further condensed into a thermodynamic description. In a similar vein, climate is a state of the atmosphere resulting from averaging over the short term variability of meteorological fields; the random-looking evolution of deterministic chaotic systems associated with the property of sensitivity to the initial conditions — a feature that stands at first sight in direct opposition to their defining character as deterministic systems — is best captured by switching from a pointwise description running on single trajectories to a coarse-grained description where one deals with ensembles of trajectories; and the very concept of attractor, central to the entire field of nonlinear dynamics, reflects the existence of regularities and structures not foreseen in the properties of the initial conditions and of the individual observables that become apparent once the evolution is embedded in phase space, an abstract concept that has little to do with our everyday perception of space-time events.

Can one then identify, in the light of the foregoing, emergence to the new collective features showing up in the course of switching to higher levels of description? The answer is, generally, in the negative. Indeed, if complexity theory is to be a predictive theory a much stronger property is needed, in which the new entities taking over at a given level determine the evolution of the system in a self-consistent manner, by talking to each other as it were, without the need to refer each time back and forth to what is going on at a lower level. We refer to this property as closure: given a set of n variables {x_i} representing the state of the individual subunits of a complex multivariate system, there exist new variables {z_k} whose number is much less than n, that obey to evolution laws whose structure rests solely on these variables. Alternatively, the system capitalizes on new structures arising through a process of self-organization and to which the aforementioned {z_k} are associated and sets new rules supported entirely by these structures.

An example in which this mechanism is operating pertains to the normal form description of bifurcations. Let the time evolution of {x_i} be given by the set of coupled equations

\[ \frac{dx_i}{dt} = f_i(x_1, ..., x_n, \lambda) \quad i = 1, ..., n \tag{1} \]
where λ is a control parameter. Let λ_c be a critical value of λ beyond which new branches of solutions are bifurcating from a certain “reference” state. A major result of nonlinear dynamics [2, 3] is that for the description of the bifurcating branches as well as for transient behavior, a limited set of κ ≪ n quantities can be defined which obey a closed set of equations if the parameters lie close to their critical values λ_c. Therefore the original dynamics is effectively reduced into κ equations giving information on bifurcation, the remaining n − κ equations being essentially irrelevant as far as bifurcation is concerned. We refer to the quantities that satisfy the bifurcation equations as order parameters.

A most important point is that the bifurcation equations have exactly the same form whatever the structure of the initial laws might be. In other words, the dynamics can be cast in a universal, normal form close to the bifurcation point.

Normal forms can be regarded as a new, “higher” level of description beyond that constituted by Eqs. (1). As an example, for a system operating close to a pitchfork bifurcation, the normal form describing the bifurcation of steady state solutions is

\[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = (\lambda - \lambda_c)z - u z^3 \tag{2} \]
where z is the unique order parameter and u is a combination of λ_c and the other control parameters present in the initial equations. Emergence corresponds here to the existence of non-trivial solutions for the order parameters z, inexistent prior to the transition across the critical point λ_c, which become subsequently the quantities that “perceive” the transition to complexity heralded by the occurrence of the bifurcation. The elementary interactions between the constituent subunits are still present and are even necessary. They are, however, by no means sufficient as they cannot capture properly some key elements coming into play. In short, we have a first quantitative measure of emergence within the framework of a deterministic description: the view of emergence as an expression of ignorance cannot be sustained.
