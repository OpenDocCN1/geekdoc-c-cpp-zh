## 1. Introduction

Typically, a circuit is described by a set of equations that are partly differential and partly nondifferential, i.e. static. Such systems are called *differential-algebraic equations*. Even though for the purpose of numerical analysis it is advantageous to solve algebra-differential systems directly, and most general purpose circuit analysis programs actually do so, the question whether or not it is possible to reduce them to a purely differential form, the *state equations*, is not only of theoretical interest.

In fact, differential-algebraic systems that cannot be reduced to a system of first order differential equations, not even locally, possess singular points, where the solution either does not exist (*impasse points*) or is not unique. Of course, this is only a property of the circuit model, and not of the circuit itself, but it indicates a singular behavior of the physical circuit also from a practical point of view, like e.g. jumps from a state to a distant one. Therefore, it is important to have computable criteria that allow excluding the potential presence of singular points.

Circuits are by definition connections of circuit elements. The connections are realized by ports that carry two variables, a current and a voltage. While the sum of the currents of connected ports must be zero, the voltages must be identical. The circuit elements themselves are described by equations involving their port variables, and possibly additional, internal variables such as charges and fluxes. They are called the constitutive relations of the elements. The constitutive relations may be linear or nonlinear and the corresponding elements carry the attribute linear or nonlinear, accordingly.

The electronics literature is full of different circuit elements, the most classical being the resistor, the capacitor, the inductor, the transistor, the operational amplifier, controlled sources, etc. This list of elements is redundant in the sense that a large part of them can be obtained by connection of only a few of them, the basic circuit elements. Different sets of basic circuit elements can be chosen. Here we favor the set composed of nonlinear locally passive resistors, independent current and voltage sources and linear capacitors as 1-ports, and the ideal operational amplifier as 2-port.

In addition we find it useful to decompose the ideal operational amplifier into two juxtaposed singular 1-ports, the nullator and the norator (Fig. 2). In this way, all basic elements are 1-ports. Inductors, transistors, controlled sources, etc., can all be obtained by connecting these basic elements. Even nonlinear resistors with non-monotonic characteristics, and nonlinear capacitors and inductors have equivalent circuits composed exclusively of the basic circuit elements.

The state of a circuit at a time t0 is the minimal information necessary to determine the solution of the circuit equations for t > t0. It is given by a vector of circuit variables. This vector is not unique,

![](img/039e7d3c23390584a66737067db8b75b_531_0.png)

various choices can be taken. However, there is a privileged candidate, the vector of capacitor charges and the inductor fluxes. The state equations in this case are of the form

```
dq1/dt = F1(q1, ..., qnC, φ1, ..., φnL, × E1, ..., EnVS, I1, ..., IncS) : dφnL/dt = FnC+nL(q1, ..., qnC, φ1, ..., φnL, × E1, ..., EnVS, I1, ..., IncS) (1)
```

where nC, nL, nCS and nVS are, respectively, the number of capacitors, inductors, independent voltage sources and independent current sources. The voltages Ek and currents Ik injected into the circuit by the independent sources may depend on time or be constant. Most often, the state equations in this form exist. Two main exceptions can be distinguished:

- (1) The charges and fluxes are linearly dependent. In this case, usually a linearly independent subset can be chosen such that a system of state

![](img/039e7d3c23390584a66737067db8b75b_531_1.png)

equations of the same form as (1) exists for the reduced set of independent charges and fluxes.

(2) The system of equations in the form (1) does not exist, but it exists in almost all points locally. In this case, the solutions of the equations exist and are uniquely determined by the state variables, as long as they stay away from exceptional points, where they normally break down. This is the phenomenon of impasse points. At other exceptional points, the solution would not be uniquely determined by the variables that constitute the state at the regular points.

It is important to know whether global state equations exist, especially in order to exclude impasse points that would cause problems for numerical simulations, even when the simulations are not based on state equations. The problem is as classical one, but the renewed interest in memristors poses it once more.

## 2. The Memristor

The memristor is a 1-port described by the constitutive relations [1].

```
dq/dt = i, dφ/dt = v, φ = h(q) (charge controlled) or q = g(φ) (flux controlled) (2)
```

Setting q = q1 and φ = R1 q2 yields indeed the constitutive relations of a charge-controlled memristor:

```
dq/dt = i1, dφ/dt = v1,
φ = f(q), with f(x) = C2 R1 R2 g(x/C1) (5)
```

A similar equivalent circuit can be given for the flux controlled memristor.

The drawback of these equivalent circuits is that they use two capacitors which would suggest two state variables, namely the two charges. But by the very nature of the memristor, the charge and the flux, and therefore in the equivalent circuit the two charges are dependent and therefore there can be only one state variable. For this reason, we prefer to view the memristor as a charge or flux controlled resistor and use the constitutive relations

```
dq/dt = i, v = R(q)·i or i = G(φ)·v (6)
```
