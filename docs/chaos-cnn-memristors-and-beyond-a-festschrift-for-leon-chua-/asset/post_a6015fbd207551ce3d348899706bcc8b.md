## 1. Introduction

The study of networks in nature, science, and technology is currently one of the most active areas of research. A good deal of this research however is focused on the structural or static features of these systems although most real networks are inherently dynamic [3, 7, 8, 10–12, 14]. That is, each network element has an associated state that changes with time.

To analyze this behavior we note that the dynamics of a network can be characterized by three key features; (i) the internal (local) dynamics of the network elements, (ii) the interactions between the network elements, and (iii) the structure of the network’s graph of interactions. Features (i) and (ii)

of a network are described by dynamical systems and can therefore be studied using methods from differential equations and dynamical systems theory. In fact, the internal dynamics and interactions of a network have been studied together for centuries. A popular example are reaction-diffusion equations where the reaction corresponds to the internal dynamics and the diffusion to the interactions of the system. However, what differentiates these systems from the present study of networks is their regular structure of interactions.
