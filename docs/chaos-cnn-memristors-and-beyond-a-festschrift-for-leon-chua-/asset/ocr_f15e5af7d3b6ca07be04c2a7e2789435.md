This in turn requires very complex new mathematical tools, especially for exploiting multibody coherence effects [11]. At these frontiers of physical hardware, it becomes both essential and difficult to account for quantum effects and noise in multibody physics, and to develop a better understanding of the flow of heat (phonons). The better understanding of many-body effects may also allow breakthroughs in the efficiency of solar cells [12] and thermoelectric chips.

Using traditional methods from quantum electrodynamics (QED [13, 14]), it is extremely challenging to understand these effects for physically interesting systems. For example, to model a system with a million electrons, we are asked, in principle, to solve a partial differential equation (the Schrödinger equation) over a space of three million dimensions. Quantum optics has been very effective in understanding and controlling many-body effects such as coherence (lasers) and entanglement, in part because it uses mathematical methods like $P$, $Q$ and $W$ mappings [15, 16] to convert these systems in high dimensions into traditional partial differential equations (PDE) over three dimensions [15, 16]. To the extent that phonons are bosons, we can apply an extended version of the same mappings (e.g., see [17]) to handle phonons. The emerging field of quantum phononics, borrowing methods from quantum optics, may be of enormous importance to developing advanced memristor hardware. The $P$, $Q$ and $W$ mappings need to be used together with other mathematical methods, of course, but they bring us back to the need to develop a better understanding of partial differential equations (PDE) over three dimensions, the main theme of this chapter after the introduction. These new tools also provide a pathway to solving serious fundamental problems in the rigorous axiomatic formulation of quantum field theory in general [17].

The mapping from many body effects to PDE in 3 dimensions, in order to allow useful large-scale simulations, applications and technologies, is also an important emerging part of empirical nuclear physics [18].

Can we envision even more advanced information technologies, going beyond the capabilities of the mammal brain and the densest possible classical

massively parallel computing? Can we inject some kind of quantum computing capability into these kinds of systems? This is a very controversial area, of course, but it makes sense for some of us to explore that third frontier while others work on the more immediate ones.

In my view, we cannot make full use of quantum computing effects until we develop a new, more complete understanding of what quantum mechanics really tells us, and how it really works.

In my view [19, 20], quantum mechanics is ultimately more about the direction of the flow of time than it is about systems over a million dimensions. It is very hard for some people to imagine the possibility of technology which could allow us to affect the direction in time of the flows of information and of heat, but this makes more sense to me, at least, than metaphysical theories of physics which assume that there is no physical reality out there at all [19, 20].

This third frontier leads to a number of immediate empirical opportunities which I have already discussed elsewhere [20]. But it also leads to a kind of fourth frontier, a more scientific frontier. For me, this frontier began with a “simple” question: can we use nonlinear system dynamics in three dimensions to give us a better understanding of what an electron really is, which would then let us use methods like those of quantum optics and empirical nuclear physics [15–18] to model systems of many electrons? Yet in exploring that question, I recently learned that this mathematics is inextricably connected as well to a major high-risk high-potential technology project in Russia [18, Sec. 11.5], aimed at developing methods for the complete conversion of matter to energy. Putin has recently noted that Russia’s new project in nuclear technology, if successful, would be as far beyond fission and fusion as those are beyond gunpowder. Yet Manton emphasizes that better understanding and control over coherence effects will be essential to mastering that technology. It now seems that our “simple” question about the electron is important both to the physical assumptions and to the coherence issues which will determine the outcome of that effort.
