## 1. Introduction

Electronic circuits can be considered as dynamical systems. There is a long tradition for this since major contributions in electronics using the emerging dynamical system theory can be dated back at least to the 1930s with the work by van der Pol [1] and Andronov and co-workers [2]; to only quote the most well known. Poincaré, who may be considered as one of the fathers of the dynamical system theory, investigated himself the equation governing a singing arc [3] as described by Ginoux & Letellier [4].

It is known that van der Pol and van der Mark remarked some “noise” reminiscent of a cornemuse [5] but no time series was then published. Before such a paper, William Duddell, who discovered the “musical arc” (or the singing arc as later named) published in 1901 [6] one time series which seems to be rather aperiodic [Fig. 1(a)]. In the same system, Blondel [7] observed aperiodic oscillations too [Fig. 1(b)]. Both of them were considering these oscillations as interesting but they did not have yet the theoretical tools required to investigate them carefully. Today we could investigate a little bit more deeply the nature of such oscillations, in particular to check whether they are chaotic or not.

Due to its simplicity, the van der Pol equation became widely studied. Using analytical approach, Cartwright and Littlewood [8], and Levinson [9] remarked that for certain parameter values, some aperiodic solutions can be found. But this is actually with the first computers that the chaotic solutions were actually identified. The emergence of chaos in electronic circuits during the late 70s and early 80s was reviewed by Letellier & Ginoux [10]. Unfortunately, in this review we forgot the important contribution by Igor Gumowski and Christian

Mira (the “Toulouse Research Group”) whose a short story was given in Ref. [11]. Stimulated by a paper by C. P. Pulkin [12] who showed that in one-dimensional noninvertible map infinitely many unstable cycles may lead to bounded complex iterated sequences, Gumowski and Mira studied the piecewise-linear map [13]

```
\begin{cases}
x_{n+1} = (1 - \lambda)x_n + y_n \\
y_{n+1} = y_n + f(x_n)
\end{cases}

where f(x_n) = \begin{cases}
-2\lambda x_n - 0.9\lambda & x_n < -0.5 \\
-\dfrac{\lambda x_n}{5} & \text{if } |x_n| < 0.5 \\
-2\lambda x_n + 0.9\lambda & x_n > -0.5.
\end{cases}
(1)
```

For some λ-values, they obtained attractive limit set made of bounded cloud of points as shown in Fig. 2(a). This could have been the first now called “chaotic” solution to a piecewise-linear map reported with an explicit picture. By these times, Gumowski and Mira indicated these types of behaviors as “Pulkin phenomenon” [13]. This is only ten years later that first chaotic solutions were widely investigated in electronic circuits [10].

Our aim in this chapter is to provide a partial review of some Chua’s contributions to chaos in electronic circuits. It should be clear that our choices were biased by our interest in the topological analysis of chaotic behaviors. We thus focused our attention on some different types of chaos Chua proposed with many co-workers.

Born June 28, 1936, Leon Chua was mainly interested in nonlinear electronic circuits and the dynamical system theory. His early papers discussed the synthesis of new elements for electronic circuits [14] and, in particular, in defining piecewise-linear or nonlinear resistors (see Figs. 6 and 8 in his 1968 paper [14]). Such aim leads Chua to discover the memristor in 1971 [15] which was recently designed by Hewlett Packard laboratories [16]. While he was a student at MIT (1962), Chua was studying the “qualitative theory” from books by Nemystskii & Stepanov [17] as well as by Lefschetz [18]. The three-volume Poincaré’s book on celestial mechanics [19] was also studied. Thus Chua was developing a general theory for nonlinear electronic circuits with the desire to make a connection between the architecture of the circuit and its resulting dynamics. This is therefore a major problem, in a certain sense related to the still open question of the relationship between the algebraic structure of differential equations and the topology of the dynamics they produce.

### 2.1. *The inverse problem of the dynamical system theory*

Commonly, one investigates a given dynamical system by studying its fixed points, the nature of its asymptotic behaviors, etc. This is the *direct problem*. But sometimes, it is desirable to be able to

write down some governing equations producing some prescribed features like the number and the nature of the fixed points, some given time series, etc. This is what is called the *inverse problem*. As far as we know, one of the first problem related to the nonlinear dynamical system theory attacked by Leon Chua was the inverse problem. In 1971, he published a paper where he proposed a way to write some governing equations which possess a finite number of prescribed singular points, as well as a prescribed set of eigenvalues associated with each singular points [20]. The background in nonlinear dynamical systems theory quoted in this 1971 paper is the book by Andronov, Vitt and Khaikin [2] and the book by Nemystskii and Stepanov [17].

Two years later, Chua provided a second contribution on this inverse problem with Douglas Green [21]. Their aim was then to find a class of differential equations having for solution a limit cycle with a prescribed frequency $\omega$ and a prescribed waveform. They treated the cases of autonomous and non-autonomous systems using Fourier decomposition and Chebichev polynomials associated with a least-square technique. One of their conclusion was that such a problem can be positively addressed but there is, in fact, an infinite number of different types of systems, all having the same limit cycles for solution.

### 2.2. Periodic and almost-periodic solutions

Early analysis of solutions to nonlinear dynamical systems developed by Chua and co-workers were based on frequency domain analysis using, for instance, Volterra functional series [25]. Then the approach turned to use the Hopf theorem [26] for studying almost sinusoidal oscillations [27]. Electronic circuits were considered as dynamical systems and in 1990, Chua published a survey of the qualitative behavior of dynamic nonlinear (RLC) electronic circuits [28]. The motivation was to better understand the sometimes “bizarre” solutions observed for particular initial conditions. Among others, some solutions were investigated using “one-dimensional invariant sub-manifold”, that is, using nullclines. Some relevant contributions to nonlinear dynamics with many applications in electronic circuits were quoted such as, for instance, Minorski’s book [29] and Hayashi’s book [30]. There is also one paper published by Gollub and co-workers about chaos in a system of coupled tunnel diode relaxation oscillators [31] where they remarked that “the nature of the coupling is much more important than the number of degrees of freedom in generating chaos”. Two papers by Yoshisuke Ueda dealing with Chaos in the Duffing [32] and van der Pol [33] equations were also mentioned. These last two references are quite important in the sense that this

Ueda and Akamatsu used the van der Pol equation

```
⎧ ẋ = y
⎨ ẏ = μ(1 − x²) y − x³ + B cos ωt.  (2)
```

Note that in Ueda’s papers, chaos is still presented as a “transitional phenomenon” by Ueda, apparently corresponding to the “bizarre” features mentioned by Chua. The qualification of “transitional” by Ueda may leave the impression that chaos was only an “artefact”. Although that Ueda was not followed in his presentation of chaotic behaviors as “transitional phenomena”, many others complained too that no proof for the chaotic nature of the solution to the Lorenz and the Rössler systems was provided [34–37]. Chua questioned himself too whether Lorenz’s results [38] were actually asymptotic behaviors and not artefact. He was therefore pushed to develop some algorithms to be ensured that the numerical solutions he obtained were not artefact as we will see below.

Chua and Ushida therefore started by checking that the asymptotic behavior was actually reached when they were observing some quasi-periodic solutions [39].
