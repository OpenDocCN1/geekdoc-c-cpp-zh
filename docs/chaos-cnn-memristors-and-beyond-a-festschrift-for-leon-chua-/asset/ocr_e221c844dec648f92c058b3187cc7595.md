distributions of $\xi$ in $V_\varepsilon(x)$. Thus we arrive at the metric

```
$$\rho^\#(\xi_1,\xi_2)=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\min\left\{\varepsilon,\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^r}\sup_{x\in D^r}\sup_{y\in E^r}\left|F_{\xi_1}^{r,\varepsilon}(x,y)-F_{\xi_2}^{r,\varepsilon}(x,y)\right|\right\}.$$
```

where $D^r$ and $E^r$ are the direct products of $r$ copies of $D$ and $E$, and $F_{\xi}^{r,\varepsilon}(x,y)$ is the averaging of the $r$-dimensional distribution function $F_\xi^r(x,y)$ of $\xi$ over $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of $x\in D^r$. The metric $\rho^\#$, that might appear to be too complicated, actually has a simple probability meaning and is closely linked to the notion of weak convergence.

Let $\mathcal C^\Delta$ and $\mathcal C^\#$ be the completions of the phase space $\mathcal C$ respectively with upper semicontinuous functions via the metric $\rho^\Delta$ and with random and deterministic functions via the metric $\rho^\#$.

In dynamical systems theory, to describe the asymptotic behavior of trajectories, the notion of $\omega$-limit set (i.e. the set of the limit points of a trajectory) is usually used. Reasoning from this, for an initial state $\phi\in\mathcal C$, we will denote by $\omega_\Delta[\phi]$ and $\omega_\#[\phi]$ the sets of the limit points of the trajectory $S^t[\phi]$ respectively in the spaces $\mathcal C^\Delta$ and $\mathcal C^\#$. Finally, we will write $d(\cdot)$ for the Hausdorff dimension of the graph of a function.

**Definition.** The initial state $\phi\in\mathcal C$ is said to generate *ideal turbulence* (IT) if the set $\omega_\Delta[\phi]$ contains at least one “point” which is a fractal function, i.e. if there exists $\zeta_*\in\omega_\Delta[\phi]$ such that $d(\zeta_*)>\dim D$. The initial state $\phi\in\mathcal C$ is said to generate *stochastic turbulence* (SIT) if the set $\omega_\#[\phi]$ contains at least one “point” which is a random function (stochastic process). If there is a great many (in one sense or another, as the case requires) of initial states which generate IT or SIT, then IT or respectively SIT is said to occur in the dynamical system.

The majority of evolutionary boundary value problems (BVPs) for partial differential equations induce dynamical systems of shifts along solutions. It is reasonable to say that *there occurs IT or SIT in a BVP* if such a turbulence occurs in the dynamical system associated with the BVP.

The modern-day mathematical apparatus of dynamical systems theory (particularly, one-dimensional) allows one to investigate in detail the properties of ideal turbulence in the BVPs which are reducible to continuous argument difference equations or differential-difference equations.

(see, for example, [16, 19, 23]). It has long been known that many BVPs for transmission lines fall into this class (see, for example, [1,4]), but an effective study of such BVPs by the “reduction method” became possible only in the last 20–30 years owing to the progress in continuous argument difference equations theory.

In this regard a transmission line with Chua’s diode are among the representative examples.

# 3. Transmission Line with Chua’s Diode

We consider the BVP for a lossless transmission line with Chua’s diode

```
$$v_x=-Li_t,\qquad i_x=-Cv_t,\qquad 0\le x\le l,\quad t\in\mathbb R^+, \tag{1}$$
$$v(0,t)=0,\qquad i(l,t)=G(v(l,t)-E-Ri(l,t)). \tag{2}$$
```

Here $v(x,t)$ and $i(x,t)$ are the voltage and current along the line, $L$, $C$ and $R$ are the inductance, capacitance and resistance per unit length, $E$ is the voltage across a voltage source (in series with the diode), and $G$ is the piecewise linear function

```
$$G(u)=\begin{cases}m_0u,& |u|\le 1,\quad m_0<0,\\m_1u-(m_1-m_0)\operatorname{sgn}u,& |u|\ge 1,\quad m_1>0,\end{cases} \tag{3}$$
```

that specifies the $v$–$i$ characteristic of the diode. We assume that the solutions of the BVP are continuous and piecewise smooth, which forces the initial conditions

```
$$v(x,0)=\varphi(x),\qquad i(x,0)=\psi(x),$$
$$\varphi,\psi:[0,l]\to\mathbb R, \tag{4}$$
```

to be likewise continuous and piecewise smooth, and be in agreement with the boundary conditions (2).
