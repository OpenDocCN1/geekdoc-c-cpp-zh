At any given time, there were researchers in NOEL proving theorems, writing code, running software simulations, designing integrated circuits, building electronic systems, running physical experiments and writing papers. The lab was always full of people and was a fertile ground for sprouting technical discussions and research projects among physicists, mathematicians, circuit designers, post-docs, students and professors.

I started my Ph.D. research working on CNN, particular on the stability of various types of CNNs [9–11]. I also created a maze-solving CNN that uses the dynamics of the network to find a solution to a maze. When Bert Shi was the Teaching Assistant (TA) for Leon’s circuit theory class, he used this problem as an exercise problem for the class. As I will describe later on, this has profound consequences (for me). Sometime in the middle of my stay at NOEL, I changed my research focus to concentrate more on chaotic dynamics[12] and synchronization of chaos[13]. Even though this appeared to be a drastic change in research focus, the mathematical tools used (linear algebra, dynamical systems) are similar and the underlying theme of my research both in CNN and in chaotic dynamics was complex yet orderly behavior in a collection of interconnected systems. The mathematics
department at UC Berkeley, located in Evans Hall not far from Cory Hall, is well known for its research faculty and their contributions to nonlinear and chaotic dynamics, with world renowned mathematicians such as Stephen Smale, Morris Hirsch and Charles Pugh on its staff. Being able to talk to them face to face was a boon to our research. In particular, Moe Hirsch’s work on cooperative systems[14–16] shows that state trajectories of cooperative systems are eventually monotone, i.e. they preserve the natural partial ordering of the states over time and we used these results extensively in our work on CNN stability. I also had many interesting discussions with Charles Pugh who was my advisor for my Master’s thesis in mathematics. The following coincidence is worth mentioning. On my first day of work at IBM I went to the security office to get my badge. I was quite surprised to see Charles Pugh standing in line at the same office, also waiting for his badge. It turned out he was visiting Mike Shub, who was my manager at IBM at the time, and Charles and I both arrived at IBM on the same day and went to the security office at around the same time!

there exists countably infinite many periodic orbits near a homoclinic orbit of a 3-D dynamical system leading to chaos. Such Shilnikov bifurcations were found in Chua’s circuit[18]. Sharkovsky[19]ⁱ showed that the least periods of periodic orbits on a continuous map on an interval follow a specific ordering of the integers, now known as the Sharkovsky ordering. A consequence of Sharkovsky’s theorem is that any continuous map on the unit interval with a period 3 orbit contains orbits of any period. While he was at NOEL he studied chaos in a Chua’s circuit with a transmission line that is described by a functional differential equation[20]. I do not speak Russian and Alexander did not speak much English, so my conversation with Alexander sometimes resembled the conversation he had with Jim Yorke as described in Ref. [21]. Before he left, he gave me some nice picturesque postcards from the Ukraine (Fig. 5). Incidentally, my Master’s thesis in mathematics focuses on extending another theorem by Sharkovskyʲ to star-shaped trees[22].

We developed the first chaotic communication system that is inherently error-free[23] and built an implementation using Chua’s circuit as the chaos generator. In this setup, the chaotic signal is modulated by the information signal by embedding the information source into the dynamics of the chaotic circuit. The modulated chaotic signal is then sent to the receiver. At the receiver, an identical chaotic
circuit demodulates the signal to recover the information signal. At the 1993 NOLTA conference in Hawaii, I was supposed to demo this at the end of Leon’s talk. We built two identical Chua’s circuits, one embedded in a receiver circuit and one embedded in a transmitter circuit. A low-amplitude music signal comprising of a selection from Mozart’s Eine Kleine Nachtmusik was fed into the transmitter and is completely masked by the chaotic signal. This combined signal is then sent via a coax cable of several feet to the receiver circuit to be decoded. I have checked all the setup at NOEL and also right before the talk. Of course Murphy’s Law kicked in and when I turned on the system in an auditorium full of people, the sound that came out of the receiver’s speakers is … absolute silence. Since Chua’s circuit contains an active nonlinear resistor,ᵏ it consumes power and this was delivered via a battery. It took several minutes of profuse sweating to realize that somehow I must have unplugged the battery to conserve power and neglected to plug it back in for the demo. Luckily the crowd was patient enough and eventually I was able to demo the system successfully. Ever since then, whenever something goes wrong, I would always check the power switch or power supply, even when I am writing software code. A video demoing the implementations we made around that time is available at http://youtu.be/1o2z5KVuB7s.

## 5. Fun and Games

It was not always just work at NOEL. As children of the video game generation, many of the students unwound by playing computer games. The World Wide Web was just in its infancy, and somehow I became the person responsible for downloading games demos from an FTP server onto our computers for our enjoyment. Maciej Ogorzałek, who is a frequent visitor, would copy these games demos from the PC for his young sons. Since Maciej spent some time “testing” these games out in the lab, we were always wondering whether he was downloading the games for himself to play.

This was also long before Massive Multiplayer Online Games (MMOG) such as World of Warcraft existed. One popular game we played was Xtrek, a (primitive by today’s standard) MMOG that ran on Unix workstations. On one occasion we sneaked into a conference room to play Xtrek by projecting it onto the big screen, making us feel like Captain Kirk on the bridge of the starship Enterprise. One time we were playing Xtrek late at night on the computers in NOEL when Leon walked in to tell us about some ideas he had. Luckily he was so excited to tell us what he was thinking about that he did not notice all the moving spaceships on our computer screens. How I wish I had a boss key¹ at the time although a spreadsheet would not have made much sense either for the research that we were doing.

We did many other activities together, including Hong Kong movie nightsᵐ and The Simpsons marathon. Bert Shi and I were both part of the UC Berkeley Wushu team. I participated in the annual UC Berkeley Wushu competition one year and placed third in the beginner category. I should mention that there were a total of 3 entrants in that category. One year we had a lab picnic at the Berkeley marina and Philippe Deregel volunteered to prepare crêpes for all of us. Since Philippe is French, he pronounced crêpe like a Frenchman would, which sounded to my unsophisticated ears very much like a word in English that is much less palatable.

When Arnold Schwarzenegger came to the UC Berkeley campus to film “Junior”, Ann Chen and
I went to see the movie being made and found out just how tedious movie making is. We sat there for several hours only to see a few takes being shot over and over again. Reading countless papers and writing pages of code don’t seem so boring after that.

One highlight over the years was being invited to Leon’s house for fine cheese, dinner and drinks. In addition to walls of bookcases filled with books on a diverse set of topics, Leon’s house also contained many interesting artifacts from around the world that he has collected over the years. Fig. 6 shows me attempting to blow a horn (albeit unsuccessfully). Leon is a fine wine connoisseurᵉ and his wine cellar is always a delight to visit. He would show us his extensive French wine collection including bottles of wine of size Nebuchadnezzar (i.e. 15 liters!) and his homemade earthquake-resistant wine holders.
