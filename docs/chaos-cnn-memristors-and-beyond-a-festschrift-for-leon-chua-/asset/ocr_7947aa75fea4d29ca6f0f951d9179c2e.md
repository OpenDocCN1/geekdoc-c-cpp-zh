More sophisticated models based on coupled ionic/electronic drift-diffusion [12] and tunneling mechanisms (in particular Simmons tunneling) [16] have been developed. In [7] it has been shown that the dynamical behavior of this element can be determined by calculating a Bernoulli-type differential equation

i(t) - (\dot{v}(t)/v(t)) i(t) + (k/v(t))[i(t)]^3 = 0.  (5)

Another approach in [2] provides the analytical treatment of a wider class of memristors, including the HP memristor with nonlinear drift and points out that in general, the dynamics of those systems can be described by a (generalized) Abel-type differential equation.

However, those descriptions are valid only for single memristors. Going further, even simple memristor circuits reveal very interesting properties. In Fig. 3, a circuit consisting of a capacitor, memristor and inductor is depicted. Let’s imagine that the capacitor will be initially charged to +20 mV in one case and to −20 mV in another case. In Fig. 4 the quantities show that on the one hand, the initial voltage of +20 mV leads to a smooth relaxation of all quantities. On the contrary to this, the initial voltage of −20 mV leads to a short current spike and a following relaxation.

This simple example shows that memristors can change a circuit behavior dramatically which may be considered to develop a new type of circuit realizations. To describe simple networks with memristors, in the following we will apply the multilinear theory of Schetzen [10].

# 3. The Multilinear Approach

# 3.1. Basics

According to [10], the multilinear theory is based on the assumption that every (node) voltage and every (branch) current can be expressed as a series of the form

V(t) = Σ_{n=0}^{∞} V_n(t).

![](bbox=[0.11, 0.88, 0.45, 0.96])

The n-th order voltages (or currents) V_n(t) must be defined such that a multiplication of every source in the circuit with the same constant α will result in the new voltage (or current)

V(t) = Σ_{n=1}^{∞} α^n V_n(t).  (6)

Both of the Kirchhoff laws for current and voltage still hold for each order n [10]. And for each of the basic circuit elements, there are small linear sub-circuits (the “n-th order models”) which implement the relationship between V_n(t) and I_n(t). The n-th order model of a circuit element does not depend on any quantity of order n or higher, but it may contain a time-dependent voltage source depending on voltages or currents of any order lower than n [10].

Given a nonlinear circuit, the analysis works as follows: One starts by replacing each circuit element with its first order model. The resulting linear circuit is solved, and the time-dependent sources of the second-order models are calculated. Then, the circuit elements are replaced by their second-order models, and the circuit (which is still linear) is solved again. This process may be repeated several times. In the end, the voltages and currents of the different orders are added simply, setting α = 1, to get an approximation of the voltages and currents in the real circuit.

Using the multilinear theory of nonlinear networks, one will get the same results, which could be obtained by an analysis based on Volterra series [10]. One advantage of the multilinear theory is the fact that the network structure of the circuit is not lost in the process. It is hoped that this might help with the study (or even the design) of nonlinear circuits.
