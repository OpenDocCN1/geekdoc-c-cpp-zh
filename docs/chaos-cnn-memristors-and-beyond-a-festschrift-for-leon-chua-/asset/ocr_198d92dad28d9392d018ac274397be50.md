It is often surprising how even simple beings possess a high level of adaptability while moving into various environments. Studies on how the control of locomotor activity is realized in a large category of animals reveal a hierarchical organization of the neural networks devoted to the locomotion control task. The key feature of these networks is that the pattern of neural activity is mapped into the pattern of locomotor activity. The generation of the rhythmic patterns of muscles activity takes place at the neural level called Central Pattern Generator (CPG). This structure has been revealed both in vertebrates (located in the spinal cord) and in invertebrates (located in relevant ganglia).

The CPG includes subnetworks of Command Neurons (CNs) and Local Pattern Generating Neurons (LPGNs). The former ones act as decisional centers: they receive stimuli from the sensorial organs or from higher level neurons. Therefore, at this level some important decisions such as escape reactions or the choice of the right locomotion pattern (i.e. swimming if the being is on the water or walking if it is on the terrain) take place. The LPGNs are responsible of coordination of the organs devoted to locomotion (cilia, legs). The neurons of this subsystem are called also motor-neurons, because they constitute the last neural level before movement actuators. In many biological cases this functional subdivision does not correspond to different neural networks: the same networks may act at different levels of the structure. It is also not so clearly established which are the mechanisms used to reorganize the LPGNs configuration in order to switch among locomotion patterns. Two mechanisms involved in this operation are focused by neurobiologists. In some cases it is necessary only to change the parameters of the synapses, while in other cases a strong reorganization of the network is required. In this latter case it is not rare that some synaptic connections are inhibited and other ones are excited towards neurons not yet involved in the former pattern generation.
