# 1. Introduction

Electronic circuits can be considered as dynamical systems. There is a long tradition for this since major contributions in electronics using the emerging dynamical system theory can be dated back at least to the 1930s with the work by van der Pol [1] and Andronov and co-workers [2]; to only quote the most well known. Poincaré, who may be considered as one of the fathers of the dynamical system theory, investigated himself the equation governing a singing arc [3] as described by Ginoux & Letellier [4].

It is known that van der Pol and van der Mark remarked some “noise” reminiscent of a cornemuse [5] but no time series was then published. Before such a paper, William Duddell, who discovered the “musical arc” (or the singing arc as later named) published in 1901 [6] one time series which seems to be rather aperiodic [Fig. 1(a)]. In the same system, Blondel [7] observed aperiodic oscillations too [Fig. 1(b)]. Both of them were considering these oscillations as interesting but they did not have yet the theoretical tools required to investigate them carefully. Today we could investigate a little bit more deeply the nature of such oscillations, in particular to check whether they are chaotic or not.

Due to its simplicity, the van der Pol equation became widely studied. Using analytical approach, Cartwright and Littlewood [8], and Levinson [9] remarked that for certain parameter values, some aperiodic solutions can be found. But this is actually with the first computers that the chaotic solutions were actually identified. The emergence of chaos in electronic circuits during the late 70s and early 80s was reviewed by Letellier & Ginoux [10]. Unfortunately, in this review we forgot the important contribution by Igor Gumowski and Christian

Mira (the “Toulouse Research Group”) whose a short story was given in Ref. [11]. Stimulated by a paper by C. P. Pulkin [12] who showed that in one-dimensional noninvertible map infinitely many unstable cycles may lead to bounded complex iterated sequences, Gumowski and Mira studied the piecewise-linear map [13]

```
\begin{cases}
x_{n+1} = (1 - \lambda)x_n + y_n \\
y_{n+1} = y_n + f(x_n)
\end{cases}

where f(x_n) = \begin{cases}
-2\lambda x_n - 0.9\lambda & x_n < -0.5 \\
-\dfrac{\lambda x_n}{5} & \text{if } |x_n| < 0.5 \\
-2\lambda x_n + 0.9\lambda & x_n > -0.5.
\end{cases}
(1)
```

For some λ-values, they obtained attractive limit set made of bounded cloud of points as shown in Fig. 2(a). This could have been the first now called “chaotic” solution to a piecewise-linear map reported with an explicit picture. By these times, Gumowski and Mira indicated these types of behaviors as “Pulkin phenomenon” [13]. This is only ten years later that first chaotic solutions were widely investigated in electronic circuits [10].

Our aim in this chapter is to provide a partial review of some Chua’s contributions to chaos in electronic circuits. It should be clear that our choices were biased by our interest in the topological analysis of chaotic behaviors. We thus focused our attention on some different types of chaos Chua proposed with many co-workers.
