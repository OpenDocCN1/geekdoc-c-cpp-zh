concept of stability which would not be enough to plausibly model an electron. For example, many physicists rely on the concept of “Q stability,” in which the states in S are minima of energy only with respect to perturbations which leave the charge Q unchanged [27]. It is known that there are solutions of the simple nonlinear Klein-Gordon equation which meet this criterion, but provide a kind of “ridge” of solutions of ever lower localized energy; in practice, one of these “solitons,” when bouncing around a complex universe, would normally leak away its energy and dissipate to nothing. Similar situations can arise when the energy H meets the first order conditions for a minimum of energy, but not the requirement that the Hessian of energy be strictly positive except for perturbations within the set S.

# 4.2. From Skyrme to Bogomolnyi, Prasad and Sommerfield (BPS)

Two types of “soliton” model have attracted great interest in empirical nuclear physics, and in research which aims at a grand unification of EWT and strong nuclear forces: the Skyrme model, and the Bogomolnyi-Prasad-Sommerfield (BPS) monopole. The seminal paper of Prasad and Sommerfield describing that monopole [29] has a citation index of more than 2,000 in Google Scholar. Both types of “soliton” are discussed in great detail in [18, 26, 27].

Both models share a rather surprising property with Eq. (1) through 7 (and with traditional EWT): they require solutions which do not go to zero as one goes to a radius r infinitely far away from the center of the soliton. If you look closely at Eqs. (1) and (6), for example, you will see that a solution cannot possess finite energy unless the field φ goes to a unitary matrix as r → ∞. The Skyrme model involves a similar matrix field U which is required to be unitary at all points in space. The system studied by BPS was previously discussed by ‘tHooft, who described it as:

```
L = 1/4 G^a_{μν} G^{a}_{μν} - 1/2 (D_μ Q^a)^2 - 1/8 λ (Q^2 - F^2)^2, (10)
```

where the underlying (real) fields are Q^a and A^a_μ for a = 1,2,3 and μ = 0,1,2,3, where λ and F are parameters, and where he used the definitions:

```
G^a_{μν} = ∂_μ A^a_ν - ∂_ν A^a_μ + e ε_{abc} A^b_μ A^c_ν
D_μ Q^a = ∂_μ Q^a + e ε_{abc} A^b_μ Q^c (11)
```

From the last term in Eq. (10), it is clear that the three component real vector Q must go to a vector of length F as r → ∞, not to zero. Note that Q is what some would call an “isovector” or a “scalar” or “just a triplet of scalars”; its three components do not change, in the field equations, when we perform a Lorentz transformation.

It is conceivable that someone will someday discover a relativistic field theory without second derivatives in the Lagrangian, in which chaotons exist which obey the traditional boundary condition that all fields go to zero as r → ∞. However, I do not see any sign in the literature that anyone knows how to do this yet, or that models of that kind would find it easy to explain the remarkable quantization of charge which we observe across all of physics. Thus for now, the approach based on nonzero boundary conditions seems more promising.

The Skyrme model has generally been used only to model protons and neutrons and complex nuclei as solitons. There have been a very few efforts to use it as a model of the electron, without success. Even as a model of the proton and neutron, it does not provide a way to explain why deep inelastic scattering shows that the proton and neutron do seem to be bound states of more fundamental components, such as quarks. This is one of the reasons why I propose that we start from the BPS monopole, rather than the skyr mion, as the main starting point in trying to model the electron.

An additional reason, of course, is that we can stay close to EWT, which is our best description today of the behavior of electromagnetic fields and related phenomena. Nevertheless, if the “solitons” of the two systems in Sec. 2 do not have the desired strong stability, it is possible that the addition of a “Skyrme” term in φ or another simpler “fixer” term [33] could solve the problem. It is not necessary to impose the requirement that φ be strictly unitary, in order to do this. In fact, it may not even be necessary to require that φ be unitary asymptotically; one can imagine an alternative Higgs term more in the spirit of:

```
V(φ) = λ₁(1 - |Tr(φ)|²)² + λ₂|det φ|² (12)
```
