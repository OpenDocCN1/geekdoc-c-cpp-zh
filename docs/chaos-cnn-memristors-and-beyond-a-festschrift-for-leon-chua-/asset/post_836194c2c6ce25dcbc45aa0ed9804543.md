In general, it seems that there are three ways that we could probe the electron, to determine its radius. First, we can do highly precise measurements [28] of exactly where the center of electric charge is located. Those are the experiments for which there is an upper bound of 10⁻²² meters. However, Prasad and Sommerfield [29] have shown that “soliton” solutions do exist for a field theory similar in spirit to Sec. 2, in which the origin of magnetic charge is all concentrated in exactly one point (radius zero), even as overall energy and other fields are smoothly distributed over a larger radius. The Lagrangians of Sec. 2 are a kind of extension of that earlier work, in which I hope that electric charge is quantized and localized in the same way that magnetic charge is in the earlier work.

It is also possible to probe the electron through high-energy collisions with objects like protons and neutrons. However, those collisions are strongly influenced by the complex physics of the neutron and the proton, which makes them less relevant here.

In order to probe whether there is “something else” of great importance as big as three femtometers, the obvious probe to use is high energy electron-electron (Moller) scattering. In fact, recent work on high-energy spin-polarized Moller scattering has shown major deviations from QED, which people are working to explain by invoking electroweak theory (EWT) [21]. According to the standard model of physics, QED itself should really be seen as one part of the larger theory, EWT. The approach proposed here is really just a variation of that approach. In fact, the equations of Sec. 2 are very close to the standard equations of electroweak theory. The only difference between these systems and the usual bosonic part of EWT is that I assume a Higgs field φ which is a twistor instead of a two-spinor. Yet in discussing how he arrived at EWT, Weinberg has often noted that he had several possible choices for a Higgs field which would have done equally well in performing the only empirical task assigned to it — trying to explain boson masses. The small change here would not undo the empirical success of the theory — but if generates electrons as solitons, it may eliminate the need for anything but bosons. Because of the P, Q and W mappings which work for bosons [15–17], it results in a physical theory which does not require renormalization in order to be mathematically well-defined. Masses emerge directly from integrating the Hamiltonian — including the mass of the electron itself, which is not explained at all by QED or renormalized traditional EWT.

## 4. Details of the Challenge and Prior Work

### 4.1. Types of stability: from solitons to chaoitons

For practical purposes, the goal in Sec. 2 is to find a set S of solutions to the system in Eq. (1) through 7 (or a variation of that system) which have the following two properties. First, each member of the set S consists of a set of field values W(x), B(x) and φ(x) across all points x in three dimensional space for some time t, such that these field values represent a static equilibrium state of the Lagrange-Euler equations. Second, each member of the set has the same total energy H. Any small perturbation of any member of the set should lead to a new state which has a higher energy, or is another member of the set, or is “equivalent to one of the members of the set.” This last option can be important when a small change leads to a small apparent oscillation which can be “gauged away” or the like.

Many other concepts of stability have appeared in the literature on “solitons” in three dimensions.

Many of these concepts are either too restrictive or too loose to represent what we need to satisfy, for an adequate model of the electron. The formal concept of soliton in pure mathematics [30] would require that an electron would keep moving in the same direction, with the same momentum, after a collision. The concept of solitary wave [31] is too loose, and has generally been applied to the study of nonrelativistic systems in any case.

In the 1990’s [32], I proposed that we study a class of objects which I called “chaotic solitons” or “chaoitons,” which would require strong stability but also allow for the possibility of stable oscillations or even stable localized chaotic patterns. But in the case where energy density is positive definite, I showed that strong energy minimization (as discussed above) is sufficient to prove that a general set of dynamic solutions S constitute a chaoiton. The work of recent years suggests that we do not need to consider chaos or oscillation to model an electron; thus it is enough to seek a static chaoiton here.

Unfortunately, a large part of the prior work on “solitons” in physics assumes a similar but weaker concept of stability which would not be enough to plausibly model an electron. For example, many physicists rely on the concept of “Q stability,” in which the states in S are minima of energy only with respect to perturbations which leave the charge Q unchanged [27]. It is known that there are solutions of the simple nonlinear Klein-Gordon equation which meet this criterion, but provide a kind of “ridge” of solutions of ever lower localized energy; in practice, one of these “solitons,” when bouncing around a complex universe, would normally leak away its energy and dissipate to nothing. Similar situations can arise when the energy H meets the first order conditions for a minimum of energy, but not the requirement that the Hessian of energy be strictly positive except for perturbations within the set S.

### 4.2. From Skyrme to Bogomolnyi, Prasad and Sommerfield (BPS)

Two types of “soliton” model have attracted great interest in empirical nuclear physics, and in research which aims at a grand unification of EWT and strong nuclear forces: the Skyrme model, and the Bogomolnyi-Prasad-Sommerfield (BPS) monopole. The seminal paper of Prasad and Sommerfield describing that monopole [29] has a citation index of more than 2,000 in Google Scholar. Both types of “soliton” are discussed in great detail in [18, 26, 27].

Both models share a rather surprising property with Eq. (1) through 7 (and with traditional EWT): they require solutions which do not go to zero as one goes to a radius r infinitely far away from the center of the soliton. If you look closely at Eqs. (1) and (6), for example, you will see that a solution cannot possess finite energy unless the field φ goes to a unitary matrix as r → ∞. The Skyrme model involves a similar matrix field U which is required to be unitary at all points in space. The system studied by BPS was previously discussed by ‘tHooft, who described it as:

```
L = 1/4 G^a_{μν} G^{a}_{μν} - 1/2 (D_μ Q^a)^2 - 1/8 λ (Q^2 - F^2)^2, (10)
```

where the underlying (real) fields are Q^a and A^a_μ for a = 1,2,3 and μ = 0,1,2,3, where λ and F are parameters, and where he used the definitions:

```
G^a_{μν} = ∂_μ A^a_ν - ∂_ν A^a_μ + e ε_{abc} A^b_μ A^c_ν
D_μ Q^a = ∂_μ Q^a + e ε_{abc} A^b_μ Q^c (11)
```

From the last term in Eq. (10), it is clear that the three component real vector Q must go to a vector of length F as r → ∞, not to zero. Note that Q is what some would call an “isovector” or a “scalar” or “just a triplet of scalars”; its three components do not change, in the field equations, when we perform a Lorentz transformation.

It is conceivable that someone will someday discover a relativistic field theory without second derivatives in the Lagrangian, in which chaotons exist which obey the traditional boundary condition that all fields go to zero as r → ∞. However, I do not see any sign in the literature that anyone knows how to do this yet, or that models of that kind would find it easy to explain the remarkable quantization of charge which we observe across all of physics. Thus for now, the approach based on nonzero boundary conditions seems more promising.

The Skyrme model has generally been used only to model protons and neutrons and complex nuclei as solitons. There have been a very few efforts to use it as a model of the electron, without success. Even as a model of the proton and neutron, it does not provide a way to explain why deep inelastic scattering shows that the proton and neutron do seem to be bound states of more fundamental components, such as quarks. This is one of the reasons why I propose that we start from the BPS monopole, rather than the Skyrmion, as the main starting point in trying to model the electron.

An additional reason, of course, is that we can stay close to EWT, which is our best description today of the behavior of electromagnetic fields and related phenomena. Nevertheless, if the “solitons” of the two systems in Sec. 2 do not have the desired strong stability, it is possible that the addition of a “Skyrme” term in φ or another simpler “fixer” term [33] could solve the problem. It is not necessary to impose the requirement that φ be strictly unitary, in order to do this. In fact, it may not even be necessary to require that φ be unitary asymptotically; one can imagine an alternative Higgs term more in the spirit of:

```
V(φ) = λ₁(1 - |Tr(φ)|²)² + λ₂|det φ|² (12)
```
