### 2. History

While on a visit to Japan in October 1983, having witnessed a futile attempt at producing chaos in an electrical analog of Lorenz’s equations, Prof. Leon Chua was prompted to develop a chaotic electronic circuit [4]. He realized that chaos could be produced in a piecewise-linear circuit if it possessed at least two unstable equilibrium points — one to provide stretching, and the other to fold trajectories. With this insight, he systematically identified those third-order piecewise-linear circuits containing a single voltage-controlled nonlinear resistor that could produce chaos. Specifying that the driving point characteristic of the voltage-controlled nonlinear resistor N_R should be chosen to yield at least two unstable equilibrium points, he invented the circuit shown in Fig. 1 [4].

Let the nonlinear resistor N_R in Chua’s circuit have a symmetric piecewise-linear DP characteristic as shown in Fig. 2. Imagine that the values of the parameters are chosen such that the circuit possesses three equilibrium points (one at the origin where N_R has a locally negative slope or conductance G_a, and two in the outer regions where N_R has a locally negative conductance G_b) and that all three equilibrium points are unstable. There are three eigenvalues associated with each equilibrium point [15]. We assume that the equilibrium point at the origin has an unstable real eigenvalue and a stable pair of complex conjugate eigenvalues. In addition, suppose that the outer equilibrium point P₋ has a stable real eigenvalue and an unstable complex conjugate pair. Two trajectories starting close together on the eigenplane in the outer region would be stretched apart as they spiralled away from the equilibrium point. If they could be folded back by the dynamics in the inner region and reinjected toward P₋ along the stable real eigenvector, a homoclinic orbit would be produced, and chaos would result.

Figure 3 shows the structure of a spiral chaotic attractor in Chua’s circuit [15]. The piecewise-linear state space is divided (by N_R) into an inner region (denoted D₀) and two outer regions (D₋₁ and D₁). There are equilibrium points in each of the three regions. A homoclinic trajectory can be formed when the outer equilibrium points (P₋ and P₊) have a stable real eigenvalue and an unstable complex conjugate pair of eigenvalues and the equilibrium point (0) at the origin has an unstable real eigenvalue and a stable complex conjugate pair. A trajectory starting from an initial state X₀ on the eigenplane Eᶜ(P₋) near P₋ spirals away from the equilibrium point along the eigenplane Eᶜ(P₋) until it enters the D₀ region, where it is folded back into D₋₁ and returns to the unstable eigenplane Eᶜ(P₋) close to P₋. In this way, trajectories are repeatedly stretched apart (in D₋₁) and folded (in D₀) to produce chaos [15].

A trajectory starting from an initial state X₀ on the eigenplane Eᶜ(P₋) near P₋ spirals away from the equilibrium point along the eigenplane Eᶜ(P₋) until it enters the D₀ region. In this way, a symmetric double scroll chaotic attractor is formed.

Prof. Chua’s doctoral student, Farhad Ayrom, had been working on synthesis techniques for piecewise-linear nonlinear resistors [5]. Leon phoned Farhad from Japan and asked him to synthesize a piecewise-linear resistor N_R with a prescribed driving-point characteristic. Using Ayrom’s design, Zhong built the first physical realization of Chua’s circuit the next day [1]. With the parameter values in Table 2, they recorded a double scroll attractor, as shown in Fig. 6 [27].

Since its invention in 1984, there has been an intensive effort to understand every aspect of the dynamics of Chua’s circuit with a view to developing it as a paradigm for learning, understanding, and teaching about nonlinear dynamics and chaos [20]. That the circuit does in fact exhibit chaos in the sense of Shilnikov was proved by Chua et al. in 1986 [3]. By adding a linear resistor in series with the inductor, the circuit has been generalized to the canonical Chua's oscillator [7,8]. This augmented six-element circuit, shown in Fig. 7, is canonical in the sense that every continuous three-dimensional odd-symmetric piecewise-linear vector field may be mapped onto the circuit [7,8]. Consequently, the circuit can exhibit every dynamical behavior that is known to be possible in a system described by a continuous odd-symmetric three-region piecewise-linear vector field. With the appropriate choice of parameters, the circuit can be made to follow the classic period-doubling, intermittency, and torus breakdown routes to chaos [7,8]. For a comprehensive bibliography of papers on Chua's circuit, we refer the reader to Chua's historical review paper [4].

The original five-element Chua's circuit quickly established itself as the first provably chaotic electronic circuit. As a result, it attracted huge interest in the electrical engineering community. With a solid underpinning theory and a robust practical implementation, Chua's circuit played an important role in the 1990s in the development of applications of chaos including chaos communications [10], cryptography, and chaos control [12]. When applications demanded high speed operation and integrated circuit implementation, appropriate variants were developed [9,25]. In 2008, a simplified four-element Chua was proposed [2]. Today, we have a three-element chaotic circuit which uses a memristor to realize simultaneously nonlinearity, local activity and memory [24].

In this chapter, we will discuss the six-, five-, four- and three-element chaotic circuits which have been inspired by Chua's original invention.
