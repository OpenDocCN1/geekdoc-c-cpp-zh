Heart cell monolayers are thin layers of tissue grown in culture dishes from embryonic or neonatal cardiac cells. Cardiac cells from very young animals have the capacity to form gap junctional connections with neighbouring cells in culture. After a few days in culture, embryonic cardiac cells are capable of supporting propagating waves of excitation over long distances. Cardiac monolayers were popular thirty years ago as model systems of two-dimensional conduction. More recently, the availability of potential mapping techniques have renewed interest in cultured monolayers, as they allow controlled environments for studying conduction on microscopic [36] and macroscopic [43] scales.

We measured calcium transients from dispersed monolayer cultures of embryonic chick heart cells as a function of age in culture, plating density, and gap junction connectivity. We found that while periodic target patterns predominate for well connected cardiac substrates, bursting patterns were frequently observed in relatively poorly connected monolayers (Fig. 1). Spiral waves were initiated by a wave that propagates in a unidirectional fashion: waves blocked at a point near the site of initiation leading to unidirectional propagation and a wavefront free end. The free ends propagate back toward the initiation site, and, as more tissue is recruited into the wavefront, are able to pass through previously unexcitable regions leading to a spiral wave. Although in most cases bursting was associated with clearly visible reentrant waves in our experimental system, in some cases bursting target waves were observed [6]. Since our imaging system has relatively low spatial resolution, we could not rule out that reentry occurred at smaller (cellular) scale. As cardiac cells tended in our hands to be either quiescent or beat periodically, we primarily focused our attention on the contribution of reentry to bursting rhythms. However, recent results from other groups strongly suggest that bursting is an intrinsic property of cardiac cells. Harada [23] showed, using an elegant experimental system which captured long term recordings of single cells and small cell clusters at high magnification, that cardiac cells display bursts of activity, and can even spontaneously transition between regular periodic activity bursting rhythms. Tong and Holden [42] showed that a cardiac cell model displays bursting dynamics for certain parameters. Finally, Himel [24] showed that pharmacological agents can induce bursting rhythms in the form early and delayed after depolarizations in isolated neonatal cardiac cells. These results suggest that communication between cardiac cells may not be relevant to the genesis of bursts in this system, and that spiral waves are the result of, but do not cause, bursting. However, it is difficult to reconcile this interpretation with the presence of cohesive waves in culture, the increasing period of rotation during bursts, or the prevalence of non bursting target waves in more well connected monolayer systems. Further studies, such as the use of higher resolution imaging equipment and simulations with populations of bursting cardiac cells, are needed to resolve this issue.

## 3. Simulating Network Bursting with CAs

Simulating the dynamics observed in the isotropic cell culture experimental system posed several challenges. First, the monolayer culture is heterogeneous at microscopic space scales, with cell-free patches and dense tissue areas. We assume that these heterogeneities play a critical role as the unidirectional propagation observed at initiation sites doesn’t occur in homogeneous excitable systems. It’s possible to use PDE and ODE models to simulate cardiac propagation at microscopic scales, however, it would be difficult to draw conclusions from a single simulation run since heterogeneities in our system are distributed throughout the tissue and do not have a single representative geometry. Furthermore, simulations at microscopic scales typically use many subcompartments per cell [40] and are therefore computationally expensive. Up-scaling cell-level simulations to macroscopic scales while running a sufficient number of geometries to gain confidence in the generalizability of the results is therefore impractical. Our group [3, 41] and others [1] have used a more simplified approach, consisting of a uniform reaction diffusion system with randomly distributed defects at simulation grid points. However, the physical interpretation of a single grid point defect in this case is difficult: in general, the underlying lattice spacing should be an order of magnitude smaller than the heterogeneities being simulated in order to avoid artefacts. Since we are primarily interested in how network activity generates bursting, we opted to use CAs as cellular activity is abstracted to a small number of essential states. The computational efficiency of CAs allow parameter sweeps and randomly generated geometries to be quickly simulated, and the simplicity of the formulation facilitates analysis of wave speed and propagation failure from first principles [3, 5].

A simple cellular automata model was developed which demonstrates that the addition of a limited number of rules can result in bursting behaviour in groups of spontaneously active, excitable, but nonendogenously bursting cells. Spontaneous activity is simulated by giving all grid points a probability of firing when excitable. The slowing and eventual annihilation of the spiral is modelled by adding a fatigue term. Fatigue refers to a phenomenon observed in cardiac tissue where periods of sustained rapid activity adversely affect conductive properties of the substrate. These effects

are thought to be due to the gradual build-up of ion species such as sodium, potassium or calcium [2,9,28,44] which move across cell membranes during each action potential. Rapid activity overwhelms the cells ability to restore the ions concentration to steady state levels. The resulting imbalances are indirectly responsible for secondary effects such as hyperpolarisation or reduced cell coupling, which alter functional characteristics (such as period and conduction velocity) of the tissue. These long term effects are referred to as fatigue in conductive tissue [2] and overdrive suppression in pacemaking tissue [28]. Fatigue is simulated in the cellular automata models by decreasing a cell’s ability to be stimulated by its neighbours as a function of its recent activity.

## 4. Bursting in a Greenberg-Hastings Model with Spontaneous Activity and Fatigue

We study a very simple cellular automaton modified from early proposals by Greenberg-Hastings [21] that we believe captures the effects of a heterogeneous cellular structure. The Greenberg-Hastings model is formulated as follows. Each site at time t is assigned a state, u_j(t), where the subscript j refers to the cell number. The neighbourhood of a given site is determined using a square (Moore) metric of diameter r. The state is an integer: 0 is a rest state; states 1,2,...,E are excited states; states E+1,E+2,...,E+R are refractory states; state (E+R+1) is identified with the rest state 0. The update rule for the state of a site is as follows: If (1 <= u_i(t) <= E+R) then u_i(t+1)=u_i(t)+1; and if (u_i(t)=0) then u_i(t+1)=0 unless u_i is excited by its neighbours, or becomes spontaneously active with probability p_s.

A cell becomes excited if it is not refractory a sufficient number of excited cells is present in a defined neighbourhood around the cell. These models display bursting if each cell is given a probability of firing when u_i(t)=0, and if excitability is modified by a fatigue term which increments for each cell every time it fires, and decays away exponentially at other times. The fatigue term in this formulation is added to the threshold for excitation.

The model shows initiation and annihilation of spirals similar to experimental observations. Impulses are initiated by the chance simultaneous firing of several sites in close proximity to each other, so that additional sites in the neighbourhood fire. Local heterogeneity prevents spread of excitation in all directions, resulting in unidirectional propagation. Two wave free ends double back to form doubled armed spiral waves. The build-up of fatigue eventually makes propagation impossible and the rotor is annihilated.

Spiral wave initiation in cellular automata depends on asymmetric initiation of a wave. Initiation of a wave occurs when a number of grid points greater than or equal to threshold K_exci are active within a neighbourhood. As the probability of initiation is low, we assume that at any initiation site only K_exci grid points are active, and that there are no grid points close to the initiation site that are refractory. If this is the case, unidirectional propagation can only occur if one or more of the initiating grid points become refractory before the target wave advances past the immediate neighbourhood of the initiation site. Consider a GH model with a Moore neighbourhood of size 1, K_exci=3, E=5, and R=6. There are 56 ((8·7·6)/6!) different positions that three active grid points can have in a given 3×3 neighbourhood. Cells can have activation states ranging from 1 to 5 in each of these configurations. At least one grid point has a value of 1 at the moment of initiation. There are three possible locations for this grid point, giving 3×5×5−14=61 different permutations (where 14 is the number of identical repeats) for the 56 spatial configurations at the initiation site. Of this 3416(56×61) different configurations, a certain portion give rise to unidirectional propagation and rotor waves, while the rest result in target patterns.

The probability that wave initiation results in a spiral can be determined by iterating the model for each of the 3416 possible configurations. As this is impractical, one spatial configuration (with 61 different configurations of activation state) is analysed in detail. The representative spatial configuration is chosen so that the results can be generalized to the remaining 55.
