Adding the fourth requirement

It was shown in Ref. [71] that the Chua circuit satisfies the four requirements which lead to the Corollary as follows [69]:

**Corollary:** Given a third order system ẋ = f(x) which has a pair of symmetric Shil’nikov connections (as per the four conditions (i) to (iv)) every knot and link type lives in an arbitrarily small tubular neighborhood of the Shil’nikov connections. This property persists under small C¹ perturbations.

As a consequence, the Chua attractor is characterized by a universal template which contains all possible knots. This is not the case of the Lorenz system for which only degenerate heteroclinic orbits have been found by setting b = 0 [72, 73]. This is therefore another interest of the Chua circuit and its double-scroll attractor.

### 3.3. A zoo of chaotic attractors

Using the slightly different version of the Chua circuit

```
⎧ ẋ = κ α (y − x − φ)
⎨ ẏ = κ (x − y + z)
⎩ ż = −κ (β y + γ z)  (13)
```

where the piecewise-linear function is

```
φ = b x + (a − b)(|x + 1| − |x − 1|)/2 ,  (14)
```

Chua and co-workers obtained various chaotic attractors [74]. The most typical examples are shown in Fig. 18. The most suggestive plane projection was shown for each case according to the orientation of the attractor in the phase space. Let us start with two asymmetric funnel attractors [Fig. 18(a)] which can fuse into a single symmetric attractor through a merging attractor crisis when one of the parameters is varied. Such a symmetric attractor looks like the one shown in Fig. 18(b). This is not exactly a common double scroll in the sense that there are foldings in each scroll in the latter but not in the former. Depending on parameter values, the winding number in the funnel may vary but there is no crucial departure in the resulting topology (we precise that we focus here on the relevant main topological types of attractors). A very special attractor, here named the “cord” attractor in the view of the “cords” linking the two scrolls, is shown in Fig. 18(c). To our knowledge, this attractor was never reported elsewhere.

The symmetric attractor shown in Fig. 18(d) looks like a Burke and Shaw attractor [75, 76]. Such an attractor is common in Lorenz-like systems [77,78]. In fact, there is also a Lorenz-like attractor [Fig. 18(e)] solution to the Chua circuit (13) but it has an inversion symmetry and not a rotation symmetry like the original Lorenz attractor. It thus corresponds to an embedding of the Lorenz attractor using variable x or y of the Lorenz system since, in that case, an inversion symmetry is recovered [79]. It is also possible to observe in the Chua circuit (13) two attractors obtained after a period-doubling cascade as observed in the Lorenz system for large R values [80]. These attractors have a Logistic-like first-return map to a Poincaré section. Nevertheless, these attractors are related by an inversion symmetry and not by a rotation symmetry as observed in the Lorenz system.

A “strange” attractor with a complex topology has also been observed [Fig. 18(g)]. Its complexity comes from the central zone, between the two scrolls. It will be shown below that such a structure is related to a toroidal structure. An attractor [Fig. 18(h)] similar to an attractor solution to a simple model for a pulsating star [81] was also found in the Chua circuit (13). This attractor has a very different structure from all the others already discussed. The topology of the similar attractor produced by the pulsating star model was investigated in Ref. [82]. The double scroll shown in Fig. 18(i) is interesting due to its central zone. The two scrolls are quite similar to the two scrolls of the common double scroll attractor but the way in which they are linked is rather unusual. It looks like a Lorenz attractor whose central part was mirrored. Note that such a special topology seems to be only observed in a system with an inversion symmetry.

The last three attractors [Figs. 18(j), 18(k), and 18(l)] are all related to the Rössler spiral attractor. The first ones [Fig. 18(j)] are two symmetry-related spiral attractors. They may become a single symmetric chaotic attractor through a merging attractor crisis. The case of the attractors shown in Fig. 18(k) looks like a two-fold cover of a spiral Rössler attractor [79]. It is thus a symmetric Rössler attractor with two foldings, one being the symmetric of the other under an inversion symmetry. A second example of such a two-fold cover was also obtained [Fig. 18(l)], but with a different orientation in the phase space compared to the attractor shown in Fig. 18(k). In spite of this, the last two attractors are topologically equivalent. The richness of the dynamics produced by the Chua circuit could result from the fact that it is characterized by a universal template as shown by Ghrist and Holmes [69].

## 4. Toroidal Chaos

![](img/039e7d3c23390584a66737067db8b75b_239_0.png)
![](img/039e7d3c23390584a66737067db8b75b_239_1.png)

**Fig. 19.** Quasi-periodic regime solution to Chua circuit (13). Parameter values: C1 = 0.0297 μF, C2 = 0.3606 μF, Ga = 0.599 mS, Gb = 0.77 mS, R = −1/0.0007 Ω, R0 = 13.4 Ω, L = 7.682 mH.

Using the Chua circuit (13), Chua and co-workers [74] obtained a quasi-periodic regime [Fig. 19(a)] whose Poincaré section is a regular closed loop [Fig. 19(b)] as expected. More surprisingly, they observed a folded torus, that is, a toroidal chaotic attractor (Figs. 20). Toroidal chaos produced by a three-dimensional autonomous system is quite rare, and only very few examples are known [83–85]. Varying the value of the capacitance C1 allows to develop the toroidal chaotic regime [Figs. 20(b) and 20(c)]. Just before a boundary crisis leaving a single fixed point for the attractor, the toroidal attractor [Fig. 20(d)] has a structure reminiscent of the double scroll attractor. When the capacitance C1 is increased, another very strange toroidal structure is obtained [Fig. 20(a)] just before a second boundary crisis. These types of toroidal chaos are not yet fully understood and remain to be more carefully investigated. Let us note that it is possible to recover parameter values reported in Table 1 from the characteristics of the electronic components as follows: α = C2/C1, β = R²C2/L, γ = RR0C2/L, a = RG_a, b = RG_b and κ = ±1 if RC2 ≥ 0. The equivalent electronic circuit is explicited in [74].

Another toroidal chaotic attractor was also found by Chua and co-workers [74]. It is shown in Fig. 21(a). It is interesting in the sense that it has a single folding (bottom right of the Poincaré section as shown in Fig. 21(b)) as the attractor solution to the rather complicated model proposed by Bo Deng [83]. This attractor is therefore a good benchmark attractor to develop a topological analysis for three-dimensional toroidal chaotic attractors.

A “torus circuit” was investigated by Matsumoto and co-workers [86] using the governing equations

```
⎧ ẋ = −α f(y − x)
⎨ ẏ = −z − f(y − x)      (15)
⎩ ż = β z
```

where the piecewise-linear function reads as

```
f(u) = −a u + (a + b)/2 (|u + 1| − |u − 1|).      (16)
```

For some parameter values, a toroidal chaotic attractor was obtained (Fig. 22). It was shown that three mechanisms responsible for the breakdown of a torus T² are realized in the torus circuit. They are [87]

- (i) a transition via a cascade of period-doubling bifurcation;
- (ii) an abrupt transition to chaos in the homoclinic region via saddle-node bifurcation of a limit cycle;
- (iii) a soft transition to chaos due to the loss of torus smoothness.
