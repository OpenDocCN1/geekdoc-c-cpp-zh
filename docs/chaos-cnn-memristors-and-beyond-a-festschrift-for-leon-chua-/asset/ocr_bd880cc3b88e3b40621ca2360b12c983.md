This paper is to look at our “old studies” on a transmission line with “the newly won positions” and describe in greater detail the vibrations in “the zone of turbulence”. The paper consists of two parts. In the first we tried to clarify what is the ideal turbulence, in the form being laconic so to keep the mathematical rigor (so we had after all to adduce some cumbersome formulas). In the second part, we show that ideal turbulence is typical for the idealized transmission line with boundary nonlinearity and, in particular, highlight parameters regions which correspond to the most developed turbulence, when the statistical properties of the vibrations are described by random processes.

# 2. Concept of Ideal Turbulence

The notion of *ideal turbulence* as spatial-temporal chaos in ideal media (without inherent resistance) was proposed one of the authors back in 1983; originally, it was named *dry turbulence* by analogy with von Neumann’s dry water [12, 13]. Since then ideal turbulence is kept in the area of scientific interests of the authors. As a result a mathematically rigorous definition of ideal turbulence and approaches to its study have been elaborated (see our works given in the bibliography).

A meaningful mathematical definition of turbulence can be given for dynamical systems on spaces of smooth or piecewise smooth functions.

Let {𝒞, Sᵗ} be an infinite-dimensional dynamical system, where 𝒞 is the space of continuous piecewise smooth functions φ : D → E, D and E are regions of Euclidean spaces, t ∈ ℤ⁺ or t ∈ ℝ⁺. The trajectory Sᵗ[φ] of φ ∈ 𝒞 is understood as a sequence when t ∈ ℤ⁺ and as a directedness when t ∈ ℝ⁺ (not to be confused with the orbit of φ).

The *spatial-temporal chaotization* in the dynamical system is perceived as an evolution of the functions φ ∈ 𝒞 under Sᵗ, which leads to that their behavior becomes more and more intricate and the limit states of the system are already impossible to describe with continuous functions (see Fig. 1). The attractor of the system, i.e. the set attracting all or almost all the trajectories, is not contained entirely in the phase space, and hence the system needs to be extended on a wider function space. The spaces of fractal and random functions are particularly appealing for use as such wider space.

For a space containing fractal functions we take the space of multivalent functions ζ : D → E, whose graphs are closed sets (such functions are called upper semicontinuous), and a metric is taken as follows

$$\rho^{\Delta}(\zeta_1,\zeta_2)=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\min\left\{\varepsilon,\sup_{x\in D}\Delta\left(V^{\varepsilon}_{\zeta_1}(x),V^{\varepsilon}_{\zeta_2}(x)\right)\right\}.$$

where Δ(·, ·) is for the Hausdorff distance between sets, Vᵋζ(x) ⊂ ζ(Vᵋ(x)), and Vᵋ(x) is the ε-neighborhood of x ∈ D.
