structure of a system is static and must therefore be studied using techniques outside standard dynamical systems theory.

Moreover, A major obstacle in understanding the dynamics of a high dimensional system is that the information needed to do so is spread throughout the various system components. Because of this it is tempting to find ways of simplifying the structure of a system while preserving some fundamental properties or features of the system’s dynamics. One of the key dynamical quantities of a network is the spectrum associated with its graph structure.

To aid in understanding how a system’s structure effects its dynamics the authors introduced the concept of an *isospectral graph transformation* in [5]. The main idea behind an *isospectral graph transformation* is that a graph can be modified in a number of ways that preserve its spectrum. As a special case, this includes reducing a graph to a smaller graph with less vertices. By so doing one is effectively concentrating the structural information contained in the graph.

By extending the notion of an isospectral graph transformation to a dynamical system it is also possible to modify the structure of almost any *discrete-time* dynamical system $(F,X)$ with dynamics $F$ and phase space $X$ in a way that preserves the system’s dynamics. As with isospectral graph reductions this is done by concentrating the information spread between the system components of $(F,X)$. The resulting transformed system is denoted by $(\mathcal{X}_S F, X_S)$ and is called a *dynamical network expansion* of $(F,X)$.

The importance of a *dynamical network expansion* is that it allows for better estimates (or at least no worse) of the untransformed system’s global stability. A major result of [5], restated here, (see theorem 3) asserts that the global stability of a system $(F,X)$ is better understood by studying one of its isospectral transforms $(\mathcal{X}_S F, X_S)$.

Importantly, the definition of a dynamical network is general enough that any dynamical system $(F,X)$ where $F:X\to X$ is Lipschitz continuous and the dimension of $X$ is greater than one can be considered to be a dynamical network. Therefore, the dynamics of a large number of dynamical systems can be analyzed in terms of their graph structure.

It is also worth mentioning that this idea of concentrating structural information by use of graph transformations has already lead to the introduction of new equivalence relations on graphs [5] and

to improvements in the classical eigenvalues estimates of Gershgorin et al. [4].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the mathematical framework for investigating the dynamics of a network. This section also provides sufficient conditions under which a dynamical network has a globally attracting fixed point (is strongly synchronizable). Section 3 then introduces the notion of an dynamical network expansion which is then used to gain improved estimates for whether a dynamical network has a global attractor. At each step in this paper examples are used to illustrate basic concepts and idea.

# 2. Networks as Dynamical Systems

As mentioned in the introduction, the dynamics of a network can be analyzed in terms of three key features; (i) the internal (local) dynamics of the network elements, (ii) the interactions between the network elements, and (iii) the topology or structure of the graph of interactions of the network.

To study the dynamics of networks our first task is to establish a mathematical framework for the investigation of networks as dynamical systems. This is done following the approach given in [1].

Let $\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and $T_i:X_i\to X_i$ be maps on the complete metric space $(X_i,d)$ where

$$L_i=\sup_{x_i\ne y_i\in X_i}\frac{d(T_i(x_i),T_i(y_i))}{d(x_i,y_i)}<\infty. \tag{1}$$

Let $(T,X)$ denote the direct product of the local systems $(T_i,X_i)$ over $\mathcal{I}$ on the complete metric space $(X,d_{max})$ where for $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X$

$$d_{max}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\{d(x_i,y_i)\}.$$

**Definition 1.** A map $F:X\to X$ is called an *interaction* if for every $j\in\mathcal{I}$ there exists a nonempty collection of indices $\mathcal{I}_j\subseteq\mathcal{I}$ and a continuous function

$$F_j:\bigoplus_{i\in\mathcal{I}_j}X_i\to X_j,$$

that satisfies the following Lipschitz condition for constants $\Lambda_{ij}\ge 0$:

$$d(F_j(\mathbf{x}|_{\mathcal{I}_j}),F_j(\mathbf{y}|_{\mathcal{I}_j}))\le \sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_j}\Lambda_{ij}d(x_i,y_i). \tag{2}$$

for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X$ where $\mathbf{x}|_{\mathcal{I}_j}$ is the restriction of $\mathbf{x}\in X$ to $\bigoplus_{i\in\mathcal{I}_j}X_i$. The *(interaction)* map $F$ is defined as follows:

$$F(\mathbf{x})_j=F_j(\mathbf{x}|_{\mathcal{I}_j}),\quad j\in\mathcal{I},\ i\in\mathcal{I}_j.$$
