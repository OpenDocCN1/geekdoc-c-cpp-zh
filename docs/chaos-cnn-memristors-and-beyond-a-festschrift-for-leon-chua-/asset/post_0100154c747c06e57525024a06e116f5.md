
## 2. Chua’s Presentation of Lagrangian Circuit Elements

A Lagrangian n-port is described by a C¹ Lagrangian function

```
L(q₁,q₂,…qₘ, q̇₁,q̇₂,…q̇ₘ, φₘ₊₁,…,φₙ, 
      q̇ₘ₊₁,…,q̇ₙ) (1)
```

for some partition {1,…m} and {m+1,…n} of the ports. qⱼ is the charge variable associated with port j, and its time derivative q̇ⱼ is the port current. φⱼ is the flux variable associated with port j, and its time derivative φ̇ⱼ is the port voltage. If m = n, the Lagrangian is a function of charge and current only, and if m = 0 it is a function of flux and voltage only. The constitutive relation of the n-port is derived from the Lagrangian by the equations

```
vⱼ = d/dt (∂L/∂q̇ⱼ) − ∂L/∂qⱼ  j = 1,…m

iₖ = d/dt (∂L/∂φ̇ₖ) − ∂L/∂φₖ  k = m+1,…n
```

Note that, although the theory is presented here in the context of n-ports, it can equally be applied to (n+1)-terminal elements.

Some examples of Lagrangian n-ports are as follows:

### Example 1. A Lagrangian of the form L(q₁) gives

```
v₁ = d/dt (0) − ∂L/∂q₁ = h(q₁).
```

This is the constitutive equation of a charge-controlled capacitor. In particular, if

```
L(q₁) = − q₁² / (2C)
```

we have a linear capacitor defined by

```
v₁ = q₁ / C.
```

### Example 2. A Lagrangian of the form L(q₁) = L(i₁) gives

```
v₁ = d/dt (∂L/∂i̇₁)   or   φ₁ = f(i₁).
```

This is the constitutive equation of a current-controlled inductor.

Building on these two basic examples we find examples such as the following:

### Example 3. A Lagrangian of the form L = f₁(q₁)+ f₂(q̇₁) gives

```
v₁ = d/dt (∂f₂/∂q̇₁) − ∂f₁/∂q₁,
```

the series combination of a current-controlled inductor and charge-controlled capacitor.

### Example 4. A Lagrangian of the form L(q₁,q₂,…qₙ) gives

```
v₁ = −∂L/∂q₁ = f₁(q₁,q₂,…qₙ)
 v₂ = −∂L/∂q₂ = f₂(q₁,q₂,…qₙ)
   ⋮
 vₙ = −∂L/∂qₙ = fₙ(q₁,q₂,…qₙ)
```

This is the constitutive equation of an n-port charge-controlled capacitor. Similarly, a Lagrangian L(φ₁,φ₂,…,φₙ) corresponds to an n-port flux-controlled inductor.

Further examples include the following:

### Example 5. The Lagrangian L = −n q₁ φ̇₂ represents an ideal transformer:

```
v₁ = n φ̇₂ = n v₂
 i₂ = d/dt (−n q₁) = −n i₁
```

### Example 6. The Lagrangian L = G φ₂ φ̇₁ represents an ideal gyrator:

```
i₁ = G φ̇₂ = G v₂
 i₂ = d/dt (−G φ₁) = −G v₁
```

### Example 7. A Lagrangian of the form L = A q₁ q₂ q₃ gives

```
v₁ = −A q₂ i₃
 v₂ = −A q₁ i₃
 v₃ = d/dt (A q₁ q₂) i.e. φ₃ = A q₁ q₂
```

This is an example of the family of circuit elements known as traditors [6], defined more generally by a

Lagrangian $L = A\eta_1\eta_2\dots\eta_{n-1}\dot\eta_n$, where each $\eta_j = q_j$ or $\phi_j$.

Of the three-port traditors, the Lagrangian $L = A q_1 q_2 \dot\phi_3$ gives

$v_1 = -A q_2 v_3$
$v_2 = -A q_1 v_3$
$q_3 = A q_1 q_2$

while the Lagrangian $L = A \phi_1 \phi_2 \dot q_3$ gives

$i_1 = -A \phi_2 i_3$
$i_2 = -A \phi_1 i_3$
$\phi_3 = A \phi_1 \phi_2$

The first of these is characterized by an algebraic relationship between the charge and voltage variables, and so represents a three-port capacitor, while the second is characterized by an algebraic relationship between flux and current, and so represents a three-port inductor. Most traditors, on the other hand, do not correspond to basic circuit elements.

The key principle here is the description of all of these elements by a scalar circuit-theoretic Lagrangian function. We see that this description can encompass a variety of basic circuit elements, along with others that do not arise as basic elements.

It may also be observed that all of the examples above are lossless circuit elements. An $n$-port representation N is lossless iff the total energy E entering N from $t=0$ to any time $\tau$ depends only on $(q_1,q_2,\dots q_m,\dot q_1,\dot q_2,\dots \dot q_m,\phi_{m+1},\dots \phi_n,\dot\phi_{m+1},\dots \dot\phi_n)$ evaluated at time $\tau$, i.e.

$\int_0^{\tau}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_j(t)\frac{dq_j}{dt}+\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}i_k(t)\frac{d\phi_k}{dt}\right]dt = E(q_a(t),\phi_b(t),\dot q_a(t),\dot\phi_b(t))$

By this definition, a network is lossless if the energy required to travel between two points of the state space is independent of the path taken. Once again, this concept is founded in classical mechanics.

There is a close relationship between the concept of the Lagrangian and that of energy transfer.

### Lemma 1. $N$ is lossless iff there exists a $C^1$ function $E(q_a(t),\phi_b(t),\dot q_a(t),\dot\phi_b(t))$, the energy function,

such that the following energy identity is satisfied:

$\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_j(t)\dot q_j+\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}i_k(t)\dot\phi_k = \sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial q_j}\dot q_j+\frac{\partial E}{\partial \dot q_j}\ddot q_j\right)+\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial \phi_k}\dot\phi_k+\frac{\partial E}{\partial \dot\phi_k}\ddot\phi_k\right)$

Proof. Differentiate both sides of (3) with respect to time.

### Theorem 1. Every Lagrangian $n$-port is lossless. In particular, its energy function is given by

$E(q_a(t),\phi_b(t),\dot q_a(t),\dot\phi_b(t)) = \sum_{j=1}^{m}\dot q_j(t)\frac{dL}{d\dot q_j}+\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\dot\phi_k(t)\frac{dL}{d\dot\phi_k}-L \quad (2)$

Proof. Substitute (2) into (4) and expand derivatives.

### Theorem 2. If a Lagrangian $n$-port with energy function $E(q_a(t),\phi_b(t),\dot q_a(t),\dot\phi_b(t))$ given by (2) has ports $1,\dots m$ terminated in short circuits and ports $m+1,\dots n$ terminated in open circuits, then $\frac{dE}{dt}=0$ and $E(t)=E(0)\forall t\ge0$.

Proof. The terminating conditions imply that $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\right)=\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j}\ j=1,\dots m$ $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\phi_k}\right)=\frac{\partial L}{\partial \phi_k}\ k=m+1,\dots n$

It follows that $\frac{dL}{dt}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j}\dot q_j+\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\ddot q_j\right)+\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \phi_k}\dot\phi_k+\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\phi_k}\ddot\phi_k\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot q_j\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\right)+\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot\phi_k\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\phi_k}\right)$

and hence

```
d/dt ( sum_{j=1}^{m} q̇_j(t) dL/dq̇_j + sum_{k=m+1}^{n} φ̇_k(t) dL/dφ̇_k - L ) = 0
```

It is possible to expand on the relationship between the Lagrangian and energy functions. If

```
E(q_a(t), φ_b(t), q̇_a(t), φ̇_b(t)) = E_0 + E_1 + E_2 + ⋯
```

and

```
L(q_a(t), φ_b(t), q̇_a(t), φ̇_b(t)) = L_0 + L_1 + L_2 + ⋯ (3)
```

where E_γ is the γ^th degree homogenous component of E expanded in ascending powers of q̇_j and φ̇_k, i.e. it is of the form

```
∑_l a_l(q_a, φ_b) q̇_1^{γ1}⋯q̇_m^{γm} φ̇_{m+1}^{γ_{m+1}}⋯φ̇_n^{γn}   γ1 + γ2 + ⋯ + γn = γ
```

with a corresponding definition for L_γ, we have the following theorem:

### Theorem 3. The γ^th degree homogeneous components of E and L are related by

```
E_γ = (γ - 1) L_γ,   γ = 1, … n.
```

In particular, E_1 = 0.

### Proof.

```
E_γ = ∑_{j=1}^{m} q̇_j dL_γ/dq̇_j + ∑_{k=m+1}^{n} φ̇_k dL_γ/dφ̇_k - L_γ = (γ - 1) L_γ
```

An n-port is non-energic if the instantaneous power flow into it is always zero — it merely transfers power from one set of ports to another. The next theorem follows:

### Theorem 4. A Lagrangian n-port with L of the form (3) is non-energic iff L = L_1, i.e. each term in L can contain q̇_j and φ̇_j to the first degree only.

### Proof. Follows from Theorem 4.

Lagrangian 1-ports with L = L_1 are of the form L(q_1, q̇_1) = f(q_1) q̇_1 or L(φ_1, φ̇_1) = f(φ_1) φ̇_1, giving,

respectively

```
v_1 = d/dt (f(q_1)) - f'(q_1) q̇_1 = 0   or   i_1 = 0,
```

the short circuit and the open circuit.

For n = 2 and L = L_1, the possibilities are:

```
L(q_1, q_2, q̇_1, q̇_2) = f_a(q_1, q_2) q̇_1 + f_b(q_1, q_2) q̇_2,
```

which gives

```
v_1 = (∂f_a/∂q_2 - ∂f_b/∂q_1) q̇_2
v_2 = (∂f_b/∂q_1 - ∂f_a/∂q_2) q̇_1
```

a non-linear gyrator;

```
L(φ_1, φ_2, φ̇_1, φ̇_2) = f_a(φ_1, φ_2) φ̇_1 + f_b(φ_1, φ_2) φ̇_2,
```

which again gives a nonlinear gyrator; and

```
L(q_1, φ_2, q̇_1, φ̇_2) = f_a(q_1, φ_2) q̇_1 + f_b(q_1, φ_2) φ̇_2,
```

which gives

```
v_1 = (∂f_a/∂φ_2 - ∂f_b/∂q_1) φ̇_2
i_2 = (∂f_b/∂q_1 - ∂f_a/∂φ_2) q̇_1
```

the equations of a nonlinear transformer.

## 3. Summary

This paper provides an introduction to Lagrangian circuit elements, following the presentation given by Leon Chua in graduate courses and invited lectures at various points over the course of his career. It highlights a number of aspects of his approach to nonlinear circuit theory: its roots in fundamental mathematical disciplines; the inclusion of elements beyond the basic set of circuit elements, based on the logical extension of existing classifications; and the pedagogical emphasis on relating new material and concepts to concepts with which the audience will already be familiar. The most spectacular example of the success of Chua’s fundamental methods is the sudden flowering of interest in the memristor in recent years, following technological developments unimaged at the time of the device’s inception. Lagrangian circuit elements, with their natural links to concepts of losslessness in circuits, may well experience a similar flowering of interest at some time in the future.
