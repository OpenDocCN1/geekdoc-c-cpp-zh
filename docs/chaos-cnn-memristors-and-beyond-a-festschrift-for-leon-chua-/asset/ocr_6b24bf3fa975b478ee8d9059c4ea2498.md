# 1. Introduction

Henri Bergson (1859–1941) was one of the most influential philosophers of the beginning of the 20th century. A central theme of his thought is the problem of time [1, 2]. He refused the idea of an homogeneous and reversible, quantitative and measurable ‘scientific’ time, since in his conception what is measured is not the time interval itself, but only a portion of space. He stated that the time interval has not physical meaning, but it is the single consciousness which registers the real duration of time, which is qualitative and heterogeneous, not measurable and irreversible. Time according to Bergson is therefore a continuous flow of unique states of the consciousness. In this sense the concept of Bergson’s time includes that of strange attractors before the discovery of these by scientists [3].

Bergson’s idea of time is that the duration is not the sequence of instants, since this would imply that only the present could exist and the past

could not preperate into the present, thus avoiding the possibility of evolution and of a real duration. Bergson retained that the duration is the continuous progress and growth of the past, which preserves itself entirely, so that the past is always present in the human consciousness. The man actions and thoughts are thus dictated by the whole history of the past, even if the man clearly and distinctly perceives only a part of the past. The consequence of this conception of the past is that the consciousness cannot pass two times through the same identical state. Even if the external circumstances are the same, the person which experiences them is not the same, since he/she is in continuous evolution thanks to the irreversibility of the duration and the continuous growth of the experience. The idea of Bergson about time is therefore that it is dynamic and not static. In this sense, the idea of Bergson is anticipated by the thought of St. Augustine, who conceived the time as a dynamic reality that is an extension of the soul itself.
