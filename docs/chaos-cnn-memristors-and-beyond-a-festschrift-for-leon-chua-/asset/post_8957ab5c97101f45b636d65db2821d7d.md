
Chua and Ushida investigated Duffing’s equation

```
ẍ + 0.1 ẋ + 2x + x³ = 0.4 cos t + 0.4 cos 0.35 t
+ 0.4 cos 0.155 t. (3)
```

This motivation was that, although describing a symmetrical mechanical pendulum, the Duffing system was often studied by Hayashi who used an electronic analog computer to make simulations [30]. It was also often quoted in books dealing with electronic circuits. Although deeply impressed by the Lorenz paper [38], Chua did not investigate the Lorenz system because it did not correspond to an electronic circuit by these times. In fact, the Lorenz system was only introduced in IEEE Journal in 1983 by Colin Sparrow [40]. The Duffing equation as investigated by Chua and Ushida can be rewritten as a forced second order differential equation

```
{
ẋ = y
ẏ = −0.1 y − 2x − x³
+ A(cos ω₁ t + cos ω₂ t + cos ω₃ t)
}(4)
```

where A = 0.4 and ω₁ = 1, ω₂ = 0.35 and ω₃ = 0.155. The system is here forced with multiple input frequencies [Fig. 4(a)]. Only an excerpt of a time series was proposed by Chua and his co-workers [39]. With all the conditions specified in the paper

we obtained a period-211 limit cycle as exhibited by computing 50,000 points in the Poincaré section after the transient regime [Fig. 4(b)]. Thus, in spite of a phase portrait which could lead to conclude to a chaotic solution, the asymptotic behavior is periodic as stated in Chua and Ushida’s paper.

## 2.2.1. Early investigations of chaotic solutions

The first occurrence of a chaotic attractor in Chua’s works was the result of a collaboration with scientists involved in numerical studies, namely Jacques Neirynck (1931-) who spent few years in the Philips Laboratories and was in charge of Computer-Aided Design, and Philippe Verburgh (1957-) who was in the Circuit Theory Group of the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology (Lausanne). One of their motivations was to demonstrate that all properties for a chaotic motion was indeed observed, that is, sensitivity to initial conditions, hyperbolicity, existence of a strange attractor and a broadband frequency spectrum. This study was performed “through a long series of piecewise linear analysis using exact analytical formulas” [41].

Chua and co-workers investigated the stability of periodic solutions and, finally, some chaotic behaviors as shown in Fig. 6. They noted the sensitivity to initial conditions and checked that it was a “real phenomenon” not caused by numerical instability. As they claimed, “these results represent the most reliable numerical confirmation to date of chaotic motion in a real physical circuit”, that is, realistically modeled by ordinary differential equations [41].

To end their investigation, they computed a Poincaré section [Fig. 6(b)] and performed a spectral analysis. The signature of an attractive set was thus obtained according to the picture revealed by points in the Poincaré section: “These points seem to be all attracted toward certain well-defined curves in the state space.” The Poincaré section shown in Fig. 6(b) is an example of the well-defined curve Chua and co-worker observed.

At this step, it is possible to draw the path followed by Chua and co-workers before investigating chaotic behaviors. (i) Chua introduced some nonlinear elements, (ii) he investigated the stability of periodic solutions, (iii) the evolution of asymptotic behaviors versus circuit parameters was investigated in terms of Hopf bifurcations, (iv) he developed algorithms to numerically integrate the equations (to be ensured that numerical instabilities were avoided) and (v) he investigated the dynamics of nonlinear circuits not satisfying any known stability theorem. This whole path was traced within the field of electronics without any major connection with the emerging “chaos world”. Note that Chua should be regarded as a theoretician in electronics and was always focussed to develop a general theory for electronic circuits, dynamics included.

## 2.3. Relaxation oscillations

In 1983 with Thomas S. Parker, Chua investigated relaxation oscillations using a piecewise-linear version of the van der Pol equation [44]. The electronic circuit (Fig. 7) was simplified compared to the one used by Ueda [Fig. 5(a)]. The governing equations

![](img/039e7d3c23390584a66737067db8b75b_229_2.png)

Fig. 7. The circuit producing relaxation oscillations investigated by Parker and Chua [44].

are
{ \dot{x} = y
\dot{y} = 1/ε [ -f(x) y - x + B cos ωt ]
where f(x) = | Rx + 2R x0      x < -x0
           | -R x          for |x| < x0
           | Rx - 2R x0      x > +x0.
(7)

The parameter values used for numerical simulations were R = 1 Ω, x0 = I0 = 1 A and ω = 1 rad.s⁻¹. Mostly, periodic solutions were investigated for providing “intuitive understanding” of relaxation oscillations in connection with the mathematical papers by Levinson [9] and Levi [45]. In particular, Levi “proved what types of behavior are possible and how this behavior is structured as B is varied.” In order to do that, Levi used rotation numbers as introduced by Levinson. Using numerical simulations, Parker and Chua showed “how the different subharmonic solutions to the piecewise-linear relaxation oscillation equation are intimately related.” It should be clear that B actually plays the role of an initial condition for system (7) when rewritten in the autonomous form

```
{ \dot{x} = y
\dot{y} = 1/ε [ -f(x) y - x + u ]
\dot{u} = v
\dot{v} = -ω² u.
(8)
```

Parameter B is thus “hidden” in the autonomous form as an initial condition u0 = B. This is therefore the phase space which is investigated and not the parameter space [46]. Parker and Chua thus provided tables of rotation numbers in agreement with Levi’s results.

In the other hand, Levinson found that “ most of the solutions [...] are certainly not periodic. [...] Of the continuum of sequences there is a countable infinity of periodic sequences” [9]. This leads Parker and Chua to remark that for some ε and B values, Levinson showed that “given any (perhaps nonperiodic) sequence of component subharmonics, there exists a trajectory which follows that sequence.” The existence of “essentially random behavior” was mentioned in the conclusion. When numerical simulations are performs, it can be observed that most of parameter values lead to limit cycle

but we identified a quasi-periodic regime [Fig. 8(a)] and a metastable toroidal chaos [Fig. 8(b)] which ends with a period-2 limit cycle. Although chaotic attractors were not yet clearly investigated, plane projections of phase portraits were already widely used.

In another paper with Alistair Mees, Chua investigated chaos as a lack of synchronization [47] where chaos was defined by its properties: (i) sensitivity to initial conditions, (ii) non periodicity, (iii) broad band Fourier spectrum, and (iv) complicated recurrences. They investigated chaotic motion using a kind of symbolic dynamics validated by numerical simulations. Again, the analytical approach was preferred for providing a proof for chaotic motion. Three quotations

- T. Li & J. A. Yorke, A period-three implies chaos, American Mathematics Monthly, 82, 985, 1975;
- R. May, Deterministic models with chaotic dynamics, Nature, 256, 165–166, 1975.
- J. Guckenheimer, Symbolic dynamics and relaxation oscillations, Physica D, 1, 227-235, 1980.

are remarkable in the sense that they are manifestations of the growing Chua’s interest for “chaos theory”.

With Tang and Mees, Chua then investigated an astable multivibrator consisting of a capacitor connected across a resistive (memoryless) nonlinear circuit [48]. They investigated how the loss of synchronization implies chaos. In their circuit, they proved their model has the following features:

- (1) There are no stable periodic solutions of any order.
- (2) There is no almost periodic solution that is not periodic.
- (3) There are unstable periodic solutions. If α is an integer, these exist for all possible periods.
- (4) Any two trajectories either coincide exactly after a finite time, or are separated by order 1 infinitely often.
