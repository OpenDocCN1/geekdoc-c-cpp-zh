The solution reads

$$t-t_0=\int_{u(t_0)}^{u}du'\exp\left(-\Phi(u')\right), \tag{53}$$

where $u(t_0)=u_0$. It yields a singularity at finite time in the forward time direction if $\int_{u_0}^{u}du'\exp(-\Phi(u'))$ is a converging integral, which requires $c$ to be negative. This occurs at the end of the stick interval, as illustrated in Fig. 9(a), but their is no finite time singularity in the full DR system. There the divergence of the singularity is replaced by a large back and forth swing ending with a slowing down and a return to the SM. A physical consequence of this behavior is that, before the catastrophe $u$ is negative and positive at the end of it, changing the strain from dilative to compressive. This does not seem to be what happens in real earthquakes, where the strain remains dilative after the quake and so can generate secondary quakes weeks after a major slide. This points toward a large effect of friction during the quake itself. In the DR model this means that $\xi$ should be rather small to enhance friction with respect to inertia: a large $\xi$ means that inertia is dominant, which means that an almost complete back and forth swing is needed to return to a small velocity at the end of the fast phase. By decreasing $\xi$ one increases the effect of friction during the fast phase. This makes possible to end it with $u$ still positive (as observed) whilst the friction term $\ln(v)/\xi$ is increased in Eq. (30). This puts physical constraints on the DR model to represent large earthquakes, particularly on the value of $\xi$ (in real life it is constrained by the ratio of the strain before and after the quake, a measurable quantity in principle).

Note that the solution (52) was derived within the adiabatic approximation (42), which limits its validity to a domain we are going to precise. On the surface $S$, the time derivative of $v=\exp(-\xi u-\theta)$ writes $\dot v=-e^{-2(\xi u+\theta)}[\xi+\epsilon\theta+(1+\epsilon)\xi u]$ by using Eq. (49) and the second equation (45). Therefore in the space $(u,\theta)$ the straight line of Cartesian equation

$$\epsilon\theta(u)=-(1+\epsilon)\xi u-\xi. \tag{54}$$

splits the SM into a part such that trajectories crossing this line tend to still lower values of $\dot v$, and so follow even more closely the SM, although when the crossing is in the other direction, $\dot v$ tends to increase, as well as $v$ itself (always positive) and so ends up with the finite time divergence just described. A rough estimate of condition (42) limits the 2D flow to the domain located above the line

$$\theta(u)=-\xi u-\ln(\gamma). \tag{55}$$
