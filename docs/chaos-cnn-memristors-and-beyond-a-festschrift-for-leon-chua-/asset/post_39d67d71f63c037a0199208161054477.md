## 2. The Behavior of Single Memristors

According to Chua’s definition [4], a memristor is a device which has a characteristic curve in the \(\Phi-Q\) domain where \(\Phi(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}v(t')dt'\) denotes the flux and \(Q(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}i(t')dt'\) denotes the charge. The characteristic curve may be represented by either of the two possibilities,

$$\Phi=F(Q)\quad\text{or}\quad Q=G(\Phi). \tag{1}$$

In the first case, we call the device a charge-controlled memristor whereas in the second case, the device is called a flux-controlled memristor. The memristance \(M\) and the memductance \(W\) are defined by

$$M=\frac{dF(Q)}{dQ}\quad\text{and}\quad W=\frac{dG(\Phi)}{d\Phi}, \tag{2}$$

respectively. In 1976, Chua and Kang generalized the memristor to the concept of memristive systems [5]. The memristance of such a system depends on an internal state vector \(\mathbf{x}\). A memristive system is defined by

$$v(t)=M(\mathbf{x})\cdot i(t)$$
$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}=f(\mathbf{x},i). \tag{3}$$

A capacitance and an inductance can be defined as well with an additional state vector giving the memory-related quantities \(L(\mathbf{x})\) (meminductance) and \(C(\mathbf{x})\) (memcapacitance) [14]. The best-investigated type of memristor is the \(TiO_2\)-memristor which usually is called the HP memristor for the sake of simplicity. Starting with a linear drift model [13], the memristive behavior could be described simply by a series of voltage dividers. A more advanced model takes into account a nonlinear drift and the boundaries of the memristor by means of a window function \(F(x)\) (e.g., [9]),

$$v(t)=\left(R_{ON}x+R_{OFF}(1-x)\right)\cdot i(t)$$
$$\dot{x}=\lambda\cdot i(t)\cdot F(x). \tag{4}$$

More sophisticated models based on coupled ionic/electronic drift-diffusion [12] and tunneling mechanisms (in particular Simmons tunneling) [16] have been developed. In [7] it has been shown that the dynamical behavior of this element can be determined by calculating a Bernoulli-type differential equation

$$\dot{i}(t)-\frac{\dot{v}(t)}{v(t)}i(t)+\frac{k}{v(t)}[i(t)]^3=0. \tag{5}$$

Another approach in [2] provides the analytical treatment of a wider class of memristors, including the HP memristor with nonlinear drift, and points out that in general, the dynamics of those systems can be described by a (generalized) Abel-type differential equation.

However, those descriptions are valid only for single memristors. Going further, even simple memristor circuits reveal very interesting properties. In Fig. 3, a circuit consisting of a capacitor, a memristor, and an inductor is depicted. In Fig. 4 the quantities show that, on the one hand, the initial voltage of \(+20\) mV leads to a smooth relaxation of all quantities. In contrast, the initial voltage of \(-20\) mV leads to a short current spike and a following relaxation.

## 3. The Multilinear Approach

### 3.1. Basics

According to [10], the multilinear theory is based on the assumption that every (node) voltage and every (branch) current can be expressed as a series of the form

$$V(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}V_n(t).$$

![](img/039e7d3c23390584a66737067db8b75b_543_0.png)

The \(n\)-th order voltages (or currents) \(V_n(t)\) must be defined such that a multiplication of every source in the circuit with the same constant \(\alpha\) will result in the new voltage (or current)

$$V(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha^n V_n(t). \tag{6}$$

Both of the Kirchhoff laws for current and voltage still hold for each order \(n\) [10]. And for each of the basic circuit elements, there are small linear sub-circuits (the “\(n\)-th order models”) which implement the relationship between \(V_n(t)\) and \(I_n(t)\). The \(n\)-th order model of a circuit element does not depend on any quantity of order \(n\) or higher, but it may contain a time-dependent voltage source depending on voltages or currents of any order lower than \(n\) [10].

Given a nonlinear circuit, the analysis works as follows: One starts by replacing each circuit element with its first order model. The resulting linear circuit is solved, and the time-dependent sources of the second-order models are calculated. Then, the circuit elements are replaced by their second-order models, and the circuit (which is still linear) is solved again. This process may be repeated several times. In the end, the voltages and currents of the different orders are added simply, setting \(\alpha=1\), to get an approximation of the voltages and currents in the real circuit.

It is hoped that this might help with the study (or even the design) of nonlinear circuits.
