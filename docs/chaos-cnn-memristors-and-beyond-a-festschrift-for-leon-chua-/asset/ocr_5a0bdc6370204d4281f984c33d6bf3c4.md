But how far can it spread? If we look at f(y)_1 and we use the same formulas (1)-(3) to compute f(y)_1, we see that f(y)_1 = f(x)_1 since y_0 = x_0, y_1 = x_1, y_2 = x_2, and consequently we have also f(y)_i = f(x)_i for all i ≥ 1. That is to say, that y perturbation propagates, in one iteration, to at most one site to the right.

Moreover, as above, looking at f^2(y)_2, f^2(y)_3, etc. the y perturbation is propagated, in two iterations, at the far right at most to the second site. It is possible that this perturbation propagates also to the left of 0, but we focus only on the propagation to the right of the (left) perturbations of x.
Therefore, doing more iterations, the perturbation propagates to the right step by step. The closest site to 0 never reached by any left perturbation y of x in n iterations is denoted by 	ilde{Λ}_n^+(x). 	ilde{Λ}_n^-(x) is defined in the same way for right perturbations of x that propagate to the left of the site 0.

It so happens that in this definition the site 0 plays a priviliged role while the rule is translation invariant regardless of the site position. In order to make the definition shift invariant a new exponent is defined as
Λ_n^±(x) = sup_{j∈ℤ} 	ilde{Λ}_n^±(σ^j x) (7)
It is then defined the time average speed of propagation (by unit time) as:
λ^±(x) = lim_{n→∞} 1/n Λ_n^±(x) (8)
This average speed was termed by Wolfram and Shereshevsky the left (in the case “-”) and right (in the case “+”) Lyapunov exponents. It can be proved using the Kingman subadditive ergodic theorem that the above limit indeed exists in the sense of the mathematical measure theory (i.e. almost everywhere). Later on, a slightly different concept of an average Lyapunov exponent was given in [19].
