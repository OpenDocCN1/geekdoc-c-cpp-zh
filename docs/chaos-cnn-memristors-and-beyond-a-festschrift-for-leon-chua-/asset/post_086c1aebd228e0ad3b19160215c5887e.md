## 2.1. Retinal position, spatial frequency and orientation

The spatial receptive field of V1 simple cells can be accurately modelled by a Gabor function [9, 13, 15], which accounts for neuronal selectivity along the dimensions of retinal position, spatial frequency and orientation. For vertical orientations, the Gabor function has the form

$$g_e(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma_x^2}-\frac{y^2}{2\sigma_y^2}\right)\\\times\cos(\Omega_xx+\phi) \tag{1}$$

The standard deviations of the Gaussian envelope, $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_y$, control the width of the receptive field and therefore the selectivity to spatial position. The parameter $\Omega_x$ determines the peak spatial frequency tuning, and $\phi$ is a phase shift parameter. By the uncertainty relationship, there is an inverse relationship between selectivity to spatial position and selectivity to spatial frequency. The subscript “e” indicates the even symmetry of the cosine function when $\phi=0$. Other orientations can be obtained by rotating this function. Figure 1 shows Gabor functions with four different orientations.

The response of an orientation selective simple cell whose receptive field is centered at retinal position can be modelled by

$$r_s(x,y)=\left(\left|\iint g_e(x-x',y-y')I(x',y')\,dx'\,dy'\right|^+\right)^2 \tag{2}$$

![](img/039e7d3c23390584a66737067db8b75b_204_0.png)

where $I(x,y)$ represents the input image intensity and $|x|^+=\max\{0,x\}$ is the positive half-wave rectifying nonlinearity. The combined operation of half-wave rectification and squaring is called *half-squaring*.

The response of a complex cell can be modelled as the sum of four simple cell responses whose phase parameters differ by $\pi/2$. Since filter kernels that differ in phase by $\pi$ are equivalent, except for a change in sign, the response is equivalent to squaring and summing the non-rectified responses of two filters: one with kernel $g_e$ and the other with kernel $g_o$, which is identical to $g_e$ except that the cosine term is replaced by a sine term. The subscript “o” refers to the odd symmetry in $x$ when $\phi=0$. These two kernels are said to be in phase quadrature, since they differ in phase by 90 degrees. This sum of squared filter outputs is called the *orientation energy*, and is maximized for input stimuli whose orientation match the orientation of the filter kernels. Although the simple cells responses are phase sensitive, i.e. their response to a sine wave grating depends upon its phase, complex cell responses are largely phase invariant. Notationally, it is convenient to express this pair of filter kernels as a single complex valued filter kernel

$$g(x,y)=g_e(x,y)+jg_o(x,y) \tag{3}$$

$$=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma_x^2}-\frac{y^2}{2\sigma_y^2}\right)\\\times\exp\left(j(\Omega_xx+\phi)\right) \tag{4}$$

The orientation energy is the squared modulus of the complex valued filter response.

### 2.2. Temporal frequency and direction of motion

By adding a temporal filtering stage to the Gabor spatial filters, we add selectivity for temporal frequency and direction of visual motion. These filters exploit the fact that rigid motion can be viewed as an orientation in the space-time domain [1, 24].

The temporal response of cortical cells can be modelled a function with the following form

$$h_e(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\tau^\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{t}{\tau}\right)\cos(\Omega_tt+\phi_t)u(t) \tag{5}$$

where $u(t)$ is the unit step function which is 1 for and 0 otherwise, $\tau$ is the time constant of the envelope, $\alpha$ determines the degree of skewness, and $\Omega_t$ and $\phi_t$ determine the frequency and phase of the

sinusoidal modulation [5]. For long time constants, the peak temporal tuning of this function is determined primarily by Ω_t.

The spatio-temporal receptive fields of cortical receptive fields can be modelled by combining the functions in (1) and (5) as follows:

$$f_e(x,y,t)=g_e(x,y)h_e(t)-g_o(x,y)h_o(t) \tag{6}$$

where the function h_o is obtained from h_e by replacing the cosine by a sine. Motion selective simple cell responses can be modelled according to (2), where is g_e replaced by f_e, and the integral extends over time as well. Given the equations in (1) and (5), this simple cell would be tuned to horizontal motion with velocity −Ω_t/Ω_x. The sign of the velocity tuning (positive or negative) indicates the selectivity to direction of motion (right or left). Selectivity to motion in the opposite horizontal direction can be obtained by switching the sign of Ω_t or Ω_x. Selectivity non-horizontal directions of motion can be obtained by rotating the spatial receptive field profiles.

As in the case of orientation, motion tuned complex cell responses are obtained by summing four simple cell outputs. This sum is called the *motion energy*, and is equivalent to summing the squared (non-rectified) outputs of two phase quadrature linear filters, where the phase quadrature partner of (6) is

$$f_o(x,y,t)=g_e(x,y)h_o(t)+g_o(x,y)h_e(t) \tag{7}$$

Notationally, it is convenient to express this pair of filters as a single complex valued filter kernel

$$f(x,y,t)=f_e(x,y,t)+jf_o(x,y,t) \tag{8}$$
$$\qquad\qquad=g(x,y)h(t) \tag{9}$$

where

$$h(t)=h_e(t)+jh_o(t) \tag{10}$$
$$\qquad=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\tau^\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{t}{\tau}\right)$$
$$\qquad\times\exp(j(\Omega_t t+\phi_t))u(t) \tag{11}$$

Notice that although the real valued kernels f_e and f_o are not separable in space and time, the complex kernel is.

### 2.3. Binocular disparity

For simplicity, we ignore the temporal filtering component of the responses of disparity tuned cortical neurons in the following discussion. However, this can be incorporated by adding a temporal filtering component to the monocular spatial filters discussed below, in a manner as discussed in the previous subsection [5].

Disparity selective simple cells in the visual cortex can be modelled by a linear binocular filter that sums the responses of two monocular filters modelled by Gabor functions, followed by a half squaring nonlinearity [2–4, 17]. The linear binocular filter output is

$$r_{lin}=\iint g_{eL}(x_L-x',y_L-y')I_L(x',y')dx'dy'$$
$$\qquad+\iint g_{eR}(x_R-x',y_R-y')I_R(x',y')dx'dy' \tag{12}$$

where I_L(x,y) and I_R(x,y) are the image intensities of the left and right eyes, and g_eL and g_eR are Gabor functions (1) whose parameters are identical except possibly for their phase values φ_L and φ_R. The monocular receptive field profiles of the left and right eyes are centered at locations (x_L,y_L) and (x_R,y_R). The binocular simple cell output is r_s=(r_lin)^2.

As in the case of orientation and motion tuning, complex cells are modelled by summing the outputs of four simple cell outputs. This sum is called the *binocular energy*, and can be obtained by summing the squared (non-rectified) outputs of two phase quadrature binocular filters, where the phase quadrature partner of (12) is obtained by each monocular Gabor function by its phase quadrature partner.

For monocular RFs tuned to vertical orientations, the resulting binocular filter will be selective for horizontal disparity. The disparity tuning of the cell is determined by the difference in the center locations Δc = x_R − x_L and the difference in phase Δφ = φ_R − φ_L. For a disparity of d, I_R(x,y) ≈ I_L(x−d,y). The binocular complex cell will respond maximally when the input has a disparity approximately

$$D_{pref}\approx\Delta c-\frac{\Delta\phi}{\Omega_x} \tag{13}$$

As discussed in the previous section, we must compute phase quadrature pairs of filter responses, which can be considered as the real and imaginary parts of a complex valued filter output. The spatial phase parameters is a free parameter, but it is only necessary to compute the pair of filter responses with φ = 0, since quadrature pairs at other phase shifts can be computed by linear combinations of these two filter responses. In addition, different values of phase parameters only alter the phase properties of the simple cell responses. There is no difference between the responses of complex cells computed from the outputs of simple cells with different phase parameters, since the complex cell outputs are combined in such a way as to make their responses phase invariant. Since the responses of complex cells are much more robust to changes in the stimulus

Indeed, many biologically inspired algorithms for extracting disparity and motion information use complex cell outputs exclusively.
