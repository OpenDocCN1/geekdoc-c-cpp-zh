# 1.  Introduction

Professor Leon Chua has been making great contributions to dynamical systems theory and its applications, including chaotic electrical circuits such as Chua’s circuit (e.g., Chua et al. [1]), cellular neural networks [2], and memristors [3] that may realize the implementation of plastic circuits similar to biological neural networks. Thus, his achievements cover many disciplines such as nonlinear science, engineering, and neuroscience.

In this chapter, we present another approach that combines chaotic dynamics with neural networks, namely the modelling of chaotic neural networks [4,5].

The brain is a large-scale nonlinear network composed of neurons and synapses. A seminal mathematical model of a neuron was proposed by McCulloch and Pitts in 1943 [6]. This model uses binary output values (0 and 1, that is, non-firing and firing, respectively), with discrete-time updating, which makes its operation similar to that of a logic element.

The actual behavior of biological neurons is far more complicated and dynamic than that of the McCulloch–Pitts model. In particular, strange attractors with chaotic dynamics and their different bifurcations, or routes to chaos are observed in nerve membranes [5, 7–10] both experimentally (in squid giant axons) and numerically (in nerve equations like the Hodgkin-Huxley equations [11]). In this chapter, we introduce a neural network model composed of neurons with such chaotic neurodynamics [4, 5] and its extended models.

## 2. A Model of Chaotic Neural Networks

As a simple neuron model that can reproduce chaotic dynamics observed in squid giant axons, a chaotic neuron model has been proposed [4, 5, 12]. This model is based on Caianiello’s neuronic equation [13] and the Nagumo–Sato model [14], and is represented as follows:

```
$$y(t+1)=f\left[A(t)-\alpha\sum_{d=0}^{t}k^d y(t-d)-\theta\right], \tag{1}$$
```

where $y(t+1)$ is the output of the neuron at $t+1$ and has a continuous value between 0 and 1; $t$ is a discrete time step ($t=0,1,2,\ldots$); $f$ is the sigmoidal output function, e.g., $f\{x(t)\}=1/\{1+\exp(-x(t)/\epsilon)\}$ with the steepness parameter $\epsilon$; $A(t)$ is the input strength at $t$; $k$ is the decay factor; $\alpha$ is the refractory scaling parameter; and $\theta$ is the threshold value for firing. The term $\alpha\sum_{d=0}^{t}k^d y(t-d)$ in Eq. (1) represents the accumulation of refractory effects due to a past firing sequence, each effect of which decays exponentially with factor $k$ and is linearly superimposed [14].

By defining the internal state $x(t)$ as $x(t)=A(t)-\alpha\sum_{d=0}^{t}k^d y(t-d)-\theta$, the dynamics of a single chaotic neuron receiving temporally constant stimulation $A(t)=A$ can be described by the following one-dimensional map [4]:

```
$$x(t+1)=F(x(t))=kx(t)-\alpha f\{x(t)\}+a, \tag{2}$$
```

where $a=(A-\theta)(1-k)$.

Figure 1 shows the graph of the chaotic neuron map, which is bimodal and similar to return plots of the experimental data obtained from squid giant axons [15, 16]. Here, the bimodal map is a piecewise monotone continuous map with two turning points $P_1$ and $P_2$ [17]. The map of the chaotic neuron in Fig. 1 has $(+ - +)$ signs marking the slopes on the three monotonic intervals. As the decay factor $k$ in Eq. (2) usually takes a value in

0 < $k$ < 1 to characterize the monotonous decay, the derivatives in the two (+)-regions, which correspond to non-firing and firing, are also between 0 and 1. The derivatives in the middle (−)-region, on the other hand, can be less than −1 if the steepness parameter $\epsilon$ is sufficiently small [4]. This middle (−)-branch describes a continuous type of threshold separatrix peculiar to excitable dynamics in nerve membranes [18]; it generates sensitive dependence on initial conditions because of its steep slope of less than −1. Thus, the chaotic neuron model provides a new kind of bimodal map related to excitable nerve dynamics, one that is different from conventional bimodal maps such as cubic maps and circle maps.

Figure 4 shows (a) the bifurcation diagram of the internal state $x(t)$, (b) the average firing rate $\rho$, and (c) the Lyapunov exponent $\lambda$ when the value of the bifurcation parameter $a$ in Eq. (2) is changed from 0 to 1. Here, the average firing rate $\rho$ and the Lyapunov exponent $\lambda$ are defined as follows:

```
$$\rho=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}y(t), \tag{3}$$

$$\lambda=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\ln\left|\frac{dF(x(t))}{dx(t)}\right|. \tag{4}$$
```

The dynamics of the $i$th chaotic neuron integrated in a chaotic neural network with $M$ external neurons and $N$ constituent neurons that are mutually connected is generally described as follows [4, 19, 20]:

```
$$y_i(t+1)=f_i\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{ij}\sum_{d=0}^{t}k_e^dA_j(t-d)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}\sum_{d=0}^{t}k_f^dh_j\{y_j(t-d)\}-\alpha\sum_{d=0}^{t}k_r^dg_i\{y_i(t-d)\}-\Theta_i\right\}. \tag{5}$$
```

where $y_i(t+1)$ is the output with a continuous value between 0 and 1 at discrete time $t+1$; $f_i$ is the continuous output function; $A_j(t-d)$ is the strength of the input from the $j$th external neuron at discrete time $t-d$; $v_{ij}$ and $w_{ij}$ are synaptic weights from the $j$th external neuron and from the $j$th constituent neuron, respectively; $k_e$, $k_f$, and $k_r$ are the decay factors for the external inputs, the feedback inputs, and the refractoriness, respectively $(0 \le |k_e|, |k_f|, |k_r| \le 1)$; $h_j$ and $g_i$ are the transfer functions of the axon in the $j$th neuron for propagating action potentials and the refractory function that represents the strength of the refractory effects caused by the output $y_i$, respectively, where both $h_j$ and $g_i$ are usually assumed to be the identity functions for the sake of simplicity, i.e., $h_j(y_j)=y_j$ and $g_i(y_i)=y_i$; and $\alpha$ and $\Theta_i$ are the refractory scaling parameter and the resting threshold value, respectively.

By defining three internal state variables $\xi_i$, $\eta_i$, and $\zeta_i$ (which correspond to the externally applied spatio-temporal inputs from neurons outside the network, the spatio-temporal feedback inputs from the constituent neurons within the network, and the refractoriness, respectively), as $\xi_i(t+1)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{ij}\sum_{d=0}^{t}k_e^dA_j(t-d)$, $\eta_i(t+1)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}\sum_{d=0}^{t}k_f^dh_j\{y_j(t-d)\}$, and $\zeta_i(t+1)=-\alpha\sum_{d=0}^{t}k_r^dg_i\{y_i(t-d)\}-\Theta_i$, we obtain the following difference equations for chaotic neural networks:

```
$$\xi_i(t+1)=k_e\xi_i(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{ij}A_j(t), \tag{6}$$

$$\eta_i(t+1)=k_f\eta_i(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}y_j(t), \tag{7}$$

$$\zeta_i(t+1)=k_r\zeta_i(t)-\alpha y_i(t)-\theta_i. \tag{8}$$
```

where $\theta_i\equiv\Theta_i(1-k_r)$. The network dynamics is calculated by simultaneous Eqs. (6)–(8) for $i=1,2,\ldots,N$, whereas the output $y_i(t+1)$ of the $i$th neuron is obtained by transforming the internal states to the output value through the output function $f_i$ as $y_i(t+1)=f_i\{\xi_i(t+1)+\eta_i(t+1)+\zeta_i(t+1)\}$.
