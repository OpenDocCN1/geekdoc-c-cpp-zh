## 5. Bursting in CAs with Heterogeneity in Cell Spacing

The bursting Greenberg-Hastings CA has several limitations when compared to real cardiac tissue. First, the CA displays artefacts associated with the underlying grid. These artefacts are apparent at neighbourhood sizes close to the base lattice spacing: simulations with small Moore neighbourhoods have planar wave fronts with sharp corners instead of smoothly curved waves. Although the use of larger neighbourhood sizes alleviate this problem, larger neighbourhoods average out asymmetries due to random activation, and therefore do not display unidirectional propagation and bursting. Second, the Greenberg-Hasting formalism doesn’t simulate local changes in cell connectivity correctly. For example, unidirectional propagation caused by asymmetries in cell loading, which is observed in real cardiac tissue [36], cannot be replicated in a CA in which an excitable cell determines activity based only on the number of neighbouring active cells. Finally, experiments in which a control parameter is continuously varied are difficult to simulate: we observed subtle dynamic changes when continuously lowering connectivity of cardiac cell networks using pharmacological agents, but connectivity in the Greenberg-Hastings CA can only be changed by integer values. We incorporated several modifications to the original formulation that address these issues.

In the original Greenberg-Hastings model all cells were on a square lattice. We employ a method first used by Marcus and Hess [30] and introduce spatial heterogeneity in the cell density by perturbing the location of each cell. Given the original coordinates (x0, y0) with a base grid spacing of 1, the

new coordinates are $(x_0 + e_x, y_0 + e_y)$, where $e_x, e_y$ are uniformly distributed in the interval $(-0.5, 0.5)$. We also assign a random weight for a cell’s influence on its neighbours. Each cell is assigned a random weight, $S_i$, uniformly distributed in the range $(0.5 < S_i < 1.5)$. The results are qualitatively the same for constant $S_i$. However, if each weight $S_i$ is a whole number, the values of neighbourhood size where propagation fails cluster around the square root of whole numbers. Incorporating random values for $S_i$ is a simple way to introduce a continuously varying number of neighbours with varying neighbourhood size. This approach allows analytic determination of wave speed as a continuous function of neighbourhood size, as well as an estimate of parameters that give rise to blocked wave propagation [4, 5]. The model generates smooth wavefronts for large neighbourhoods while displaying irregular fronts with local asymmetries for small neighbourhood sizes. Wave propagation in the revised model more closely mimic what is thought to occur in real tissue, where conduction is irregular at cellular space scales but is continuous at larger space scales [40].

The condition for a cell ‘i’ to become excited is as follows. If $u_i(t)=0$, then $u_i(t+1)=1$ if the sum of all excited cells divided by the sum of all nonexcited cells in a neighbourhood is over a threshold $\theta$. The use of the ratio allows the model to simulate the effects of current sinks that are known to modulate propagation in cardiac systems [3]. The model can be used to simulate a classic cardiac experiment that demonstrates the interplay between local geometry and cell-cell connectivity. Figure 2 demonstrates the effect of a sudden expansion on propagation. Due to asymmetric cell loading at the expansion point, waves can propagate into the narrow channel from the expanse, but cannot propagate in the reverse direction. The wave can propagate in both directions if coupling is decreased by changing the neighbourhood size from 3 to 1.8. This behaviour cannot be simulated using a standard Greenberg-Hastings CA as the effects of asymmetric cell loading on an excitable cell are not accounted for.

In order to terminate bursting activity, we add a fatigue term similar to that used in the original formulation: the fatigue term of cell ‘i’ at time $(t+1)$ is $\gamma_i(t+1)=\gamma_i(t)+F_{add}$ if $u_i(t+1)=1$ and $\gamma_i(t+1)=g\gamma_i(t)\times F_{sub}$ if $u_i(t+1)\neq 1$. The fatigue term is added to the threshold $\theta$. The model bursts as a function of network connectivity. The model displays target patterns for large neighbourhood sizes. ## 6. Network Bursting in CAs in the Absence of Reentry

The cellular automata model can also display triggered activity for large neighbourhood sizes. We explored the dynamics of the CA discussed in the last section, but here a small subset of cells beat with a fixed period randomly chosen within an interval instead of having all cells have a low probability of spontaneously firing [6]. Oscillators are phase reset when excited by neighbouring cells. Triggering in the model occurs when a wave acts to synchronise pacemakers, which increases the probability of subsequent synchronous activations. Pacemakers desynchronize over time because faster pacemakers fire before others and are subsequently refractory to phase resetting. We quantified the dynamics of the model by running the simulation for a large number of randomly assembled grids for different neighbourhood sizes. The potential for a grid to generate triggered bursting was measured by initiating a wave at one edge of the grid, and testing whether a triggered wave is generated after a short interval. The probability for generating spiral wave bursts is highest at r = 1.65 while the probability for triggered target wave generation is highest at r = 2.1 [6]. Triggered waves occur relatively infrequently: spontaneous reentry occurs in three times as many randomly generated simulation grids [6]. In contrast to spiral wave bursting, triggered wave bursting does not require addition of a fatigue term for spontaneous termination.

## 7. Conclusions

Initiation of bursting in CAs depends on the interplay between local differences in geometry and the connectivity of the network. Although cardiac tissue may be more accurately represented at the cell level with biophysically realistic continuous partial and ordinary differential equation models, the computational efficiency of CAs make them well suited for modelling the effects of randomly distributed heterogeneities, asymmetries in connectivity and variation in local spontaneous activity. The inherent stochasticity of paroxysmal cardiac arrhythmias such as nonsustained ventricular tachycardia may be a reflection of similar variability at microscopic space scales. If so, CAs can give valuable insights into arrhythmogenesis in diseased tissue.
