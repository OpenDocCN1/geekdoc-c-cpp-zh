
## CHAPTER 35: SOLITONS FOR DESCRIBING 3-D PHYSICAL REALITY: THE CURRENT FRONTIER

PAUL J. WERBOS*
National Science Foundation, Arlington, Virginia 22230, USA*

This chapter begins with a review and assessment of four key frontiers for the fields of memristors, neural networks and chaos: (1) use of learning architectures to expand the possible markets for dense memristor chips, crucial to applications such as power grid intelligent enough to improve the economics of renewable energy; (2) advanced modeling and hardware to build such chips, from graphene to magnetoresistance; (3) second generation quantum computing; and (4) development and use of chaotic soliton models to rebuild the foundations of physics. Current more detailed reviews of the first three frontiers are summarized. The bulk of the paper discusses how a “soliton” model of the electron can fill outstanding gaps in basic physics, such as explanation of the mass and radius of the electron without a need for renormalization. It reviews the two soliton models most widely discussed in physics today, the Skyrme model and the BPS monopole, and proposes alternative Lagrangians starting from the bosonic sector of electroweak theory which may have the required properties.

### 1. Introduction and the Larger Context

There are many exciting new frontiers opening up for advanced research involving memristors, chaos and beyond. This introduction will provide a very broad review of four of these frontiers — learning based architectures for memristor systems, new physical hardware, the injection of quantum computing capabilities into these systems, and extension of the underlying understanding of physics. After the introduction, this paper will focus on the fourth of these frontiers. It will specify three new dynamical systems, describe their properties, their potential use in providing a new model of the electron, and outline the challenges ahead.

The most important new frontier for practical applications of memristors today is the greater use of learning in order to make greater use (and find greater application) of all kinds of massively parallel hardware [1]. Traditional systems for neural networks and machine learning had difficulties in meeting the real needs of applications with a high degree of complexity, or with a mix of continuous and discrete variables; however, we now understand the principles which allow us to overcome those limitations [2, 3].

There is a clear four step roadmap [3] for developing more powerful general-purpose learning systems by: (1) developing more general systems for “vector intelligence” by better integrating fundamental principles for optimal decision-making and prediction; (2) moving up a level by injecting new approaches to handling complexity in space (as a few systems now begin to do, such as some breakthrough systems which have recently broken world records in image recognition [4, 5]); (3) handling skill learning and complexity over time in a more principled way (as no working systems really do quite yet); and finally (4) adding capabilities for brain-like stochastic search [6] which exist at the highest levels of the mammal brain.

Even the fulfillment of this roadmap in technology will not end up replicating the fullest capabilities of the human mind [7], but it will enable many important technological capabilities. For example, the current White House policy statement on the smart grid [8] states that “NSF is currently supporting research to develop a ‘4th generation intelligent grid’ that would use intelligent system-wide optimization to allow up to 80% of electricity to come from renewable sources and 80% of cars to be pluggable electric vehicles (PEV) without compromising reliability, and at minimum cost to the Nation (Werbos 2011).” [9]. In paying for electricity, the world now pays on the order of 10 cents per kilowatt hour (kwh) for 20,000 terawatt hours per year (IEA/OECD data on total electricity in 2008). That amounts to $2 trillion per year. The most cost-effective sources of solar power and wind can supply electricity at about that price, or less, in quantity in the US, and at lower cost in most other places. (By contrast, the latest estimates I hear from industry sources say that the total cost for new nuclear power plants, exploiting the new expedited safety reviews, will still end up costing 13.5 cents per kwh, and present greater problems outside the US.)

This would seem to say that we can become independent of fossil fuel, avoid military conflicts over fossil fuel, and avoid the large-scale environmental threats associated with fossil fuel or nuclear proliferation, simply by shifting over to wind and solar, at a small cost or small profit in the end. But in the real world, the ten cents cost which gets paid to generators is not the full cost of using wind and solar. Use of wind or solar on the real world power grid involves huge new costs in stabilizing the power grid (an operation conducted in real time), and in backup and storage. Yet wind and solar farms contain a lot of switchable power electronics, which could convert these energy sources into assets rather than liabilities, if only we could generate the right control signals to make use of this control authority in real time (every two seconds rather than every 5 to 15 minutes). At present, the most advanced system for optimal power flow in practical use is the system recently developed by Marija Ilic of Carnegie Mellon, which makes approximate calculations every 15 minutes, and lacks full measures to anticipate areas of future power scarcity, but has shown major savings over older systems in the areas where it has been tested. But with cellular types of neural networks and new cellular hardware, Liang et al. [10] have illustrated how it is possible to perform the same kind of task, with a system designed for anticipatory optimization [2], in real time. The sheer speed due to new computing hardware is a crucial factor here. If solar farms and wind farms can be converted from being a net cost to a net asset in stabilizing the grid, the benefits could clearly be on the order of trillions of dollars per year — and perhaps large enough to overcome the obstacles which block today’s efforts to solve the urgent problems of the world in that sector.

Of course, the development of new computer hardware to exploit memristor capability and learning together, to provide ever greater computational horsepower, is another important frontier being funded by NSF, a frontier which needs to be advanced in lockstep with the advancement of learning architectures [1]. This frontier requires the use of very fundamental dynamical systems and quantum physics [11] to exploit the utmost in what we can do with hardware. There are many possible physical platforms for memristors, as other chapters in this book will probably review more systematically. In the short term, many of us pay special attention to the stories of transistors now reaching hundreds of gigahertz from IBM, using graphene, and from Intel, using InAs, but many believe that systems using magnetic spin effects or quantum tunnel junctions could do better (in memory and density as well as speed) once the physical problems are better understood and overcome. This in turn requires very complex new mathematical tools, especially for exploiting multibody coherence effects [11]. At these frontiers of physical hardware, it becomes both essential and difficult to account for quantum effects and noise in multibody physics, and to develop a better understanding of the flow of heat (phonons). The better understanding of many-body effects may also allow breakthroughs in the efficiency of solar cells [12] and thermoelectric chips.

Using traditional methods from quantum electrodynamics (QED [13, 14]), it is extremely challenging to understand these effects for physically interesting systems. For example, to model a system with a million electrons, we are asked, in principle, to solve a partial differential equation (the Schrödinger equation) over a space of three million dimensions. Quantum optics has been very effective in understanding and controlling many-body effects such as coherence (lasers) and entanglement, in part because it uses mathematical methods like P, Q and W mappings [15, 16] to convert these systems in high dimensions into traditional partial differential equations (PDEs) over three dimensions [15, 16]. To the extent that phonons are bosons, we can apply an extended version of the same mappings (e.g., see [17]) to handle phonons. The emerging field of quantum phononics, borrowing methods from quantum optics, may be of enormous importance to developing advanced memristor hardware. The P, Q and W mappings need to be used together with other mathematical methods, of course, but they bring us back to the need to develop a better understanding of partial differential equations (PDEs) over three dimensions, the main theme of this chapter after the introduction. These new tools also provide a pathway to solving serious fundamental problems in the rigorous axiomatic formulation of quantum field theory in general [17].

The mapping from many body effects to PDEs in 3 dimensions, in order to allow useful large-scale simulations, applications and technologies, is also an important emerging part of empirical nuclear physics [18].

Can we envision even more advanced information technologies, going beyond the capabilities of the mammal brain and the densest possible classical massively parallel computing? Can we inject some kind of quantum computing capability into these kinds of systems? This is a very controversial area, of course, but it makes sense for some of us to explore that third frontier while others work on the more immediate ones.

In my view, we cannot make full use of quantum computing effects until we develop a new, more complete understanding of what quantum mechanics really tells us, and how it really works.

In my view [19, 20], quantum mechanics is ultimately more about the direction of the flow of time than it is about systems over a million dimensions. It is very hard for some people to imagine the possibility of technology which could allow us to affect the direction in time of the flows of information and of heat, but this makes more sense to me, at least, than metaphysical theories of physics which assume that there is no physical reality out there at all [19, 20].

This third frontier leads to a number of immediate empirical opportunities which I have already discussed elsewhere [20]. But it also leads to a kind of fourth frontier, a more scientific frontier. For me, this frontier began with a “simple” question: can we use nonlinear system dynamics in three dimensions to give us a better understanding of what an electron really is, which would then let us use methods like those of quantum optics and empirical nuclear physics [15–18] to model systems of many electrons? Yet in exploring that question, I recently learned that this mathematics is inextricably connected as well to a major high-risk high-potential technology project in Russia [18, Sec. 11.5], aimed at developing methods for the complete conversion of matter to energy. Putin has recently noted that Russia’s new project in nuclear technology, if successful, would be as far beyond fission and fusion as those are beyond gunpowder. Yet Manton emphasizes that better understanding and control over coherence effects will be essential to mastering that technology. It now seems that our “simple” question about the electron is important both to the physical assumptions and to the coherence issues which will determine the outcome of that effort.
