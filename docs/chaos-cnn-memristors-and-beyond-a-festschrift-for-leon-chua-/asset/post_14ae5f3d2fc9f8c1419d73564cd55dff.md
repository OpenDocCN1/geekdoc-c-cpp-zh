
not suitable as a model of the electron, for several reasons.

In the first place, it has only one quantized charge, which is generally interpreted as magnetic charge, not electric charge; magnetic charge is actually a promising idea under serious consideration in grand unification physics, but without electric charge we do not have an electron. In any case, the exact relation between the A field of that model and the ordinary A, W and B fields is not at all clear. Secondly, though the BPS solution does also allow for some electric charge, discovered in the work of Julia and Zee, it does not actually quantize the electric charge; if electrons were BPS solitons, they would presumably vary continuously all over the map in how much charge they possess. And finally, the family of solutions discovered by Prasad and Sommerfield are a continuous family, with individual members defined by the amount of electric charge and by a continuous parameter C. In the limit as λ goes to zero, they compute the mass-energy of the “soliton” as exactly:

```text
M = (C/α) cosh² γ  (13)
```

where α and γ are parameters of the field theory itself. From Eq. (13), it would appear that we have a “ridge” of solutions of different energy, which can dissipate away to zero in practice, even if the magnetic charge itself is fixed and localized. Or to put it another way, the small perturbations which result from differentiating the Prasad-Sommerfield solutions with respect to C would be able to reduce the total energy.

Adding a simple fixer term may be able to solve this problem, but that may or may not be unnecessary when we move to a more realistic model in any case.

It would be very useful and instructive to analyze another possible model, which is a kind of hybrid between Eqs. (10)–(12) and the systems of Sec. 2:

```text
L = L_W + L_B - 1/2 L_φl - 1/2 L_Qr - V,  (14)
```

where the first two terms are defined as in Eqs. (2) and (3), where I have replaced the isovector φ by a set of two three-isovectors, Q and φ, where

```text
L_φl = c_l | (∂_μ - i/2 g τ·W_μ - i/2 g' B_μ) φ |²  (15)

L_Qr = c_r | (∂_μ - i/2 g' B_μ) Q |²  (16)

V = λ₁(Q² - F₁²) + λ₂(φ² - F₂²)  (17)
```

Manton [18, Sec. 11.5] has shown that EWT itself does not possess topological solitons, because the nonzero boundary condition does not impose enough constraint on the usual version of the Higgs field as r → ∞.

Section 2 attempts to solve these problems through a unique new integration of key features.

First, of course, it is a slight variation of EWT itself, such that the connection to electromagnetism and to the W and B fields is quite explicit.

Second, by borrowing the asymmetric left-hand versus right-hand coupling from EWT, it allows coupling to two different components of the fields, one on the left and one on the right, which makes it possible to have two topological charges. This allows quantization of the electric charge, as well as the magnetic charge, at the same time — crucial to modeling the electron, while not disrupting the potential value later on to grand unification physics. Of course, the violation of parity is a fundamental property of EWT and of the empirical work which led up to EWT.

Third, the field φ has eight degrees of freedom in the center of the soliton, but the boundary conditions reduce that to four asymptotically — two on the left, and two on the right, exactly what we need to create the kind of topology which Manton [18] and the others call for.

Even if better models are developed someday which can describe the electron, these fundamental properties appear essential, one way or another.

## 5. Beyond the Fourth Frontier: Connections to the Rest of Physics

If and when someone fully meets the challenge described in Sec. 2, it will open the door to two kinds of follow-on. First, there are technological connections discussed in earlier sections. Second will be the challenge of unifying a new view of the electron with larger questions in physics. Until we actually have a new model of the electron in hand, we cannot envision all the details, but we know that the connections will be important.

First of all, we may well find that there are several possible variations of these models, which yield solitons (and excited states) with different mixes of electric and magnetic charge and mass. That would give us some choice, in addressing further empirical issues. With this family of models, it is not necessary to inject back-door assumptions like infinite negative mass, or like renormalization in general, to make them mathematically meaningful; however, in quantizing these bosonic field theories, we do have a choice between P, Q and W mappings [15–17], if and when we really need a field operator representation. As in quantum optics and in skyrmion research [18], we can get very far using semiclassical methods alone.

Second, these solitons could be used as a kind of alternative quark model, based on magnetic and electric charge rather than the more complex properties assumed today, as proposed years ago by Julian Schwinger [34]. This would fit with the startling empirical findings of Macgregor [35] of Lawrence Livermore, who found evidence that simple electromagnetic coupling (alpha) seems to be decisive in determining the masses and lifetimes of all the known elementary particles. Though I strongly disagree with Comay’s proposed Lagrangian for electromagnetism [36], his concept of the nucleon as a bound state of four objects, core and quarks, all defined by electric and magnetic charge and mass rather than the usual properties, and some of the associated empirical arguments, may be worth thinking about further, in conjunction with the other sources cited in [34].

Finally, the unification of such classical field models with general relativity would be quite straightforward, using the recipe given by Moshe Carmeli [37]. This should also be true for the alternative model of gravity by Moffat, which currently appears more promising in view of empirical data in 2012 suggesting a low density of dark matter.

Of course, this would still leave open the mysteries related to superweak interactions (CP violation), dark energy, dark matter, and perhaps some other interesting phenomena beyond today’s standard model of physics — but it would be a great step forwards in any case.
