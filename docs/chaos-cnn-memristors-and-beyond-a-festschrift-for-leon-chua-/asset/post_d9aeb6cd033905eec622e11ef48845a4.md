
Neighboring grid points can be considered a special case of cellular automata [32]. Cellular automata were originally designed by John von Neumann to model self-reproducing networks of cells in analogy to cellular reproduction through the replication of DNA. Cellular automata also model for an excitable medium how a two-dimensional automaton leads to target patterns or spiral waves. The simple mechanism that generates these target and spiral wave patterns illustrates that the key of self-organization is the local rules of interaction between neighboring sites.

The local activity principle of cellular automata leads us to Leon O. Chua who studied this principle in cellular automata (CA), cellular nonlinear networks (CNN), and electronic circuit theory [7]. These approaches allow a mathematical definition which can easily be translated into other non-electrical homogeneous media. The transistor is an example of a locally-active device, whereby a “small” (low-power) input signal can be converted into a “large” (high-power) output signal at the expense of an energy supply (namely a battery). No radios, televisions, and computers can function without using locally-active devices such as transistors. Recently, the concept of locally-active memristors has been exploited for nanoelectronic device applications [5, 11], as well as for pinpointing the dynamical mechanisms for generating action potential in ion channels, such as the sodium and potassium channels in the celebrated Hodgkin-Huxley nerve membrane model.

For the formation of complex biological and chemical patterns, Schrödinger and Prigogine demanded nonlinear dynamics and an energy source as necessary conditions. But, for the exhibition of patterns in an electronic circuit (i.e. non-uniform voltage distributions), the claim for nonlinearity and energy source is too crude. In fact, no patterns can emerge from circuits with cells made of only batteries and nonlinear circuit elements which are not locally active.

In general, a spatially continuous or discrete medium made of identical cells interacting with all cells located within a neighborhood is said to manifest complexity iff the homogeneous medium can exhibit a non-homogeneous static or spatio-temporal pattern under homogeneous initial and boundary conditions.

The principle of local activity can be formulated mathematically in an axiomatic way without mentioning any circuit models. Moreover, any proposed unified theory on complexity should not be based on observations from a particular collection of examples and explained in terms that make sense only for a particular discipline, say chemistry. Rather it must be couched in discipline-free concepts, which means mathematics, being the only universal scientific language.

However, in order to keep physical intuition and motivation behind this concept, we start with a special class of Cellular Nonlinear Networks (CNN), namely the reaction-diffusion CNNs [7]. A CNN is any spatial arrangement of locally-coupled cells, where each cell is a dynamical system which has an input, an output, and a state evolving according to some prescribed dynamical laws, and whose dynamics are coupled only among the neighboring cells lying within some prescribed sphere of influence centered at the cell’s location. In short: a CNN is defined by the state equations of isolated cells and the cell coupling laws (in addition to boundary and initial conditions).

In a reaction-diffusion CNN, each cell located at any 3-dimensional grid point with coordinates (α,β,γ) is defined by a state equation

\[
\dot{x}_{\alpha\beta\gamma} = f(x_{\alpha\beta\gamma})
\]

and a “diffusion” cell coupling law

\[
\dot{x}_{\alpha\beta\gamma} = D\nabla^2 x_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
\]

where D is a constant diffusion matrix with diagonal elements (diffusion coefficients) associated with the state variables of \(x_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\). The term \(\nabla^2 x_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\) is the discrete Laplacian operator. If we add the cell coupling law to the cell state equation, we obtain the CNN reaction-diffusion equation

\[
\dot{x}_{\alpha\beta\gamma} = f(x_{\alpha\beta\gamma}) + D\nabla^2 x_{\alpha\beta\gamma}.
\]

### 1.3. From discrete to continuous dynamics

If the discrete Laplacian operator is replaced by its limiting continuum version, we get the standard reaction-diffusion partial differential equations (PDE). According to extensive numerical simulations, CNN reaction-diffusion equations and their associated PDEs have similar qualitative behavior. For example, various spatio-temporal patterns from Prigogine’s Brusselator and Oregonator PDE have been simulated by the corresponding reaction-diffusion CNNs.

A locally active CNN cell must behave exactly like a transistor: At a cell equilibrium point Q there exists a local (i.e. infinitesimal) input current signal such that by applying this small information-bearing signal about the DC operating point Q imposed by the battery, we can extract more local (infinitesimal) information-bearing energy δs(Q) at Q over some time interval, at the expense of the battery, which supplies the signal energy amplification. A CNN cell is said to be locally passive [8], if there is no cell equilibrium point where it is locally active. There are well-known sets of necessary and sufficient conditions for testing whether a cell is locally passive [10]. A violation of any one of these conditions would make a cell locally active. Thus, there are constructive procedures for testing local activity.

For reaction-diffusion CNN equations, the CNN parameters must be chosen so as to make its cells locally active, in order to make it possible for complex phenomena such as pattern formation to emerge. These parameters can be derived numerically from the set of local activity criteria. In a so-called linear stability analysis of, e.g. Prigogine’s dissipative structures, at least one eigenvalue of the Jacobian matrix of f(·) associated with the kinetic laws of reaction-diffusion equations must have a positive real part for patterns to exist. But this is only one of four independent conditions each of which is sufficient for local activity. Another one can be satisfied with all eigenvalues having negative real parts. This is the standard operating mode of most useful transistor amplifiers.

The key concept of this essay claims that a locally-active cell with only negative eigenvalues appears to be dead, when uncoupled because each is asymptotically stable, but together could generate patterns when they are coupled by a dissipative environment. This is counter-intuitive because dissipation should tend to damp out any non-uniformity! A mathematical theory which explains this perplexing phenomenon is what Turing [48] and Smale [46] had sought in vain. Prigogine was totally confused that he could only lament that a new term “instability of the homogeneous” needed to be introduced. We claim the principle of local activity is precisely this missing concept and foundation. In particular it is the condition that the real part of the “complexity function”, the cell impedance in circuit parlance, be negative at some frequency. It is this non-intuitive condition that we call “edge of chaos”, which is a completely different concept from that advocated in the popular literature.

The fundamental new concept here is that if the cells are not locally active, no passive coupling circuits of any complexity can be found to create patterns in the resulting CNN. The universality of the concept of local activity is manifested clearly by its local activity criteria which are independent of the nature of the CNN cells. The only assumption we demand is that any system exhibiting complexity can be represented by a mathematical model which can be decomposed into elements and coupling laws.

## 2. The Local Activity Principle and Symmetry Breaking in the Universe

The emergence of complex patterns is explained by locally active units (elements, cells, agents etc.) in homogeneous media. In mathematical terms, a homogeneous state means symmetry. Thus, local activity leads to symmetry breaking of homogeneous and uniform states. The concept of locality, symmetry breaking and emergence of new structure is also fundamental in quantum cosmology. In order to understand cosmological applications of the local activity principle, a deeper analysis of quantum cosmology and quantum field theory is necessary.

In general, principles of symmetry play a central role in physics. The invariance and covariance properties of a system under specific symmetry transformations can either be related to the conservation laws of physics or be able to establish the structure of the fundamental physical interactions. This is the most essential aspect of symmetry as it concerns the basic principles of physics and the interactions themselves and not only the properties of geometric figures [25, 26].

### 2.1. Mathematical definition of symmetry

In geometry, figures or bodies are called symmetrical when they possess common measures or proportions. Thus the Platonic bodies can be rotated and turned at will without changing their regularity. Similarity transformations, for example, leave the geometric form of a figure unchanged, i.e. the proportional relationships of a circle, equilateral triangle, rectangle, etc. are retained, although the absolute dimensions of these figures can be enlarged or decreased. Therefore one can say that the form of a figure is determined by the similarity transformations that leave it unchanged (invariant).
