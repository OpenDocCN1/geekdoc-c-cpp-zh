where $t_a,t_b$ are the numerical integration time boundaries. The half-height half-width $\tau_x$ of the correlation function (and $\Delta\nu$ of the spectrum) are reported in Fig. 4. Both widths display the critical slowing down which is well-known for gradient flows near bifurcations. Consider first the left curve. The figure displays a strong increase of the correlation time $\tau_x$ of the fluctuation $(x(t)-x_0(t))$ in the intermediate time range, reaching its maximum value at time $t\sim1.5\,t_0$. This sudden enhancement of the correlation time corresponds to the “slowing down” before the critical time, it is preceded by a slow growth of $\tau_x$ (not shown in the figure, increasing by a factor ten for $-100t_0<t<0$), and is followed by a rapid decrease before the critical time.

This rapid decrease is connected with the rapid growth of the variance close to $t_c$, and does not exist when the correlation function is defined as $\Gamma_{x_1}(t_1,\tau)/\sigma(t-\tau/2)\sigma(t+\tau/2)$ [1] where $\sigma(t)$ is the time dependent variance of the random function $x_1(t)$. In the case of earthquakes, this result leads to the following behavior: $\tau_x$ slowly grows during nearly a week, it strongly increases few hours before the catastrophe reaching its maximum about two hours before $t_c$, then it strongly decreases. Such a remarkable behavior should be used as precursor. Mathematically the increase of the correlation length *before* the catastrophe can be understood when looking at the formal expression (29). The second derivative of the potential vanishes at $t=t_0$, that leads to the flatness of $g_0(t)$ in the whole domain $0<t<t_c(a)$, as shown in Fig. 5(a). Fig. 5(b) shows that the “precursor time”

(see caption) is given by the relation

$$t_{prec}\sim2t_0 \tag{33}$$

which corresponds to about 4 hours.

As for the spectral width, the result is just the opposite: it continuously decreases from large negative time, until the time $t\sim1.5t_0$, where it suddenly grows. The slow decrease of $\Delta\nu$, corresponding to a slow shift of the spectrum towards low frequencies, is followed by a rapid spectral broadening at the end of the intermediate domain, before the transition time $t_c(a)$. As $a$ increases, the shift towards low-frequencies remains visible with an appropriate time window, but the last stage (spectral broadening) becomes unnoticed. Note that the choice of the time extension $w$ of the window function is important for such a study, here we have chosen $w\sim t_0$, because the maximum of the correlation time is of order $t_0$ before the catastrophe. The two stages of the spectral width change are both important, because they occur *before the transition*.

The growth of the fluctuations and their shift to lower frequencies can be understood as follows. As the transition approaches the potential $V(x,t)$ becomes flatter and flatter, making weaker and weaker the restoring force towards equilibrium. Therefore, at constant noise source, the amplitude of the fluctuations driven by this noise source will grow because the damping is less and less efficient. Moreover, the typical time scale for this damping will get larger and larger because of the decreasing stiffness of the potential, which will favor noise at lower and lower frequencies.
