
We now determine the dynamics of the delay by the following equation:
\[\frac{d\tau}{dt} = -\beta \frac{d\Delta}{d\tau}.\tag{8}\]

Here Δ is the same as Δ̄ but after a substitution (approximation) τ(t′) → τ(t). We choose the value of the parameter β in such a way as to satisfy 1/β ≫ 1/ν, i.e. the delay time τ evolves much slower than the time of averaging. The form of Eq. (8) suggests that the functional Δ may be regarded as a potential in the space of delay τ. Since the dependence Δ = Δ(τ) has a form of parabolas in the neighborhoods of periods of UPOs, one may expect that the nearby solution of varied delay will converge towards the minima, τ → Tk, where Tk is the period of the desired UPO.

After denoting the gradient as G(t) ≡ dΔ/dτ, and differentiating it with respect to time, we can collect the above results into one system:
\dot{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X}, K[s(t) - s(t - \tau(t))]), \tag{9a}
\dot{\tau} = -\beta G, \tag{9b}
\dot{u} = \gamma[s(t - \tau(t)) - u], \tag{9c}
\dot{G} = -\nu G + 2\gamma[s(t) - s(t - \tau(t))][s(t - \tau(t)) - u]. \tag{9d}

Equation (9c) is introduced for an approximate estimation of the time-derivative, i.e. we set \dot{s}(t - τ(t)) ≈ γ[s(t - τ(t)) - u] in Eq. (9d). Equations (9b)–9(d)) enable us to find the dynamics of the variable delay τ(t). They are valid if the parameters satisfy the following inequalities: 1/γ ≪ 1/ν ≪ 1/β.

In Fig. 4 we show the results of numerical simulations for the period-one UPO of the Rössler system, when the state variable X = (x, y, z) satisfies Eqs. 1. We see that the adaptive algorithm forces the variable delay to converge towards the period of UPO, although the initial delay τ0 = 3 differs considerably from the period T ≈ 5.88 of the UPO.

### 3.1. Brief review

Synchronization of oscillations is a phenomenon common to a large variety of nonlinear dynamical systems in physics, chemistry, and biology [31]. Whereas first investigation of the synchronization phenomenon goes back to the work by Huygens in 1665, the past decades have witnessed a considerable interest in the topic of synchronization

However, it has been shown that synchronization can appear in chaotic systems in many different ways, including the identical, phase, lag, and anticipating synchronization. The latter type of synchronization introduced by Voss [53] is most counterintuitive.

In the case of anticipating synchronization one deals with two systems, a “master” and a “slave” which are coupled unidirectionally via a time-delay feedback in such a manner that the slave system predicts the behavior of the master system. The coupling scheme introduced by Voss is as follows:
\dot{\mathbf{r}}_1 = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r}_1) \tag{10a}
\dot{\mathbf{r}}_2 = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r}_2) + \mathbf{K}[\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2(t - \tau)], \tag{10b}
where \mathbf{r}_1(t) and \mathbf{r}_2(t) are the dynamic vector variables of the master and slave system, respectively,

f is a nonlinear vector function, τ is a delay time, and K is a coupling matrix. It is easy to see that the anticipatory synchronization manifold r2(t) = r1(t + τ) is a solution of Eqs. (10). For appropriate choice of τ and K, this solution can be stable, i.e., the slave anticipates by an amount τ the output of the master. This phenomenon has been studied numerically for a variety of systems and justified experimentally in electronic circuits [55] and lasers [51].

Implementation of anticipating synchronization as a strategy for real-time forecasting of a given dynamics requires a design of coupling schemes with a possibly large anticipation time. The analysis performed in [53] shows that the scheme (10) with a diagonal matrix K is ineffective. Its maximum stably anticipation time is much shorter than the characteristic time scales of the system’s dynamics. In order to enlarge the prediction time it was proposed to extend Eq. (10b) with a chain of N unidirectionally coupled slave systems [54]:

```
ṙ_i = f(r_i) + K[r_{i-1} - r_i(t - τ)],   i = 2, ..., N + 1. (11)
```

Formally, the prediction time of this scheme is N times larger as compared to the scheme (10b). However, it was shown that the chain (11) is unstable to propagating perturbations, and this convective-like instability limits the number of slaves in the chain which can operate in a stable regime [25].

Recently, we addressed the question, whether it is possible to considerably prolong the prediction time via a suitable choice of the coupling matrix K and for typical low-dimensional chaotic systems, we gave a positive answer [39,42]. In the next two sections, we briefly outline our ideas. We describe two algorithms of design K based on the scheme (10) with the single slave system. In Sec. 3.2, we show that using the idea of phase-lag compensating coupling (PLCC), the prediction time can be enlarged several times in comparison to the diagonal coupling. In the Sec. 3.3, we describe an extended PLCC (EPLCC) algorithm that allows us to prolong the anticipating time to the characteristic period of chaotic oscillations.

### 3.2. Phase-lag compensating coupling

First, we demonstrate a heuristic idea of the PLCC algorithm with the Rössler system [45], which is given by a three-dimensional (3D) vector variable

r = [x, y, z] and vector field

```
f(r) = [-y - z, x + ay, b + z(x - c)]. (12)
```

In the following, we set a = 0.15, b = 0.2, c = 10 and suppose that both r and f are the vectors columns. Although the Rössler system has two fixed points, the strange attractor originates from one of them, r0 = [(c - s)/2, (s - c)/2a, (c - s)/2a], located close to the origin, where s = (c² - 4ab)^{1/2}. The fixed point is a saddle-focus with an unstable 2D manifold (an unstable spiral) almost coinciding with the (x, y) plane and a stable 1D manifold almost coinciding with the z axis. The phase point of the system spends most time in the (x, y) plane moving along the unstable spiral according to approximate equations ẋ = -y, ẏ = x + ay. Whenever x approaches a value x ≈ c, the z variable comes into play. The phase point leaves for a short time the (x, y) plane and then returns to the origin via a stable z-axis manifold.

Taking into account such a topology of the strange attractor, we choose the coupling matrix as K = kQ, where k is a scalar parameter defining the coupling strength and

```
Q = ( cos α  -sin α  0 ; sin α  cos α  0 ; 0  0  0 ) (13)
```

is a 3 × 3 matrix that projects the vector field onto the unstable (x, y) plane and rotates this projection by the angle α = ωτ. Here ω is a frequency of the unstable spiral, which for the Rössler system is ≈ 1. The main advantage of such a choice consists in phase-lag compensation of the time-delay feedback term in Eq. (10b). When the system moves along the unstable spiral in the (x, y) plane, the vector Qr2(t - τ) is in-phase with the vector r2(t) [cf. Fig. 5], and thus the term Kr2(t - τ) provides a correct negative feedback. We refer to this coupling law as a phase-lag compensating coupling (PLCC). We now consider a more precise and more general development of the above idea for any Rössler-type dynamical system. Suppose that a strange attractor of a 3D chaotic system ṙ = f(r) originates from a saddle-focus fixed point r0 such that f(r0)=0. Generally, the unstable and stable manifolds of the fixed point may have arbitrary orientations in the phase space. To design the coupling for this general case we first shift coordinates to the fixed point and rewrite the governing equation in the form Ṙ = JR + N(R), where J = ∂f/∂r|r=r0 is the Jacobian matrix, N(R) = f(r0 + R) − JR is a nonlinear function, and R = r − r0. Then Eq. (10b) for the slave system can be written as

```
Ṙ2 = JR2 + N(R2) + K[R1 − R2(t − τ)]. (14)
```

According to our assumptions, the Jacobian J has a pair of complex conjugate eigenvalues λ1,2 = γ ± iω with γ > 0, corresponding to 2D unstable spiral manifold, and a real negative eigenvalue λ3, representing the stable 1D manifold. By a suitable change of variables, the Jacobian J can be transformed to Jordan normal form — that is,

```
E−1 J E = ( γ  −ω  0 ; ω  γ  0 ; 0  0  λ3 ), (15)
```

where E is the matrix of eigenvectors of J. This transformation orients the unstable 2D manifold towards the (x,y) plane and the stable manifold towards the z axes. After such a transformation, we can apply the above theoretical arguments and use the coupling law K = kQ. This means that in the original (non-transformed) variables the coupling matrix has to be constructed as

```
K = k E Q E−1. (16)
```

Equation (16) gives a general algorithm of coupling design for typical chaotic systems. Application of the general coupling law (16) to the Rössler system does not advance significantly the forecasting algorithm in comparison to the above considered heuristic approach. This is because the Jacobian of the Rössler system is close to Jordan normal form and the matrix E Q E−1 does not differ significantly from the matrix Q.

The general coupling law (16) allows us to extend the prediction horizon not only for the Rössler-type systems, but also for more complex double-scroll attractors. A typical example of such a system is the Chua circuit [6] defined by the flow f(r) = [(g(y−x)−iN)/C1, (g(x−y)+z)/C2, −y/L], (17)

where x, y, and z represent, respectively, the tension across two capacitors and the current through the inductor, and iN = m0 x + 0.5(m1 − m0)(|x + Bp| − |x − Bp|) is a piecewise-linear function. We choose the parameters C1 = 0.1, C2 = 1, L = 1/5, m0 = −0.5, m1 = −0.8, Bp = 1, and g = 0.58 such that the circuit operates in the double scroll regime. These fixed points have identical eigenvalues and eigenvectors since their Jacobian matrixes coincide.
