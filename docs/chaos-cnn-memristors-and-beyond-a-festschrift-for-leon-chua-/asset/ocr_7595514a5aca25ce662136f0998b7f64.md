# 4. Discussion and Perspectives

We have presented two models of “relaxation dynamics” with similar looking behavior: long periods of “quiet” slow motion are interrupted by fast transitions, followed again by slow phases, etc. However the two models are deeply different, both because they show different mechanisms of slow to fast and reverse transitions, but also because they are objectively different in their response to an external source of noise. The saddle-node like model shows the standard “critical slowing down” with time correlations becoming more and more long ranged before the transition, something well-known in many areas. Here the correlation time reaches a maximum within a short time interval before the transition, of order $t_0$. On the contrary the DR-inspired model shows a very slow squeezing of the correlation time of the response as the transition approaches, the opposite of the dynamical saddle-node transition. In both cases the maximum of the correlation time occurs at time $t_c-t_0$, which is delayed from the catastrophe time $t_c$ by the time scale $t_0$, but in the DR model the intermediate time $t_0$ is much longer than in the saddle-node model, relations (64)–(65). Therefore the response to noise of the two models can be summarized as follows: a slowing down which persist practically until $t_c$ for the saddle-node model, and a very slow “speed-up” behavior until $t_c$ for the DR-model. The sharp difference between the two models could perhaps be shown in real life conditions, and could be a way of proving (or disproving) the validity of some models of stick-slip behavior with laboratory experiments of solid friction.

We note that the geophysics literature describes many phenomena arising during the days or hours preceding strong earthquakes, that seems better in favor of the saddle-node model involving the short intermediate time. These phenomena have been attributed [17] to the emergence of electrical currents at the earth surface, arising from hard crystalline rocks near dip-slip faults, and caused by charge carriers, stress activated in the rocks deep below.
