Furthermore, Chua proposed a more immediate way to find the index of complexity of an elementary CA rule \(\mathcal{N}\) based on the so-called Boolean cubes. Given the truth table of \(\mathcal{N}\) of Table 1, its Boolean cube can be constructed by mapping the next state for each of the eight inputs onto the vertices of a cube, where adjacent vertices rows of the truth table with only one different input (see Fig. 1). Remarkably, the index of complexity \(\kappa\) of an elementary CA rule corresponds to the minimal number of planes necessary to divide the vertices with different states (either +1 or −1) in its Boolean cube.

The 256 elementary CA rules can be partitioned according to the ratio of 1s and -1s in their corresponding truth table. For example, rule 1 and rule 254 have a single 1 and a single -1 in their truth table, respectively: then we say that both are 1/7 rules. Obviously, there are five such categories which we list in the following, including also into brackets the number of globally-independent rules belonging to each of them: 0/8 rules (1), 1/7 rules (6), 2/6 rules (18), 3/5 rules (34), and 4/4 rules (29). Within each category we can define a number of equivalence classes composed by those rules whose Boolean cubes (or those of any of the rules globally-equivalent to them) coincide when rotated. For instance, rule 1 and rule 2 are not globally equivalent, however their Boolean cubes coincide when rotated. By following this criterion, the 88 globally-independent elementary CA rules can be classified into 14 distinct equivalence classes [6] called rotation groups and denoted by the symbol \(\mathcal{R}^{i}_{j}\), where the subscript \(j\) indicates the number of the category those rules belong (ie, 0 for 0/8 rules, 1 for 1/7 rules etc.) and the superscript \(i\) is a progressive number that distinguishes different equivalence classes having the same \(j\). Observe that

all and only the quasi-globally equivalent rules are contained in three rotation classes; namely, \(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{4}\), \(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{4}\), and \(\mathcal{R}^{7}_{4}\).

## 4. Global Dynamics of Elementary Cellular Automata

### 4.1. *Periodic orbits: attractors and Isles of Eden*

Since the states of an elementary Cellular Automaton are binary arrays with length \(L\), the total number of states is \(2^L\), when \(L\) is finite. Consequently, for any deterministic elementary CA rule, the temporal evolution starting from any initial state repeats itself after at most \(2^L\) iterations (actually, this conservative estimate can be greatly reduced because different states may be equivalent under rotation). This observation allows us to define several fundamental concepts:

**Definition 14.2.** Given an initial state \(\mathbf{x}^0\) and an elementary CA rule \(\mathcal{N}\), \(t\) is the smallest non-negative integer for which there exists a positive integer \(l\) such that

\[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{N}}^{l}(\mathbf{x}^{t})=\mathbf{x}^{t}. \tag{11}\]

The parameter \(t\) is called transient length and the set of all states \(\{\mathbf{x}^n,n\in[0,t-1]\}\) is called transient. The set of all states \(\{\mathbf{x}^n,n\in[t,l+t-1]\}\) is called periodic orbit (or limit cycle) originating from the initial state \(\mathbf{x}^0\) and \(l\) is called length of the orbit. The set of all transients of a given periodic orbit is called basin of attraction of the orbit.

The periodic orbits having a non-empty basin of attraction are called ‘attractors’; otherwise, they are called ‘Isles of Eden’. In general, an elementary CA rule has both attractors and Isles of Eden depending on the length of the Cellular Automaton and the initial state of the system. Nevertheless, some rules (called *conservative*) have only Isles of Eden, for any \(L\) and all initial states, and other rules (called *strictly-dissipative*) have no Isles of Eden at all. It is possible to prove that there are 6 conservative rules (4 of which are globally independent) and 28 strictly-dissipative rules (9 of which are globally independent). When \(L\) is finite, the basin of attraction of an attractor contains a finite number of states and some of which — called *Gardens of Eden* [21] — have no predecessors. Formally, a state \(\mathbf{x}^n\) is a Garden of Eden if \(\nexists\,\mathbf{x}^{n-1}\in\Sigma\) such that

xⁿ = ℳ_N(xⁿ⁻¹). An Isle of Eden have no Gardens of Eden, and its basin of attraction is empty, by definition.

Observe that a very similar approach, which was then used for an extensive study of Cellular Automata, was taken in [22].

A periodic orbit may contain two, or more, states that are equivalent under rotation. Therefore, it is necessary to introduce a measure of the number of states between a state of the periodic orbit and any rotated version of it.

Definition 14.3. The period τ of a periodic orbit is the smallest integer for which ∃σ ∈ ℤ such that x^τ = S^σ(x⁰), where S it the right shift operator and x^τ = ℳ_N^τ(x⁰). This implies that x_i^τ = x_{i+σ}⁰

for any cell x_i⁰ of x⁰, where the subscript refers to the position of the cell within the array.

The value of |σ| ≠ 0 can be conveniently used to make a further distinction among periodic orbits: if |σ| ≠ 0 then it is called Bernoulli-σ_τ periodic orbit; otherwise, it is called period-τ periodic orbit. For period-τ orbits l = τ, ie, the length of the orbit is equal to its period; for Bernoulli-σ_τ orbits l > τ, ie, the length of the orbit is greater than its period.

space-time pattern. It is obtained by converting each state of the Cellular Automaton into a black and white string, where cells whose state is −1 (resp. 1) are in white (resp. black). ### 4.2. Classification of CA rules according to the robust periodic orbits

In order to perform a complete analysis of the dynamics of a given elementary CA rule, it is necessary to study the evolution of all 2^L possible initial states, but when L is very large such process is unfeasible. Therefore, numerous authors, including both Chua and Wolfram, limit their analysis to the so-called ‘robust’ periodic orbits, which are those obtained by using initial states chosen randomly in the state space Σ. Remarkably, the observation of the dynamics of a single robust periodic orbits is usually sufficient to make some general statements about an CA rule.
