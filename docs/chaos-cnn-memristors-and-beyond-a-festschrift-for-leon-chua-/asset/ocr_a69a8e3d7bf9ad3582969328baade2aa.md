On the opposite side the other gives a mayor role to sensors, whose signals are strictly needed to initiate and modulate locomotion. In the case of some invertebrates, and in particular of the Stick insect, there is an evidence of no central coordination: legs are rather independent and only loosely connected to generate a coordinated motion: organisation among legs takes place at the level of the leg motor neurons, and no hierarchy is needed [44]. In the course of the last decades the former approach, referred to as the Central Pattern Generator paradigm, was the one that was found in the larger number of animal species taken into account. In fact, in many experiments on both invertebrates and vertebrates, basic rhythms are generated also in the case of fictive locomotion, i.e. in the absence of real movement of the locomotor organs, and so with no sensory feedback from these ones [79]. Of course, even if not needed, sensory feedback is involved in adapting the basic oscillations and their phases to environment conditions [90]. One classical example refers to the CPG induced initiation activity in Lampreys when they are disturbed mechanically moving their tail [101]. In general, there is evidence, from different points of view, that proprioceptive signals can entrain, modulate or even reset locomotion rhythmic activity [34,81]. On the other hand, one of the main characteristics and advantages of the CPG approach, which makes it feasible for robotic implementation, is that only a reduced number of control signals are required from higher brain regions to initiate, modulate or even switch among complex patterns of locomotor activity [54,63]. According to this paradigm, (a few) descending pathways modulate or switch among different pre-programmed patterns of neural activity, which reside, for example, in the case of insects, within the thoracic ganglia. Scanning the literature, it is possible to find that mathematical models of CPG can be derived at different levels of abstraction, from the very low level of modelling the single neural compartments, to modelling networks of interconnected neurons (referred to as connectionist models) [61,62], to more abstract coupled oscillatory networks, and even to networks of intrinsically non-oscillating dynamical units, that, suitably entrained, give rise to oscillatory dynamics [37]. This last approach is called Population dynamics or neural field [92] approach, since the aim is to model the macroscopic, field generated dynamics of neural assemblies.

The CNN-based CPG approach lies rather on the boundary between the connectionist and the Neural Field approach, since, from the one hand, the network parameters are primarily taken into consideration and are indeed the main actors of the emergence of the overall spatial-temporal dynamics generated within the neural lattice, which, on the other hand, is analyzed and exploited taking into account the characteristics of the generated field.

The approach starts considering legged locomotion as a complex spatio-temporal non linear dynamical system and subsequently implementing the CPG mathematical model on large scale, digitally programmable analog processors, known as Cellular Neural Networks. One of the main advantages of such architectures is that they were conceived as being tightly related to a reference hardware relatively easy to be realised at the VLSI level. This gives the possibility to obtain real time locomotion controllers. On the other hand, most of the approaches to locomotion pattern generation and control stop at the software simulation level or at the high level hardware implementation, using finite state machines on digital micro controllers. Rarely attention is focussed on hardware architectures serving as paradigms for the topographic implementation of CPGs. Moreover, analog topographic approaches become appealing in view of controlling a large number of degrees of freedom, like in the biological case. Here the controller is topographically distributed along with the actuator and the sensor network, in order to reduce the delay among the stimulus and the consequent reaction and increase the efficiency when, for example in the case of escape reactions, speed is of primary importance.

In the proposed approach it is clear that rhythmic activity is the emerging solution of a spatially extended nonlinear dynamical system, solution which depends more on the connection parameters (the neuron synapses) than on the particular neuron model taken into account. This is in accordance with a number of relevant formal results (see for example [68]). According to the connectionist approach, the initial effort has been to design a proper cell dynamics, able to capture few essential features of the neural dynamics, but suitable to be implemented within the CNN paradigm.
