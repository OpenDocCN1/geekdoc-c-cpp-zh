### 3.3. Extended phase-lag compensating coupling

To further improve the PLCC algorithm, we employ an idea from the DFC theory. In the original DFC algorithm (cf. [32]), the coupling law is analogous to that of the anticipating synchronization. To improve the DFC, [12] proposed an extended version of the original DFC, which incorporates in the feedback loop an information from many previous states of the system. This modification, known as an extended DFC (EDFC), makes possible the stabilization of high-periodic orbits with large positive Lyapunov exponents (cf. [33]). We use the idea of the EDFC to improve the performance of the PLCC algorithm [42]. We refer to this modification as an extended PLCC (EPLCC). The PLCC uses the single difference between the current state of the master system and the time-delayed state of the response system, whereas the EPLCC extends this perturbation to an infinite series of corresponding differences that are time-delayed by integer multiples of the delay τ. Again, we demonstrate the idea for two coupled unstable spirals

Ż₁ = (γ + iω)Z₁, (21a)
Ż₂ = (γ + iω)Z₂ + kS(t). (21b)

Now the EPLCC perturbation S(t) is governed by

S(t) = e^{iα}[Z₁(t) - Z₂(t - τ)] + Σ_{n=1}^{∞} Rⁿ e^{i(n+1)α}[Z₁(t - nτ) - Z₂(t - (n + 1)τ)], (22)

where R is a memory parameter that regulates the weight of the time-delayed information. We require -1 < R < 1 to guarantee the convergence of the infinite sum. The case of R = 0 corresponds to the PLCC considered in the previous section (cf. Eq. (19b)). Note that S(t) vanishes for any value of R when the system moves along the synchronization manifold, since the equality Z₁(t - nτ) = Z₂(t - (n + 1)τ) holds on the manifold for all n.

The phase lag of each term in the sum is compensated by the multiplier e^{i(n+1)α}, and the amplitude is weighted by the multiplier Rⁿ, such that the contribution of terms exponentially decreases with the increase of time delay. At the first look, Eq. (22) seems to be very complex. Fortunately, the infinite sum can be rewritten in an equivalent, recursive form

S(t) = e^{iα}[Z₁(t) - Z₂(t - τ) + RS(t - τ)] (23)

and implemented experimentally with the single delay line (cf. [12]). Now, the stability of the synchronized state is defined by variational equations

Δ̇(t) = (γ + iω)Δ(t) - kS(t - τ), (24a)
S(t) = e^{iα}[Δ(t) + RS(t - τ)], (24b)

where Δ(t) = Z₁(t) - Z₂(t - τ) describes the deviation from the synchronization manifold. This leads to the characteristic equation

(λ - γ)(e^{-i(α - ωτ)+λτ} - R) + k = 0. (25)

For R = 0, it coincides with Eq. (20) analyzed in the previous section. Here, as well as in the previous section, we set α = ωτ and analyze the simplified version of Eq. (25)

(λ - γ)(e^{λτ} - R) + k = 0. (26)

Unfortunately, the solution of Eq. (26) for R ≠ 0 cannot be expressed through the Lambert function. Although the analytical solution of this equation is not available, the boundaries of stability can be obtained analytically. The lower bound of stability k₁ = γ(1 - R) is deduced from Eq. (26) by substituting λ = 0. The upper bound of stability k₂ is defined by a Hopf bifurcation. Substituting λ = iΩ in Eq. (26) and denoting Ωτ = β, we obtain the dependence k₂ on τ in a parametric form

τ(β) = β(cos β - R) / (γ sin β),
k₂(β) = γ(cos β - R) + (β sin β)/τ(β). (27)

These boundaries are depicted in Fig. 8 for R = -0.3. We see that the EPLCC extends the stability region of the synchronization manifold in the (τ,k)-plane. The maximum prediction time can be obtained from Eqs. (27) in the limit β → 0. The necessary condition for stability of the synchronized state is

τ < τ_H = (1 - R)/γ. (28)

Now we consider an application of the EPLCC algorithm for chaotic systems. We extend Eqs. (10) for the EPLCC as follows:

$\dot{\mathbf r}_1=\mathbf f(\mathbf r_1)$ \tag{29}

$\dot{\mathbf r}_2=\mathbf f(\mathbf r_2)+k\mathbf S(t)$ \tag{30}

where the perturbation **S**(t) is governed by the recursive equation

$\mathbf S(t)=\mathbf K[\mathbf r_1(t)-\mathbf r_2(t-\tau)+R\mathbf S(t-\tau)]$ \tag{31}

and the coupling matrix **K** is defined by Eq. (16). In Fig. 9 we show the application of the EPLCC for the Rössler system. We see that the EPLCC allows us to prolong the prediction time to the value τ = 6.3, which is equal to the characteristic period of chaotic oscillations of the system.

## 4. Conclusion

Summing up, we have presented a review of the delayed feedback control (DFC) methods, including experimental implementations, verifications for theoretical models and various modifications. We have also discussed the analytical theories developed for this method, which are connected with the bifurcations of target orbits. We stress that the research on the global properties of the DFC algorithm as well as adaptive modifications are still weakly developed and require further attention of investigators. In Secs. 2.2 and 2.3, we have presented our recent achievements in this field.

Another way of application of a time-delay feedback is the forecasting chaotic dynamics of a master system via anticipating synchronization with a slave system. In Sec. 3, we have presented two algorithms based on a phase-lag compensating coupling (PLCC) and extended PLCC that allowed us to achieve a long term anticipation with the prediction time equal approximately to the characteristic period of chaotic oscillations. We hope that our findings in this field will stimulate the search for appropriate coupling laws in other problems of anticipating synchronization, e.g., to enhance the predictability of chaotic systems with unknown dynamical models [8] or excitable systems driven by random forcing [7, 9].

Although the DFC and anticipating synchronization algorithms are similar in the sense that both involve a time-delay feedback, however, the DFC is much more effective. In its usual form, it provides a stabilization of UPOs whose periods are comparable with the characteristic period of chaotic oscillations. In contrast, the usual anticipating synchronization algorithm is able to stabilize the anticipatory manifold only for small time-delays. To prolong this time we have designed a special phase-lag compensating coupling. The difference in efficacy of these two algorithms is due to different role of the delayed feedback in these algorithms. The DFC law (2) involves both the proportional feedback term $y(t)$ and the delay feedback term $y(t−\tau)$. The stabilization of UPOs is mainly caused by the proportional feedback. The delay term here is necessary to set to zero the control force on the desired UPO. In contrast, the feedback law $\mathbf K[\mathbf r_1−\mathbf r_2(t−\tau)]$ in the anticipating synchronization algorithm (10) does not contain the proportional feedback term. It involves only the delayed feedback term $\mathbf r_2(t−\tau)$ and only this term is responsible for the stabilization of the anticipatory manifold.
