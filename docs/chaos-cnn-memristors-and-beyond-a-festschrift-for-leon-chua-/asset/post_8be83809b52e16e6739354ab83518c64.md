This paper is to look at our “old studies” on a transmission line with “the newly won positions” and describe in greater detail the vibrations in “the zone of turbulence”. The paper consists of two parts. In the first we tried to clarify what is the ideal turbulence, in the form being laconic so as to keep the mathematical rigor (so we had after all to adduce some cumbersome formulas). In the second part, we show that ideal turbulence is typical for the idealized transmission line with boundary nonlinearity and, in particular, highlight parameter regions which correspond to the most developed turbulence, when the statistical properties of the vibrations are described by random processes.

## 2. Concept of Ideal Turbulence

The notion of *ideal turbulence* as spatial-temporal chaos in ideal media (without inherent resistance) was proposed by one of the authors back in 1983; originally, it was named *dry turbulence* by analogy with von Neumann’s dry water [12, 13]. Since then ideal turbulence is kept in the area of scientific interests of the authors. As a result, a mathematically rigorous definition of ideal turbulence and approaches to its study have been elaborated (see our works given in the bibliography).

A meaningful mathematical definition of turbulence can be given for dynamical systems on spaces of smooth or piecewise smooth functions.

Let \(\mathcal C, S^t\) be an infinite-dimensional dynamical system, where \(\mathcal C\) is the space of continuous piecewise smooth functions \(\varphi: D \to E\), \(D\) and \(E\) are regions of Euclidean spaces, \(t \in \mathbb Z^+\) or \(t \in \mathbb R^+\). The trajectory \(S^t[\varphi]\) of \(\varphi \in \mathcal C\) is understood as a sequence when \(t \in \mathbb Z^+\) and as a directedness when \(t \in \mathbb R^+\) (not to be confused with the orbit of \(\varphi\)).

The *spatial-temporal chaotization* in the dynamical system is perceived as an evolution of the functions \(\varphi \in \mathcal C\) under \(S^t\), which leads to that their behavior becomes more and more intricate and the limit states of the system are already impossible to describe with continuous functions (see Fig. 1). The attractor of the system, i.e. the set attracting all or almost all the trajectories, is not contained entirely in the phase space, and hence the system needs to be extended on a wider function space. The spaces of fractal and random functions are particularly appealing for use as such a wider space.

For a space containing fractal functions we take the space of multivalent functions \(\zeta: D \to E\), whose graphs are closed sets (such functions are called upper semicontinuous), and a metric is taken as follows

\[
\rho^{\Delta}(\zeta_1,\zeta_2)=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\min\left\{\varepsilon,\sup_{x\in D}\Delta\left(V^{\varepsilon}_{\zeta_1}(x),V^{\varepsilon}_{\zeta_2}(x)\right)\right\}.
\]

where \(\Delta(\cdot,\cdot)\) is the Hausdorff distance between sets, \(V^\varepsilon_\zeta(x)\subset \zeta(V^\varepsilon(x))\), and \(V^\varepsilon(x)\) is the \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhood of \(x\in D\).

Considering distributions of \(\xi\) in \(V_\varepsilon(x)\), we arrive at the metric

\[
\rho^\#(\xi_1,\xi_2)=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\min\left\{\varepsilon,\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^r}\sup_{x\in D^r}\sup_{y\in E^r}\left|F_{\xi_1}^{r,\varepsilon}(x,y)-F_{\xi_2}^{r,\varepsilon}(x,y)\right|\right\}.
\]

where \(D^r\) and \(E^r\) are the direct products of \(r\) copies of \(D\) and \(E\), and \(F_\xi^{r,\varepsilon}(x,y)\) is the averaging of the \(r\)-dimensional distribution function \(F_\xi^r(x,y)\) of \(\xi\) over the \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhood of \(x\in D^r\). The metric \(\rho^\#\), that might appear to be too complicated, actually has a simple probability meaning and is closely linked to the notion of weak convergence.

Let \(\mathcal C^\Delta\) and \(\mathcal C^\#\) be the completions of the phase space \(\mathcal C\) respectively with upper semicontinuous functions via the metric \(\rho^\Delta\) and with random and deterministic functions via the metric \(\rho^\#\).

In dynamical systems theory, to describe the asymptotic behavior of trajectories, the notion of \(\omega\)-limit set (i.e. the set of the limit points of a trajectory) is usually used. Reasoning from this, for an initial state \(\phi\in\mathcal C\), we will denote by \(\omega_\Delta[\phi]\) and \(\omega_\#[\phi]\) the sets of the limit points of the trajectory \(S^t[\phi]\) respectively in the spaces \(\mathcal C^\Delta\) and \(\mathcal C^\#\). Finally, we will write \(d(\cdot)\) for the Hausdorff dimension of the graph of a function.

**Definition.** The initial state \(\phi\in\mathcal C\) is said to generate *ideal turbulence* (IT) if the set \(\omega_\Delta[\phi]\) contains at least one “point” which is a fractal function, i.e. if there exists \(\zeta_*\in\omega_\Delta[\phi]\) such that \(d(\zeta_*)>\dim D\). The initial state \(\phi\in\mathcal C\) is said to generate *stochastic turbulence* (SIT) if the set \(\omega_\#[\phi]\) contains at least one “point” which is a random function (stochastic process). If there are a great many (in one sense or another, as the case requires) initial states which generate IT or SIT, then IT or respectively SIT is said to occur in the dynamical system.

The majority of evolutionary boundary value problems (BVPs) for partial differential equations induce dynamical systems of shifts along solutions. It is reasonable to say that *there occurs IT or SIT in a BVP* if such a turbulence occurs in the dynamical system associated with the BVP.

The modern-day mathematical apparatus of dynamical systems theory (particularly, one-dimensional) allows one to investigate in detail the properties of ideal turbulence in the BVPs which are reducible to continuous argument difference equations or differential-difference equations.

(see, for example, [16, 19, 23]). It has long been known that many BVPs for transmission lines fall into this class (see, for example, [1, 4]), but an effective study of such BVPs by the “reduction method” became possible only in the last 20–30 years owing to the progress in continuous argument difference equations theory.

In this regard a transmission line with Chua’s diode is among the representative examples.

## 3. Transmission Line with Chua’s Diode

We consider the BVP for a lossless transmission line with Chua’s diode

\[
v_x=-Li_t,\qquad i_x=-Cv_t,\qquad 0\le x\le l,\quad t\in\mathbb R^+, \tag{1}
\]
\[
v(0,t)=0,\qquad i(l,t)=G(v(l,t)-E-Ri(l,t)). \tag{2}
\]

Here \(v(x,t)\) and \(i(x,t)\) are the voltage and current along the line, \(L\), \(C\) and \(R\) are the inductance, capacitance and resistance per unit length, \(E\) is the voltage across a voltage source (in series with the diode), and \(G\) is the piecewise linear function

\[
G(u)=\begin{cases}m_0u,& |u|\le 1,\quad m_0<0,\\m_1u-(m_1-m_0)\operatorname{sgn}u,& |u|\ge 1,\quad m_1>0,\end{cases} \tag{3}
\]

that specifies the \(v\)–\(i\) characteristic of the diode. We assume that the solutions of the BVP are continuous and piecewise smooth, which forces the initial conditions

\[
v(x,0)=\varphi(x),\qquad i(x,0)=\psi(x),
\]
\[
\varphi,\psi:[0,l]\to\mathbb R, \tag{4}
\]

to be likewise continuous and piecewise smooth, and to be in agreement with the boundary conditions (2).

### 3.1. *Reducing to a difference equation*

At the condition \(v(0,t)=0\), the general solution of the BVP is of the form

\[
v(x,t)=\alpha\left(t-\frac{x}{\nu}\right)-\alpha\left(t+\frac{x}{\nu}\right),
\]
\[
i(x,t)=\frac{1}{Z}\left[\alpha\left(t-\frac{x}{\nu}\right)+\alpha\left(t+\frac{x}{\nu}\right)\right]. \tag{5}
\]

where \(\nu=\sqrt{1/LC}\), \(Z=\sqrt{L/C}\), and \(\alpha\) is an arbitrary function. With the new variables

\[
\beta(\tau)=\alpha(2l\tau/\nu),\qquad \tau=(\nu t/l-1)/2,\tag{6}
\]

the combination of (5) and the second condition (2) yields the continuous argument difference equation

\[
\beta(\tau+1)=f(\beta(\tau)),\qquad \tau\ge -1,\tag{7}
\]

with the nonlinearity given by the piecewise linear (single-valued or multivalent) map

\[
f:z\mapsto A_sz-B_s\quad\text{for }z\in I_s,\ s=0,\pm1,\tag{8}
\]

where

\[
A_s=-1+c_s,\qquad B_s=\frac{c_s}{2}\left[E+s\left(1-\frac{m_0}{m_s}\right)\right],\qquad c_s=\frac{2Z}{1/m_s+R+Z},\qquad m_{-1}=m_1,
\]
\[
I_0=\left\{z:\left|z-\frac E2\right|\le |\delta|\right\},\qquad
I_{\pm1}=\left\{z:\pm\left(z-\frac E2\right)>\delta\right\},\qquad
\delta=\frac{m_0Z}{c_0}.
\]

The initial states \(v_0(x)\) and \(i_0(x)\) determine the initial condition for Eq. (7):

\[
\left.\beta(\tau)\right|_{\tau\in[-1,0]}=\phi(\tau),
\]

\[
\phi(\tau)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\varphi(-\theta)+Z\psi(-\theta)}{2}, & -1\le \tau<-\dfrac12,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{-\varphi(\theta)+Z\psi(\theta)}{2}, & -\dfrac12\le \tau<0,
\end{cases}
\qquad \theta=l(1+2\tau),\tag{9}
\]

therewith \(\phi(\tau)\) has the property: \(\phi(0)=f(\phi(-1))\), which is a result of the consistency of the initial and boundary conditions for the BVP.

Equation (7) can be considered as the continuous family of the same maps (oscillators) \(z_n\mapsto z_{n+1}=f(z_n),\ z_0=\phi(\tau)\), “placed” at all points of the interval \([-1,0]\). Thus the dynamics of the lossless transmission line is reduced to the dynamics of a continuous family (continuous “lattice”) of uncoupled oscillators.\* Cumulative vibrations of these oscillators are described by the (infinite-dimensional) dynamical system

\[
S:\phi(t)\mapsto f(\phi(t)),\tag{10}
\]

generated by Eq. (7) on the space of continuous piecewise smooth initial functions \(\phi:[-1,0]\to\mathbb R\) such that \(\phi(0)=f(\phi(-1))\).

### 3.2. Managing map

It follows from the previous section that the behavior of solutions for the BVP (1), (2) is determined by the one-dimensional (1D) map \(f\), which we will refer to as the managing map. As is shown in [22] the dynamics of \(f\) can prove to be intricate only in the following two cases:

\[
(*)\quad -m_0Z\le E<1+m_0R;
\]
\[
(**)\quad -m_0R-\frac{m_0}{m_1}<E<-m_0Z.
\]

We restrict ourselves to the case (*). Then \(f\) is single-valued and has an invariant interval \(I\) that “takes up” all the points \(z\in\mathbb R\): for any \(z\in\mathbb R\), there exists an \(n\ge0\) such that \(f^n(z)\in I\). Moreover, \(f\) is equivalent on \(I\) to the two-segment map

\[
g(z)=
\begin{cases}
p(z-a)+1, & z\in[0,a],\\
q(1-z), & z\in(a,1],
\end{cases}
\qquad 0<p<1,\quad q>1,\quad a=1-1/q,\tag{11}
\]

with

\[
p=\frac{-1+(Z-R)m_1}{1+(Z+R)m_1},\qquad q=\frac{1-(Z-R)m_0}{1+(Z+R)m_0}.
\]

The two-parameter family of maps (11) has been studied closely enough, in particular, its main properties were given in our above-mentioned works of the 90s. Here we discuss in more detail those needed for further.
