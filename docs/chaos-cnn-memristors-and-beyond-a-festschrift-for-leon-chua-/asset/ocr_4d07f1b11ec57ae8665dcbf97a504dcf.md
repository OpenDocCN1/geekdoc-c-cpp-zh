try to develop several credible possibilities, consistent with what we know form experiment.

At this moment in time, the key challenge in the fourth frontier is to understand what kind of stable “solitons” exist in any of three field theory systems. The first is:

```
L = L_W + L_B + L_φl + L_φr − V,  (1)
```

where the first two terms are the usual terms in electroweak theory (EWT) for the usual W and B fields (as on page 56 of Taylor [21]):

```
L_W = −1/4 F_μν · F^μν,   for F_μν = ∂_μ W_ν − ∂_ν W_μ + g W_μ ∧ W_ν  (2)
```

```
L_B = −1/4 (∂_μ B_ν − ∂_ν B_μ)(∂^μ B^ν − ∂^ν B^μ),  (3)
```

where the new “Higgs field” φ is a two-by-two complex matrix which transforms like a spinor/contravariant spinor under Lorentz transforms (i.e. a twistor [22]), and where

```
L_φl = c_l | (∂_μ − i/2 g τ·W_μ − i/2 g' B_μ) φ |²  (4)
```

```
L_φr = c_r | (∂_μ − i/2 g' B_μ) φ^H |²  (5)
```

```
V = λ | I − φ^H φ |²  (6)
```

where c_l, c_r and λ are parameters, and where I define:

```
|M|² = Tr(M^H M),  (7)
```

where the superscript H is Schwinger’s notation for the Hermitian conjugate.

A second possibility is a simple variation of the first, in which Eqs. (4) and (5) are replaced by:

```
L_φl = c_l Re( (φ^H τ^μ (∂_μ − i/2 g τ·W_μ − i/2 g' B_μ) φ) )  (8)
```

```
L_φr = c_r Re( φ τ^μ (∂_μ − i/2 g' B_μ) φ^H )  (9)
```

It is relatively simple how to translate this kind of Lagrangian density into the actual PDE, the Lagrange-Euler equations, and to work out the energy density, the Hamiltonian, which is the starting point for most practical work. The concise review in [14] is quite adequate, but a more extensive discussion may be found in [23, 24] and in texts on classical field theory.

In these models, B is a real covariant vector, and W is a “triplet,” a set of three real vectors.

(Thus the wedge in Eq. (2) is simply one way of writing the usual cross product of two vectors in three dimensions; the cross product operates across the three unwritten indices, not across the subscript μ.) So far as I know, the only truly stable “solitons” ever discovered so far, for PDE obeying special relativity over three dimensions, are: Eq. (1) a model of a big star [25] with second order derivatives in the Lagrangian; Eq. (2) models like the Skyrme model [18, 26, 27] which include a field U which is strictly defined as an object in a “topologically interesting space.” Some researchers have argued [27] that it is impossible to have truly stable solitons in any field theory, like the two Lagrangians above, in which the fields are defined as ordinary real vectors or spinors without apriori topological constraints, and in which no second order derivatives appear. If truly stable “solitons” are proven to exist in either of the systems above, this by itself will be a fundamental advance in our knowledge. These systems would also seem to enforce the quantization of spin, which is just as important to what we see in nature as the quantization of electric charge.

A more radical possibility, obviously capable of generating two charges, is a closer relative of the system studied by Prasad and Sommerfield, to be discussed in Sec. 4.

My strong conjecture is that one of these Lagrangians, or an extension of one of them, will contain a stable soliton which matches what we know about the electron. In other words, it can explain what an electron is. The challenge before us here is to prove that (and, of course, to find the extensions if necessary).

The remainder of this chapter will address three questions: (1) why do we need a new explanation of what an electron is? (2) how can we meet this challenge, building on the existing literature [18, 26, 27], including other “soliton” models? (3) where could success in this challenge lead us?
