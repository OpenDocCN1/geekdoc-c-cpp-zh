## 3.5. Inductor

The inductor in Chua’s circuit can be realized by a physical inductor. Real inductors are wirewound components and have parasitic resistance associated with the windings of the coil. If this resistance is taken into account, then a physical realization of Chua’s circuit using a real inductor has a resistor R0 in series with the inductor. A problem with the early realizations of Chua’s circuit was that the inductors

![](img/039e7d3c23390584a66737067db8b75b_28_2.png)

Fig. 22. Implementation of a nonlinear resistor with four cross-coupled MOSFETs (reproduced from [25]).

had significant parasitic DC resistance which was not taken into account by the Chua’s circuit equations. As a result, there was an unexplained difference between the simulated and experimentally measured behavior. The differences can be explained away by including the parasitic resistance in the simulations, namely by simulating Chua’s oscillator rather than Chua’s circuit equations. Chua’s oscillator, shown in Fig. 7, is described by:

```
dV_C1/dt = G/C1 (V_C2 - V_C1) - 1/C1 f(V_C1)   (21)
dV_C2/dt = 1/C2 I_L - G/C2 (V_C2 - V_C1)   (22)
dI_L/dt = -1/L V_C2 - R0/L I_L   (23)
```

### 3.5.1. Gyrator-based inductor

In 1986, Prof. Leon Chua was preparing to give a keynote lecture entitled “The Sights and Sounds of Chaos” at a major international conference. He asked two of his doctoral students, Peter Kennedy and Ken Krieg, to build a portable system that would allow him to display trajectories from Chua’s circuit on a large screen and simultaneously produce sound in the audio range. The solution was the Attractor Display System (ADS) — a system of galvanometers mounted at right angles that, when driven by X and Y signals from Chua’s circuit, would deflect a laser beam, projecting a giant Lissajous figure on a distant screen. The problem was that the mirrors had to be operated open loop to obtain a sufficiently large image but their bandwidth was less than 500 Hz.

A Chua’s circuit with a center frequency of several kHz could not be used. The challenge was to build a slow Chua’s circuit. By scaling time, the sizes of the capacitances and inductances had to be increased. Finding a larger capacitance was relatively straightforward, but the required value of inductance would have necessitated a bulky inductor with a large parasitic resistance. The solution was to realize the inductance using a gyrator terminated with a capacitor. The resulting circuit is shown in Fig. 26. The diode clamp and the capacitor C₂ ensure that the inductor emulator operates correctly.

## 5. Three-element Chaotic Circuit

We stated at the outset that, in order to exhibit chaos, an autonomous circuit consisting of resistors, capacitors, and inductors must contain

- at least one nonlinear element,
- at least one locally active resistor, and
- at least three energy storage elements [15].

The four-element Chua's circuit contains three energy storage elements and a locally active nonlinear resistor. A memristor can exhibit simultaneously the characteristics of a nonlinear element, a locally active resistor, and an energy storage element. Therefore, it should be possible to build a chaotic circuit using just three elements: a memristor and two energy storage elements. Muthuswamy and Chua reported this in 2010 [24]. Their “simplest chaotic circuit” is shown in Fig. 36.

This circuit is described by two ordinary differential equations:

```
dV_C/dt = 1/C I_L  (32)
dI_L/dt = -1/L (V_C - V_M)  (33)
```

where V_M is the voltage across the memristor M, which is defined by:

```
V_M = β(X² - 1) I_M  (34)
dX/dt = I_M - α X - I_M X.  (35)
```

The memristance R(X) is given by:

```
R(X) = β(X² - 1).  (36)
```
