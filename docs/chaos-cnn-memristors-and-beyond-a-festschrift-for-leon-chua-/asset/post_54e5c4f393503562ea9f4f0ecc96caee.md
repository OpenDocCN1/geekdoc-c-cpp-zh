
## 8.4. *Global phase synchronization in an array of piecewise linear time-delay systems*

Even though the notion of CPS is well studied in low dimensional systems, there exist very little indepth studies in higher dimensional systems such as time-delay systems which are essentially infinite-dimensional in nature and often exhibit high-dimensional, highly non-phase-coherent hyperchaotic attractors with complex topological structure. Consequently, estimating phase explicitly to identify phase synchronization in such systems is quite difficult. Nevertheless, CPS has been recently demonstrated in two coupled piecewise linear and Mackey-Glass time-delay systems [34, 35, 42] by introducing a nonlinear transformation of the original dynamical variable to recast the original non-phase-coherent hyperchaotic attractors into smeared limit cycle-like attractors in order to facilitate the estimation of the phase variable using the available methods, and can be experimentally demonstrated as shown in the previous subsections. However, these investigations are carried out so far only in two coupled time-delay systems. In view of the widespread applications of CPS in ensembles of coupled oscillators, here we investigate the existence of global phase synchronization (GPS) in an array of unidirectionally coupled piecewise linear time-delay systems.

To verify GPS in a linear array, we consider an array of unidirectionally coupled piecewise linear time-delay systems with free end boundary conditions represented by the following systems of coupled nonlinear first order delay differential equations,

```
$$\dot{x}_1=-\beta x_1(t)+\alpha f(x_1(t-\tau)), \tag{37a}$$

$$\dot{x}_i=-\beta x_i(t)+\beta f(x_i(t-\tau)) + C[x_{i-1}(t)-x_i(t)], \tag{37b}$$
```

where i = 2, 3, ..., N. α, β are the system parameters, τ is the time-delay and C is the coupling strength. The nonlinear function f(x) is chosen to be a piecewise linear function (Eq. (33)). We have fixed the parameter values at β = 1.0, α = 1.2, τ = 15, p₁ = 0.8 and p₂ = 1.33 and the nonlinear parameter αᵢ of the response systems in the array are randomly chosen in the range αᵢ ∈ (1.2, 1.4) so that all the systems are effectively nonidentical. For our simulation, we have fixed the total number of oscillators in the array at N = 30.

The uncoupled systems exhibit a highly non-phase coherent chaotic attractor for the chosen parameter values, see Fig. 19. Here we note that the phase calculated directly from the original non-phase coherent chaotic attractor cannot yield monotonically increasing behavior as it has several closed loops, which also contribute to the phase information, other than the main center of rotation of the major part of the trajectories. To overcome the problem a nonlinear transformation can be introduced so as to rescale the original non-phase coherent chaotic attractor into a smeared limit cycle-like attractor with a single center of rotation. The transformation is performed by introducing a new state variable [34, 35].

```
$$\Delta z(t+\tau)=x(t)x(t+\hat{\tau})/x(t+\tau). \tag{38}$$
```

where τ̂ is the optimal value of the delay to be chosen in order to avoid any additional center of rotation. We use the nonlinear transformation to recast the original non-phase coherent chaotic attractor of all N = 30 oscillators to smeared limit cycle-like attractors. We have fixed the value of the optimal time-delay τ̂ in Eq. (38) as τ̂ = 1.6 for all the N oscillators. We have calculated the instantaneous phase of all the oscillators using the Poincaré section technique from their corresponding transformed attractors using the following equation

```
$$\phi_i(t)=2\pi k+2\pi\frac{t_i^i-t_k^i}{t_{k+1}^i-t_k^i}, \tag{39}$$
```

where tₖⁱ is the time of the kth crossing of the flow with the Poincaré section of the ith attractor.

To confirm the existence of GPS, we have calculated the average phase difference η(t), defined as

```
$$\eta(t)=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{N}(\phi_1-\phi_j). \tag{40}$$
```

The average phase difference (η(t)) for different values of C is shown in Fig. 26 as a function of time t. In the absence of the coupling (C = 0.0), the phases of all the oscillators evolve independently and hence their average phase difference increases linearly with time. Phase slips in the phase differences for small values of the coupling strength indicates that the oscillators are in the transition state to GPS. ## 9. Summary and Conclusions

In this article, we have given a brief overview of the nonlinear dynamics of a class of piecewise linear dynamical systems, modelling interesting nonlinear electronic circuits, describable by a system of two first order coupled nonlinear (piecewise linear) differential equations with external forcing as the constituent state equations. Equivalently, the dynamical equations can be rewritten as second order damped and forced linear differential equations with discontinuous right hand sides with one or two boundaries. These include the MLC circuit, variant MLC circuit, coupled MLC circuits, memristor based MLC circuit, forced negative conductance series LCR circuit with or without quasiperiodic forcing. We have also briefly considered the dynamics of certain piecewise linear time-delay systems and their coupled versions. All the above systems were shown to admit a very large variety of dynamical states, bifurcations and routes to chaos and synchronizations. The nonlinear dynamics underlying such piecewise linear systems continue to present challenging problems and fascinating results.
