
The parameters are fixed as a = 1.0, b = 1.2 and τ = 20. On increasing the noise intensity, σ, we found that complete synchronization is established between two piecewise linear time-delay systems driven by a common Gaussian noise and without any explicit coupling between them. To quantify the degree of synchronization, we define the synchronization error as follows

```
η = ⟨√(x₁(t) − x₂(t))² + (x₁(t − τ) − x₂(t − τ))²⟩ ,
```

where ⟨·⟩ denotes the time average and we have averaged the error for N = 200,000 time steps in our simulations. The synchronization error, η, is illustrated in Fig. 17 as a function of noise intensity σ. It is clear from this figure that the degree of synchronization error decreases for increasing of the noise intensity σ and for σ > 0.006, η approaches zero indicating that the two time-delay systems driven by a common Gaussian noise oscillate in complete synchrony with each other.

We have also estimated the synchronization error as functions of both the noise intensity σ and the time-delay τ to investigate whether a common noise is capable of inducing synchronization in a wide range of delay times. The synchronization error is depicted in Fig. 18 in the range of τ ∈ (2,30) and σ ∈ (0,0.01). This figure portrays that synchronization is achieved even for feeble values of noise intensity for small values of delay time, τ ∈ (2,3) and the critical value of σ required for synchronization increases with the delay time in the range τ ∈ (3,7) and for τ > 7, the two time-delay systems are synchronized for σ > 0.006 as represented in Fig. 17. This confirms that complete synchronization can also be induced in time-delay systems that are driven by a common noise in a wide range of time-delay without any explicit couplings between them.

## 6. Delay Effects on Synchronization in Complex Networks

Recent investigations on synchronization is focused on nontrivial effects of delay coupling due to a finite propagation time of information transfer in communication channels [48–55]. Delay coupling enhances the synchronizability of networks and interestingly leads to the emergence of a wide range of new collective behavior [48, 49]. Further, it has also been shown that connection delays can actually be conductive to synchronization, so that it is possible for the delayed system to synchronize where the undelayed system does not [48].

Pecora and Carroll introduced an elegant approach, the master stability function (MSF), to investigate the stability properties of a network of oscillators, which allows one to separate the local dynamics of the individual oscillators from the coupling matrix characterizing the topology of the underlying network [56]. Despite the flurry of independent research activities on both synchronization of delay coupled networks [48–50] and in analysing the stability of synchronization of a network of oscillators by exploiting the MSF [56–58], only a very few recent studies have been dealt with both MSF and delay coupled networks in probing the stability properties of the latter [51–55]. However, these reports limited their analysis either to phase oscillators or to maps except in [51], which reported the enhancement of neural synchrony by delay in two diffusively coupled Hindmarsh-Rose (HR) neurons and in Ref. [54], which analysed optically coupled semiconductor lasers modeled by Lang-Kobayashi equations using MSF. Nevertheless, the authors of [51] admitted that their analysis could not establish the stable synchronized states in the master stability surface of the network of delay coupled HR neurons. Hence, the master stability formalism has not yet been successfully exploited in understanding the stability properties of synchronization transitions in a delay coupled network synthesized from even simple low-dimensional dynamical systems. Further, the effect of delay on the synchronizability of such a network has not yet been investigated using the MSF.

### 6.1. Master stability formalism for delay coupled networks

We consider an arbitrary delay coupled network with N identical chaotic units in accordance with the MSF [56] as follows:

```
ẋᵢ(t) = f[xᵢ(t)] − σ ∑_{j=1}^{N} gᵢⱼ h[xⱼ(t−τ)], (15)
```

where xᵢ ∈ ℝᵐ, i = 1,…,N, f is a nonlinear function describing the dynamics of the individual units, σ is the overall coupling strength, G = gᵢⱼ is a N × N matrix, which determines the topology of an arbitrary network, h is a coupling function and τ is the coupling delay. The invariant synchronization manifold is assured by the zero row sum, that is ∑_{j=1}^{N} gᵢⱼ = 0 ∀ i, of each row of the connectivity matrix G. At first, we consider the arbitrary network as undirected such that G is symmetric with real eigenvalues and then we extend our analysis to directed networks, in which case G is asymmetric leading to complex eigenvalues. It has also to be noted that our results remains unaffected in either cases, as the imaginary parts of the complex eigenvalues has only a rotational effect [59], as we will discuss below in detail. If G is diagonalizable, then the stability of the synchronization manifold is governed by the eigenvalues of G and the following block diagonalized variational equation for the transverse modes

```
ξ̇(t) = Df[x(t)]ξ(t) − ε Dh[x(t−τ)]ξ(t−τ). (16)
```

Here, x(t) is the dynamics within the synchronization manifold, ξ(t) is the perturbation transverse to the synchronization manifold, which could be complex, Df[x(t)] is the Jacobian evaluated on the synchronization manifold and Dh[x(t−τ)] is an m × m matrix that determines which components of the oscillator are coupled. The normalized coupling parameter ε = σ γₖ, where γₖ is an eigenvalue of the coupling matrix G, k = 0,1,2,…,N−1. It is to be noted that the eigenvalue γₖ is more generic because of the arbitrary G and it could be the eigenvalue corresponding to any specific topology determined by G. Here, k = 0 corresponds to the synchronization manifold with γ₀ = 0, while all the other eigenvalues for k > 0 correspond to the transverse modes determining the stability of the synchronization manifold x(t). Thus, the synchronized state is stable for a given network topology, characterized by G, when all the eigenvalues of G for k > 0 are negative (or when all the transverse Lyapunov exponents of (16) are negative). Considering the fact that G could have complex eigenvalues arising from asymmetric couplings [59], the generic variational equation, that is the MSF, can be written as

```
ξ̇(t) = Df[x(t)]ξ(t) + (α + iβ) Dh[x(t−τ)]ξ(t−τ), (17)
```

where, α and β are the real and imaginary parts of the eigenvalue. Separating ξ(t) into the real part ξᵣ(t) and the imaginary part ξᵢ(t), we get

```
ξ̇ᵣ(t) = Df[x(t)]ξᵣ(t) + α Dh[x(t−τ)]ξᵣ(t−τ) − β Dh[x(t−τ)]ξᵢ(t−τ), (18a)
ξ̇ᵢ(t) = Df[x(t)]ξᵢ(t) + α Dh[x(t−τ)]ξᵢ(t−τ) + β Dh[x(t−τ)]ξᵣ(t−τ). (18b)
```

The maximal transverse Lyapunov exponent (λ_max) estimated from Eqs. (18) yields a complex surface in the (α, β) plane, namely the master stability surface of the considered network of oscillators. For a given value of the coupling strength σ and the delay time τ, one can locate a point in the complex surface and the sign of λ_max at that point determines the stability of the synchronization manifold.
