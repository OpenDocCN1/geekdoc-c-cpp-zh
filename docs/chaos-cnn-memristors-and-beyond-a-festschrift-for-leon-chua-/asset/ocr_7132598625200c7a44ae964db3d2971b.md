Repetitive episodes of NSVT can be broadly described as generating a bursting rhythm. Bursting, which can be defined as repeated periods of rapid activity interspersed by periods of quiescence, is displayed by many different excitable cell systems. In general, there are two ways that bursts can be generated in groups of cells: one or more cells in a population have the property of endogenous bursting, or else the bursting arises from interactions between cells. Endogenous bursting cells have intrinsic conductances that produce slow potential waves, which activate voltage-sensitive conductances resulting in a spike train of activity. Bursting activity can also be generated in networks of cells without endogenous bursters. Most network bursters depend on the activity of inhibitory connections on spontaneously firing cells: low frequency patterns are generated by alternate phases of rapid spontaneous firing and suppression of activity by activated neighbours [8, 27, 35]. Other variants use networks to average ionic currents or rely on a fixed delay excitation mechanism to generate bursting [28]. Bursting in cardiac disease states can also be thought of as either originating in from an endogenous bursting focus, or caused by emergent behaviour from large networks of coupled cells. Both focal and reentrant mechanisms have been reported during nonsustained atrial tachycardias, whereas reentrant mechanisms dominate in ventricular tachycardias and fibrillation. Further, diseases such as fibrosis and ischemia that promote reentrant arrhythmias introduce scars and regional changes in conductive properties of the muscle. The study of repetitive NSVT must therefore include an understanding of wave propagation through cardiac tissue with heterogeneities.

The propagation of electrical activity in cardiac muscle involves the interaction of different ion species across a variety of ion channels and diffusion of charge through a heterogeneous substrate with dynamically changing conductances. Despite complexity inherent in conduction at microscopic scales, the heart has been approximated as a continuous excitable media in many simulation studies. Excitable media can be broadly

defined as spatially distributed systems that display undamped propagating waves which annihilate only when they reach a boundary or collide with other waves. Excitable media are most naturally represented as partial differential equations (PDEs). The number of differential equations required to represent a cell in the system may be large (see [14] for a recent review). For example, the Iyer-Mazhari-Winslow model has 67 variables and hundreds of parameters. The rational for developing such complicated models is the assumption that inclusion of all known components is needed to achieve an accurate simulation of the behaviour. Although this modelling approach is justified in many situations, the high dimensionality of such models hinders intuitive understanding of the underlying dynamics. Simplified low dimensional PDE models that encompasses many characteristics of excitable tissue including threshold behaviour, refractoriness, and dispersion effects have been developed; the two variable FitzHugh Nagumo model first formulated in 1961 continues to be used to simulate cardiac dynamics, and more recently, the Fenton-Karma three variable model [15] and its four variable counterparts [7] which also preserves cardiac tissue properties including action potential upstroke velocity and shape, different time scales for depolarization and repolarization, and action potential duration and conduction velocity restitution curves.

Excitable media can also be simulated using discrete time models with simple update rules. These models consists of a grid of points where each point takes on any of a small set of discrete states, and there is set of rules that determine future states of points in the grid based on the present state of the grid. Discrete models, also called cellular automatons (CAs), [32] have been applied to a variety of physical processes. The CAs states have values that reflect the functional characteristics of each grid point as opposed to the value of particular variables. Space is discretized by mapping the continuous effects of diffusion to simple rules based on neighbourhood interaction. Cellular automata models offer a level of abstraction over differential equation based models which allows them to represent many different natural systems. Because of their simplicity cellular automata models have been widely used.
