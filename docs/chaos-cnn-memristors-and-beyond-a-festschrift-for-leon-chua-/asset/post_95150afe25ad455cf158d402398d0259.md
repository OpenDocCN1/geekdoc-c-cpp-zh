At this moment in time, the key challenge in the fourth frontier is to understand what kind of stable “solitons” exist in any of three field theory systems. The first is:

```
L = L_W + L_B + L_φl + L_φr − V,  (1)
```

where the first two terms are the usual terms in electroweak theory (EWT) for the usual W and B fields (as on page 56 of Taylor [21]):

```
L_W = −1/4 F_μν · F^μν,   for F_μν = ∂_μ W_ν − ∂_ν W_μ + g W_μ ∧ W_ν  (2)
```

```
L_B = −1/4 (∂_μ B_ν − ∂_ν B_μ)(∂^μ B^ν − ∂^ν B^μ),  (3)
```

where the new “Higgs field” φ is a two-by-two complex matrix which transforms like a spinor/contravariant spinor under Lorentz transforms (i.e. a twistor [22]), and where

```
L_φl = c_l | (∂_μ − i/2 g τ·W_μ − i/2 g' B_μ) φ |²  (4)
```

```
L_φr = c_r | (∂_μ − i/2 g' B_μ) φ^H |²  (5)
```

```
V = λ | I − φ^H φ |²  (6)
```

where c_l, c_r and λ are parameters, and where I define:

```
|M|² = Tr(M^H M),  (7)
```

where the superscript H is Schwinger’s notation for the Hermitian conjugate.

A second possibility is a simple variation of the first, in which Eqs. (4) and (5) are replaced by:

```
L_φl = c_l Re( (φ^H τ^μ (∂_μ − i/2 g τ·W_μ − i/2 g' B_μ) φ) )  (8)
```

```
L_φr = c_r Re( φ τ^μ (∂_μ − i/2 g' B_μ) φ^H )  (9)
```

It is relatively simple how to translate this kind of Lagrangian density into the actual PDE, the Lagrange-Euler equations, and to work out the energy density, the Hamiltonian, which is the starting point for most practical work. The concise review in [14] is quite adequate, but a more extensive discussion may be found in [23, 24] and in texts on classical field theory.

In these models, B is a real covariant vector, and W is a “triplet,” a set of three real vectors.

(Thus the wedge in Eq. (2) is simply one way of writing the usual cross product of two vectors in three dimensions; the cross product operates across the three unwritten indices, not across the subscript μ.) So far as I know, the only truly stable “solitons” ever discovered so far, for PDE obeying special relativity over three dimensions, are: Eq. (1) a model of a big star [25] with second order derivatives in the Lagrangian; Eq. (2) models like the Skyrme model [18, 26, 27] which include a field U which is strictly defined as an object in a “topologically interesting space.” Some researchers have argued [27] that it is impossible to have truly stable solitons in any field theory, like the two Lagrangians above, in which the fields are defined as ordinary real vectors or spinors without apriori topological constraints, and in which no second order derivatives appear. If truly stable “solitons” are proven to exist in either of the systems above, this by itself will be a fundamental advance in our knowledge. These systems would also seem to enforce the quantization of spin, which is just as important to what we see in nature as the quantization of electric charge.

A more radical possibility, obviously capable of generating two charges, is a closer relative of the system studied by Prasad and Sommerfield, to be discussed in Sec. 4.

My strong conjecture is that one of these Lagrangians, or an extension of one of them, will contain a stable soliton which matches what we know about the electron. In other words, it can explain what an electron is. The challenge before us here is to prove that (and, of course, to find the extensions if necessary).

The remainder of this chapter will address three questions: (1) why do we need a new explanation of what an electron is? (2) how can we meet this challenge, building on the existing literature [18, 26, 27], including other “soliton” models? (3) where could success in this challenge lead us?

## 3. Why Seek A New Model of the Electron?

Most graduate schools now teach us that we already have a perfectly good model of an electron, the model given in quantum electrodynamics (QED) [13,14]. It is often said that QED is the most perfectly proven model of physics ever developed, with predictions tested across a wide range of phenomena

in electronics and photonics, accurate to sixteen decimal places. This is not quite the whole story [19, 21], but it is certainly a good starting point. “Maxwell’s Laws” are now seen as one part of QED. Physicists have developed many other ways to try to formulate quantum field theories, but for the practical, empirical challenges of electronics and photonics, the canonical version of QED developed by Schwinger et al. [13,14], based on operator fields, is still the starting point.

According to QED, the electron is a perfect point particle, with zero radius — unlike the proton or the neutron, which have a measurable radius of about one femtometer, which is also called a “Fermi length.” That is a 10⁻¹⁵ meters, a millionth of a nanometer, or one hundred-thousandth of an Angstrom unit; in other words, it is about a hundred-thousandth of the size of an atom. But so far as we know today, the radius of the electron is exactly zero, just as QED assumes; recent experiments [28] claim to show an upper bound of 10⁻²² meters for the radius of the electron.

Why would we want a better model than that? There are at least three reasons.

To begin with, QED is difficult to calculate with when we have millions of electrons in a system. There is some hope that a new model would allow new approaches to many-body computation, like those which have begun to emerge in empirical nuclear physics [18]. This may seem impossible, since the electron is a fermion; however, we now know that compound systems made up of bosons can have emergent statistics which match those of a fermion [26, 27]. Still, the possibility of faster computation for systems of electrons has yet to be proven, and is far from obvious. First we must show we can model the properties of a single electron as proposed here.

Second, a new approach is essential to someday getting back to Einstein’s core vision of modeling reality through well-defined continuous PDE. That is what really motivates me here [19, 20], but the details are beyond the scope of this paper.

Third, there are certain problems with the QED model of the electron which people have basically repressed in their minds. These involve the radius of the electron.

We know very well that the electron has a charge which generates a Coulomb field. We know how to calculate the energy of that Coulomb field. If we assume that the charge is located at the origin, we can easily compute the total mass-energy

in the Coulomb field, taking the integral of the Coulomb energy from some radius r on out to infinity. At a certain radius, r, the total mass-energy of the Coulomb field equals the total observed mass-energy of the electron. That is called the Thompson radius of the electron. It is just under three femtometers — much, much larger than the “upper limit” of 10⁻²² meters!

How does QED explain this paradox — the paradox of having an electron which has a mass much less than the mass of one part of its field?

In essence, QED does assume that the Coulomb field of the electron has infinite positive energy, as we get when we send the radius r to zero. This by itself would lead to absurd predictions, since we know the electron does not behave as if it has infinite mass. However, “to fix the predictions,” QED assumes an infinite negative offset, δM, located at exactly at the center of the electron. This is commonly presented as “part of the renormalization.” People tend to think “this is just part of how we do the calculations.” If we ask where this infinite negative energy comes from, perhaps they think subconsciously: “This is just a gift from God or from Schwinger to make our predictions work out.” But in fact, what we use to make predictions is part of the theory itself, when it makes our predictions very different from what they otherwise would be.

In summary, the paradox of mass-energy in the Coulomb field greater than the mass of the electron itself gives us only two choices of possible explanation (leaving aside anachronistic alternatives like abandoning special relativity). As in QED, we may assume the presence of negative mass-energy inside every electron, which would call for us to understand much better how negative energy can occur in nature. It would be very exciting if negative energy really did exist, if we could understand it better. But here I am advocating more attention to a different approach to explanation, until and unless we find some credible empirical evidence for negative energy. I am proposing that we model the electron as a “soliton,” as a pattern of fields in which other fields besides the Coulomb field exist, and affect the overall energy even beyond three femtometers, and in which energy density is strictly positive definite.
