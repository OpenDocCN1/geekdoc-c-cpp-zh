Interdisciplinary research is essential today where many problems simply cannot be solved with expertise in only one technical area. The opportunities to interact with many people of different disciplines, experience and culture at NOEL trained me well for that. Today, I work with teams consisting of mathematicians, physicists, electrical engineers, computer scientists and software programmers on a daily basis.

Finally, doing research is akin to playing a chess game. There are many possible paths, but only a few (or only one) give you the result that you are looking for, and many paths lead you to wrong results. Since we have a limited amount of time, we can only pursue a subset of paths. When we first started in our careers we did not know which were the best paths to take and thus many of our trials led to dead ends. But as we mature intellectually and are more experienced, we start to know which paths to take and which ones to avoid. But sometimes we are overconfident in our skills and avoid paths that we feel are too trivial, are too operoseʳ or are pointing to the wrong direction. But these paths could lead to new insights and perhaps the solution we are looking for. One lesson that Leon taught me is that sometimes we need to take an approach that initially looks unrewarding or has been tried before. We might be surprised where it could lead.

# 8. Finally it is Time to Go

After I graduated in 1995, I remained as a postdoc at NOEL for the first part of 1996. When I received several job offers including an offer to join the Mathematical Sciences department in IBM Research as a Postdoctoral Fellow,ᵘ I asked Leon for his advice. Recall that only a few years earlier, IBM had major losses and had been written off for dead. Leon showed me an article in Business Week describing how Lou Gerstner is turning the company aroundᵛ and he told me that it is a good company to work for. I followed his advice and I believe that was the right decision to make.
