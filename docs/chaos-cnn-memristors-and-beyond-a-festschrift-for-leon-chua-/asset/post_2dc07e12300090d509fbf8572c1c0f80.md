The surface $S$ splits the 3D phase space $(u,v,\theta)$ into two subspaces, the one for which $(\xi u+\theta+\ln(v))$ is positive and the one for which it is negative. In the large $\gamma$ limit, the dominant component of the time derivative of the 3D vector $(u,v,\theta)$ is $\dot v$. The negative sign on the r.h.s. of Eq. This shows that the slow manifold is everywhere attracting in this limit $\gamma$ large. Therefore the classical “scenario” for van der Pol-type relaxation oscillation with an ejection out of a repelling SM cannot hold for DR equations. We show later that in the DR case, the 3D flow escapes from $S$ because the 2D flow trajectory goes to infinity in finite time, see Figs. 9 and 10.

## 3.1.2. *Slip step*

During the slip, both $v$ and $\dot v$ are “large”, of order $\gamma$ and $\gamma^2$ respectively. Neglecting $(-1)$ on the right-hand side of Eq. (37) allows to eliminate the large parameter $\gamma$ from the equations by rescaling and addition. This is done by putting $v=\gamma V(T)$, $t=T/\gamma$, $u=U(T)+c_u\ln(\gamma)$ and $\theta=\Theta(T)+c_\theta\ln(\gamma)$. The quantities denoted as $T$, $V$, $\Theta$ and $U$ are of order 1 with respect to $\gamma$ as well as the constants $c_\theta$, $c_u$. Setting to zero the part independent of $T$ in the equations derived from (38) and (39) one finds two algebraic equations for $c_\theta$ and $c_u$ with the solution

$$\begin{cases} c_\theta=-(1+\epsilon),\\ c_u=\dfrac{\epsilon}{\xi}.\end{cases} \tag{46}$$

The functions $U(T),V(T)$ and $\Theta(T)$ are solutions of a set of three ODE’s free of the large parameter $\gamma$

$$\begin{cases} U_T=V,\\ V_T=-\left[U+\dfrac{1}{\xi}(\Theta+\ln(V))\right],\\ \Theta_T=-V[\Theta+(1+\epsilon)\ln(V)], \end{cases} \tag{47}$$

where $X_T$ is for $\dfrac{dX}{dT}$. This predicts that the slip lasts a (short) time $\delta t_{eq}$ of order $1/\gamma$ although the sticking lasts a time independent of $\gamma$. This scaling law is in excellent agreement with the numerical study of the full DR equations for $\gamma$ large.

## 3.1.3. *Matching slow and fast solutions*

There remains to interpolate between the stick and slip parts of the dynamics. To the best of our knowledge, this matching has not been done before for DR-like equations, in relation with the idea of a finite singularity of the slow dynamics. According to matching theory, the solution in the interpolation domain has to satisfy the set of equations common to the two phases, slow and fast,

$$\dot u=v, \tag{49}$$

together with Eqs. (39) and (44). The integral curves of (39) and (49) are solutions of the single linear ODE

$$\dfrac{d\theta}{du}=\epsilon\theta+(1+\epsilon)\xi u, \tag{50}$$

that gives

$$\theta(u)=ce^{\epsilon u}-\dfrac{(1+\epsilon)\xi}{\epsilon^2}(1+\epsilon u), \tag{51}$$

with $c$ arbitrary constant. In our problem this expression agrees well with the numerics, with $c$ calculated from a particular value of $(u,\theta)$ belonging to the matching region, see the discussion below concerning the Figs. 9(a), 9(b) and (8). The general time dependent problem in the matching region may be written as $\dot u=\exp(\Phi(u))$ with

$$\Phi(u)=-ce^{\epsilon u}+\dfrac{\xi}{\epsilon}u+\dfrac{(1+\epsilon)\xi}{\epsilon^2}. \tag{52}$$

Fig. 9. (a) $v(t)/\gamma$ for the 2D and 3D flows (blue and purple curves respectively) (b) Phase space $(u,\theta)$. The 3D limit cycle (closed dashed blue curve) is superimposed with the 2D flow (solid portion) on the SM. The orange and green portions correspond to the two branches of matching solutions, Eq. The solution reads

$$t-t_0=\int_{u(t_0)}^{u}du'\exp\left(-\Phi(u')\right), \tag{53}$$

where $u(t_0)=u_0$. It yields a singularity at finite time in the forward time direction if $\int_{u_0}^{u}du'\exp(-\Phi(u'))$ is a converging integral, which requires $c$ to be negative. This occurs at the end of the stick interval, as illustrated in Fig. 9(a), but their is no finite time singularity in the full DR system. There the divergence of the singularity is replaced by a large back and forth swing ending with a slowing down and a return to the SM. A physical consequence of this behavior is that, before the catastrophe $u$ is negative and positive at the end of it, changing the strain from dilative to compressive. This does not seem to be what happens in real earthquakes, where the strain remains dilative after the quake and so can generate secondary quakes weeks after a major slide. This points toward a large effect of friction during the quake itself. In the DR model this means that $\xi$ should be rather small to enhance friction with respect to inertia: a large $\xi$ means that inertia is dominant, which means that an almost complete back and forth swing is needed to return to a small velocity at the end of the fast phase. By decreasing $\xi$ one increases the effect of friction during the fast phase. This makes possible to end it with $u$ still positive (as observed) whilst the friction term $\ln(v)/\xi$ is increased in Eq. (30). This puts physical constraints on the DR model to represent large earthquakes, particularly on the value of $\xi$ (in real life it is constrained by the ratio of the strain before and after the quake, a measurable quantity in principle).

Note that the solution (52) was derived within the adiabatic approximation (42), which limits its validity to a domain we are going to precise. On the surface $S$, the time derivative of $v=\exp(-\xi u-\theta)$ writes $\dot v=-e^{-2(\xi u+\theta)}[\xi+\epsilon\theta+(1+\epsilon)\xi u]$ by using Eq. (49) and the second equation (45). Therefore in the space $(u,\theta)$ the straight line of Cartesian equation

$$\epsilon\theta(u)=-(1+\epsilon)\xi u-\xi. \tag{54}$$

splits the SM into a part such that trajectories crossing this line tend to still lower values of $\dot v$, and so follow even more closely the SM, although when the crossing is in the other direction, $\dot v$ tends to increase, as well as $v$ itself (always positive) and so ends up with the finite time divergence just described. A rough estimate of condition (42) limits the 2D flow to the domain located above the line

$$\theta(u)=-\xi u-\ln(\gamma). \tag{55}$$

These results are illustrated in Fig. 9(b). In this figure the closed curve (blue-dashed) displays the limit cycle (3D flow) evolving counterclockwise in the phase space $(u,\theta)$. Superimposed onto this curve, we plot the stick episode (solid red portion), and the matching analytical solutions (51) (orange

and green curves). The adiabatic approximation domain lies above the dashed straight line, Eq. (55). The two pieces of analytical solutions fit well the two intermediate regimes, they are calculated with two distinct values of $c$ (derived from Eq. (51) and using values of $(u,\theta)$ before and after the catastrophe respectively). The orange curve which belongs to the SM, matches the stick regime and the fast one, then it diverges on the surface $S$, while the limit cycle escapes from $S$. This happens when the adiabatic approximation fails. The escape from the SM of the 3D flow in the fast regime is visible on Fig. 10.

The physics of the oscillation can be understood as follows. The acceleration of the block at the end of the stick phase is unstable because it lowers the friction and so feeds itself. The transition to the “sliding” fast regime occurs when the acceleration becomes big enough to make the inertia of the block relevant. In this fast regime the dynamical system gets one more dimension (in other terms the effect of inertia increases by one the number of coupled ODE’s). If one neglects in this fast regime the friction, the dynamics is the one of a harmonic oscillator making a half swing while its velocity keeps the same sign. When this oscillator has slowed down enough to yield back its energy to the potential energy of the spring, its velocity returns to small values and friction becomes so large that the SM is reached again. This could explain that, in earthquakes, a finite fraction of the initial elastic energy is not dissipated in the event itself, because part of it remains as potential energy after the large scale pendulum-like motion. In this model, the spring is under dilative strain before the slip episode and under compressive strain at the end of it.

## 3.2. *Lyapunov analysis along the trajectory*

We have noted above that the SM seems to be everywhere attractive, taking argument of the negative sign on the right hand side of Eq. (38). This statement is invalid for the close vicinity of the SM, more precisely at distance of order $1/\gamma^2$ from the SM. In the very slow part of the trajectory, we may investigate how attractive is the SM by performing the linear stability analysis of the flow. Along a peculiar trajectory $(u_0(t),\ln(v_0(t)),\theta_0(t))$ the Jacobian matrix is

$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\gamma^2 & -\dfrac{\gamma^2}{\xi v_0} & -\dfrac{\gamma^2}{\xi} \\ 0 & -A & -v_0 \end{pmatrix}.$$ (56)

where

$$A=\theta_0+(1+\epsilon)(1+\ln(v_0)).$$ (57)

The eigenvalues of the matrix (56) are solutions of the equation

$$\lambda^3+\left(\dfrac{\gamma^2}{\xi v_0}+v_0\right)\lambda^2+\gamma^2\left(\dfrac{1-A}{\xi}+1\right)\lambda+\gamma^2v_0=0.$$ (58)

In the large $\gamma$ limit, the two eigenvalues are

$$\lambda(t)=-B(t)\pm\sqrt{B(t)^2-\xi v_0(t)^2},$$ (59)

with

$$B(t)=\dfrac{1}{2}v_0(1-A+\xi).$$ (60)

The three eigenvalues are drawn as functions of time in Fig. 11, for the flow corresponding to Fig. 7(a), or 9(b) or (10). One eigenvalue (curve a) is negative all along the trajectory. The two other eigenvalues are real in the first part of the trajectory, and become complex conjugate at time about $t_c\simeq 4.6$, see Fig. 7(b). The real parts are the red and blue curves in (b), the imaginary parts are drawn in Fig. 7(c). The real part crosses zero at time $t\sim t_c-2.3$, with an eigenfrequency $\omega$ of order unity.
