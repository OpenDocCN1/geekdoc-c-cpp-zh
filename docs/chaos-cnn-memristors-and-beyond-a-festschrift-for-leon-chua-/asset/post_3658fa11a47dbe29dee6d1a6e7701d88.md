Fig. 2. Leading Floquet multipliers of unstable periodic orbits arising from different bifurcations: (a) period doubling, (b) tangent or subcritical Hopf, and (c) Neimark-Sacker (discrete Hopf) bifurcations. The unit circle defines the region of stability.

The theory of DFC is difficult because the delayed feedback induces an infinite number of degrees of freedom. Even linear analysis of such systems is complicated due to the infinite number of Floquet exponents (FEs) characterizing the stability of controlled orbits. Nevertheless, some analytical approaches have been developed in vicinity to various bifurcations of periodic orbits, such as the period doubling bifurcation [18,35], the subcritical Hopf bifurcation [34,37,38] and the Neimark-Sacker (discrete Hopf) bifurcation [44]. The leading Floquet multipliers (FMs) of UPOs arising from these bifurcations are shown in Fig. 2. Note that all three types of orbits have different topological properties. The orbits with the negative real multiplier flip their neighborhood during one turn. The orbits with the complex conjugate pair of the multipliers have a finite torsion, and the orbits with the positive real multiplier have no torsion. Below we discuss separately the theoretical treatments of the DFC algorithm for these three different types of UPOs.

The orbits with the negative real multiplier [Fig. 2(a)] arise from a period-doubling bifurcation and are typical, for example, for the Rössler system. The mechanism of stabilization of such orbits by delayed feedback is well understood [18,35]. In [35] there has been derived the relationship between the FEs of periodic orbits controlled by proportional and delayed feedback algorithms. Then it is enough to know the dependence of the Floquet exponents on the control gain for the proportional feedback control algorithm. Similar dependence for the EDFC is obtained from a transcendental equation and we do not need to solve the delay differential equations.

An analytical treatment of the delayed feedback controlled UPOs with the complex conjugate pair of leading FMs [Fig. 2(c)] has been developed in [44]. Such orbits arise from a Neimark-Sacker (discrete Hopf) bifurcation and cannot appear in low-dimensional chaotic attractors. However, such orbits may appear in low-dimensional non-chaotic systems, e.g. in a weakly nonlinear van der Pol oscillator subjected to a periodic force and delayed feedback control. For this system, we have derived a simplified averaged equation and obtained analytical expressions defining the region of stabilization of the controlled system as well as an optimal value of the control gain.

The orbits with the positive real multiplier [Fig. 2(b)] come, for example, from a tangent or a subcritical Hopf bifurcation and are typical, for instance, for the Lorenz system. They satisfy the so-called odd number limitation, which states that any UPOs with an odd number of real Floquet multipliers greater than unity can never be stabilized by any DFC technique. During the last fifteen years, this limitation has been intensively discussed in the literature. The history of the limitation is dramatic. This statement was first proven by Ushio [52] for discrete time systems. [18] and [27] proved the same limitation for the continuous time DFC, and then this proof was extended for the EDFC [29]. It was commonly accepted that it is impossible to overcome this inherent limitation. However, [11] have shown by a simple example that this limitation does not hold in general for autonomous systems. Nevertheless, it remains valid for non-autonomous systems.

To overcome the odd number limitation a counterintuitive idea based on an unstable controller has been proposed [34,37,38]. By including an additional unstable mode into the control loop one artificially enlarges the set of real multipliers greater than unity to an even number. The performance of such an unstable controller has been numerically demonstrated for the Lorenz system [34] as a representative of systems with torsion-free UPOs. Later on, it has been realized that the unstable DFC allows an analytical treatment if a system is close to a subcritical Hopf bifurcation. First, such a treatment has been performed for a simple nonlinear second order electronic circuit modeling a subcritical Hopf bifurcation [38]. Then this approach has been extended for wider class of dynamical systems with an arbitrary large phase space dimension [37].

In the next two sections, we discuss in more details our recent achievements in the theory of the DFC. Section 2.2 is devoted to the modification of the DFC algorithm that improves it global properties. The ergodicity — an universal feature of chaotic systems is invoked in order to enlarge the basin of attraction of the stabilized orbit. In Sec. 2.3, we describe an adaptive modification of the DFC algorithm with the continuously varying time delay that provides an asymptotic convergence of the delay time to the period of the target UPO.

## 2.2. Improving the global properties of the DFC

The standard tool for discussing the control performance of DFC systems consists in linear stability analysis. However, even if such a local analysis predicts stable states, experimental success is not guaranteed because the control performance may strongly depend on initial conditions. The analysis of global properties of DFC systems, such as basins of attraction of stabilized orbits, is much more complicated problem. There exists virtually no systematic investigation of time-delayed feedback control beyond the linear regime. Numerical analysis of particular systems shows that the DFC algorithm can form incredibly complex basins of attraction [56,57].

To improve the global properties of the linear DFC algorithm several modifications have been proposed. A first heuristic idea has been suggested in the original paper [32]. It has been shown that limiting the size of the control force by a simple cutoff increases a basin of attraction of the stabilized orbit. This idea has proven itself in a number of chaotic systems, and now it is widely used in experimental implementations of DFC method. An alternative two-step DFC algorithm has been proposed in [50]. In [17], the authors have developed a theory which stated that depending on the type of transition at the control boundary there appear basins of attraction of different size; for a continuous transition the basin of attraction is large, while for a discontinuous transition it is small. Finally, a nonlinear DFC for systems close to a subcritical Hopf bifurcation has been proposed in [16]. It has been shown that the basin of attraction can be enlarged considerably by coupling control forces through the phase of the signal.

Unfortunately, the above-proposed nonlinear DFC schemes are not universal. In recent papers [41,42], we have elaborated a DFC algorithm with the improved global properties by invoking an ergodicity — the universal feature of chaotic systems. The ergodicity means the fact that a trajectory of any chaotic system visits a neighborhood of each periodic orbit with finite probability. The idea of using ergodicity in chaos control research was first formulated in the seminal paper by Ott, Grebogy, and Yorke (OGY) [30] and has been employed in their OGY control algorithm. However, a straightforward implementation of ergodicity in DFC schemes has not been considered. Our main motivation was to fill this gap and adapt the OGY ideas for DFC algorithm. The specific point of our problem is that the DFC force increases the phase space dimension of the closed-loop system. As well as in the OGY algorithm, we do not perturb the system until it comes in a small neighborhood of the desired orbit. Using a scalar observable, we have developed a technique which allows us to evaluate a moment when the state of the free time-delay system approaches the target orbit. At this moment, we activate the DFC force and stabilize the target. Note that the problem of evaluating the moment when the state of the free system approaches the target orbit has to be considered in an infinite-dimensional phase space, since the DFC force increases the phase space dimension.

To demonstrate the algorithm let us consider the Rössler equations [45] subjected to DFC force

```
ẋ = −y − z,          (1a)
ẏ = x + a y − K Δs(t),    (1b)
ż = b + z(x − c).       (1c)
```

Here x, y, z are the dynamic variables of the Rössler system. The parameters a = 0.2, b = 0.2 and c = 5.7 are chosen such that the system exhibits chaotic behavior. We suppose that s(t) = y(t) is an observable and the DFC perturbation K Δs(t) is applied only to the second equation of the Rössler system. Here K is the feedback gain and Δs(t) denotes the difference of the observable between the current state, and the state delayed by the period τ of UPO:

```
Δs(t) = s(t) − s(t − τ) = y(t) − y(t − τ).    (2)
```

Numerical analysis shows that the basins of attraction of the period-1 and period-2 UPOs of the Rössler system are rather large. The straightforward application of the original DFC algorithm (without waiting until the system approaches the desired orbit) is successful for these orbits at any initial conditions placed on the strange attractor. However, this is not the case for the period-3 UPOs. The free (K = 0) Rössler system has two period-3 UPOs with slightly different periods τ = 17.51 and τ = 17.60.
