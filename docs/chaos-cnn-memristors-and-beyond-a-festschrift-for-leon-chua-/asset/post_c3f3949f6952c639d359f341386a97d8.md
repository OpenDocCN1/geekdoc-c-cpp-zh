## 1. The Local Activity Principle and the Emergence of Complexity

According to several prominent authors, including Stephen Hawking, a main part of 21st century science will be on complexity research. The intuitive idea is that global patterns and structures emerge from locally interacting elements like atoms in laser beams, molecules in chemical reactions, proteins in cells, cells in organs, neurons in brains, agents in markets etc. by self-organization [28,30]. But what is the cause of self-organization? Complexity phenomena have been reported from many disciplines (e.g. biology, chemistry, ecology, physics, sociology, economy etc.) and analyzed from various perspectives such as Schrödinger’s order from disorder [44], Prigogine’s dissipative structure [42], Haken’s synergetics [14], Langton’s edge of chaos [21] etc. But concepts of complexity are often based on examples or metaphors only. We argue for a mathematically precise and rigorous definition of local activity as the cause of self-organizing complexity [10] which can be tested in an explicit and constructive manner.

Boltzmann’s struggle in understanding the physical principles differentiating between living and non-living matter, Schrödinger’s negative entropy in metabolisms, Prigogine’s intuition of the instability of the inhomogeneous, and Haken’s synergetics are in fact all direct manifestations of a fundamental principle of locality. It can be considered the complement of the second law of thermodynamics explaining the emergence of order from disorder instead of disorder from order, in a quantitative way, at least for reaction diffusion systems.

### 1.1. The local activity principle in physical chemistry

Self-organization is a well-known phenomenon in physical chemistry that can be observed in many particle systems of active media [40]. In mathematical terms, these systems are defined by reaction-diffusion equations for their individual components i with

```
ẋᵢ = gᵢ({xⱼ}) + Dᵢ∇xᵢ
```

where xᵢ(⃗r, t) are the local concentrations of i and gᵢ({xⱼ}) are a set of nonlinear functions gᵢ(xᵢ, …, xₘ) that describe the reaction rates for components i. The term Dᵢ represents the diffusion constants of the different reacting components. ∇ is the second-order derivative operator of the spatial coordinate vector ⃗r. Chemical reactions are assumed to take place in small volume elements of a homogeneous composition in which the concentrations change.

Active media are classified as bistable, excitable and oscillatory. In a bistable medium the equations gᵢ({xⱼ}) have two states as the stationary solution. Large perturbations trigger transitions between these states which may result in trigger waves of, for example, flame propagation. They can also generate a large variety of patterns. A chemical example is the autocatalytic reaction scheme in which x is multiplied [49]:

```
A + 2x ⇌ 3x
k₁   k₂
x ⇌ B
k₁   k₂
```

This simple scheme illustrates formation of spatio-temporal patterns by local finite-amplitude perturbations. It is a simplification of the autocatalytic model of the glycolysis cycle. A is a feed term and B an inert product. It can be shown that this system leads to the formation of local regions of concentration defined by sharp boundaries. These local regions provide a cell-like profile with multiplication and division behavior. It reminds us of self replication in protocellular systems. When the cell-like units exceed a particular dimension, the interior destabilizes because the necessary concentration gradient is not maintained. In this case, the cells divide. This kind of local activity by self replication was verified experimentally in a reaction system with ferrocyanide, sulfide and iodide that has a bistable stationary solution.

Different spatio-temporal patterns can emerge depending on the kinetics in this system. There are turbulent double rotating spirals, cellular, and target patterns. Reactions are represented by composition changes with a particular probability of grid positions, depending on the occupation of neighboring grid positions. In these cases, pattern formation is self-organized in the oscillatory medium of catalytic oscillators. Self-organizing patterns will only occur in specific reaction condition regimes.
