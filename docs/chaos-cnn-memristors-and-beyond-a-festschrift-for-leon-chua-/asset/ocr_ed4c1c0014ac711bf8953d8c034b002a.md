First, the obstacle must be greater in size than the width (sometimes termed wavelength, the product of wave speed and action potential duration) of the wavefront to induce a wavebreak. Second, the wave can detach from the obstacle generating a free end only if the curvature of the obstacle at the detachment point is greater than the inward propagation angle of the propagating free end, which in turn depends largely on excitability of the substrate. Situations with multiple obstacles or randomly distributed heterogeneities are more complex [4]. After a spiral wave is initiated, it can either persist, breakup into multiple wavelets, or die out. Spiral wave breakup is a complex phenomenon that has been dealt with in detail by several researchers (see [13] for an excellent review), however it is unlikely to play a role in bursting rhythms such as paroxysmal NSVT as it doesn’t result in cessation of activity. Spiral wave termination occurs in homogeneous and heterogeneous media when the wave tip travels (a process called meander in homogeneous media, and drift in heterogeneous media) leading to a collision with an unexcitable boundary [12,34].

Heart cell monolayers are thin layers of tissue grown in culture dishes from embryonic or neonatal cardiac cells. Cardiac cells from very young animals have the capacity to form gap junctional connections with neighbouring cells in culture. After a few days in culture, embryonic cardiac cells are capable of supporting propagating waves of excitation over long distances. Cardiac monolayers were popular thirty years ago as model systems of two-dimensional conduction. More recently, the availability of potential mapping techniques have renewed interest in cultured monolayers, as they allow controlled environments for studying conduction on microscopic [36] and macroscopic [43] scales.

We measured calcium transients from dispersed monolayer cultures of embryonic chick heart cells as a function of age in culture, plating density, and gap junction connectivity. We found that while periodic target patterns predominate for well connected cardiac substrates, bursting patterns were frequently observed in relatively poorly connected monolayers (Fig. 1).
