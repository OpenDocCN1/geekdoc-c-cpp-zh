
$$x_s(t) = \sum_k a_{k,s} \phi(2^s t - k),$$ (5.5)

and any function $y_s(t) \in W_s$ can be written as

$$y_s(t) = \sum_k w_{k,s} \psi(2^s t - k),$$ (5.6)

for some coefficients $\{a_{k,s}\}_{k \in \mathbb{Z}}, \{w_{k,s}\}_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \in \mathbb{R}$.

Since

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{s+1} &= W_s \oplus A_s \\
&= W_s \oplus W_{s-1} \oplus A_{s-1} \\
&= W_s \oplus W_{s-1} \oplus W_{s-2} \oplus \cdots
\end{aligned}
$$ (5.7)

we have

$$A_s = \oplus_{\ell = -\infty}^{s-1} W_\ell.$$

Observe that the $\{A_s\}$ are nested while the $\{W_s\}$ are mutually orthogonal. Consequently, we have

$$
\begin{cases}
A_\ell \cap A_m = A_\ell; m \ge \ell \\
W_\ell \cap W_m = \{0\}; \ell \ne m \\
A_\ell \cap W_m = \{0\}; \ell \le m.
\end{cases}
$$

## 5.2 ORTHOGONAL, BIORTHOGONAL, AND SEMIIORTHOGONAL DECOMPOSITION

In Section 5.1, the only requirement we had for the wavelet subspace \(\boldsymbol{W}_s\) was that it be complementary of \(\boldsymbol{A}_s\) in \(\boldsymbol{A}_{s+1}\). In addition to this, if we also require that \(\boldsymbol{W}_s \perp \boldsymbol{A}_s\), then such a decomposition is called an *orthogonal decomposition*. Let us explain the orthogonality of \(\boldsymbol{A}_s\) and \(\boldsymbol{W}_s\) a little further. For simplicity, let \(s = 0\). For this case, \(\{\phi(t - k) : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}\) spans \(\boldsymbol{A}_0\); likewise, \(\{\psi(t - k) : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}\) spans \(\boldsymbol{W}_0\). Then \(\boldsymbol{A}_0 \perp \boldsymbol{W}_0\) implies that

(5.8) \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(t)\psi(t - \ell)dt = 0 \quad \text{for all } \ell \in \mathbb{Z}.\)

In general, \(\{\phi(t-k) : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}\) and \(\{\psi(t-k) : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}\) need not be orthogonal in themselves; that is,

(5.9) \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(t)\phi(t-\ell) dt \neq 0\)

(5.10) \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(t)\psi(t-\ell) dt \neq 0.\)

Let us relax the condition that \(\boldsymbol{A}_s\) and \(\boldsymbol{W}_s\) be orthogonal to each other and assume that the wavelet \(\psi_{k,s} \in \boldsymbol{W}_s\) has a dual, \(\tilde{\psi}_{k,s} \in \tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_s\). Duality implies that the biorthogonality condition is satisfied; namely

(5.11) \(\langle \psi_{k,j}, \tilde{\psi}_{m,\ell} \rangle = \delta_{k,m} \cdot \delta_{j,\ell}, \quad j, k, \ell, m \in \mathbb{Z}.\)

Although we do not require \(\boldsymbol{W}_s \perp \boldsymbol{A}_s\), we do need that \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_s \perp \boldsymbol{A}_s\), the importance of which will become clear later.

Similar to the dual wavelet \(\tilde{\psi}_{k,s}\), we also consider a dual scaling function \(\tilde{\phi}_{k,s}\) that generates another MRA \(\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}_s\}\) of \(L^2\). In other words, \(\phi_{k,s}\) and \(\psi_{k,s}\) are associated with the MRA \(\{\boldsymbol{A}_s\}\), and \(\tilde{\phi}_{k,s}\) and \(\tilde{\psi}_{k,s}\) are associated with the MRA \(\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}_s\}\).

Let us summarize our results so far before proceeding to explain their importance.

### MRA \(\{\boldsymbol{A}_s\}$$

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{ll} A_{s+1} & = A_s + W_s; \\ A_r \cap A_m & = A_r; m \ge \ell \\ W_r \cap W_m & = \{0\}; \ell \neq m \\ A_r \cap W_m & = \{0\}; \ell \le m. \end{array}\right.
$$

### MRA \(\{\tilde{A}_s\}$$

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{ll} \tilde{A}_{s+1} & = \tilde{A}_s + \tilde{W}_s; \\ \tilde{A}_r \cap \tilde{A}_m & = \tilde{A}_r; m \ge \ell \\ \tilde{W}_r \cap \tilde{W}_m & = \{0\}; \ell \neq m \\ \tilde{A}_r \cap \tilde{W}_m & = \{0\}; \ell \le m. \end{array}\right.
$$

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l} W_s \perp \tilde{A}_s \Rightarrow \tilde{A}_r \cap W_m = \{0\} \text{ for } \ell \le m \\ \tilde{W}_s \perp A_s \Rightarrow A_r \cap \tilde{W}_m = \{0\} \text{ for } \ell \le m. \end{array}\right.
$$

The decomposition process discussed so far is called biorthogonal decomposition. To understand its importance let us briefly point out the procedure of decomposition a function into scales, as shown in Figure 5.1. The details are left for Chapter 7.

Given a function \(x(t) \in L^2\), the decomposition into various scales begins by first mapping the function into a sufficiently high-resolution subspace \(A_M\), that is,

(5.12) \(L^2 \ni x(t) \mapsto x_M = \sum_k a_{k,M} \phi(2^M t - k) \in A_M.\)

Now since

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_M &= W_{M-1} + A_{M-1} \\
&= W_{M-1} + W_{M-2} + A_{M-2} \\
&= \sum_{n=1}^N W_{M-n} + A_{M-N},
\end{aligned}
$$

(5.13)

we can write

\( x_M(t) = \sum_{n=1}^N y_{M-n}(t) + x_{M-N}(t) \)

(5.14)

where \( x_{M-N}(t) \) is the coarsest approximation of \( x_M(t) \) and

\( x_s(t) = \sum_k a_{k,s} \phi(2^s t - k) \in A_s \)

(5.15)

\( y_s(t) = \sum_k w_{k,s} \psi(2^s t - k) \in W_s \)

(5.16)

Now the importance of dual wavelets becomes clear. By using the biorthogonality condition (5.11), we can obtain the coefficients \(\{w_{k,s}\}\) as

\( w_{k,s} = 2^s \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y_s(t) \tilde{\psi}(2^s t - k) dt. \)

(5.17)

Recall that \(\tilde{\psi}(2^s t - k) \in \tilde{W}_s\) and \(A_t \perp \tilde{W}_s\) for \(t \le s\). Therefore, by taking the inner product of (5.14) with \(\tilde{\psi}_{k,s}(t)\) and by using the condition (5.11), we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_{k,s} &= 2^s \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_M(t) \tilde{\psi}(2^s t - k) dt \\
&= 2^{s/2} W_{\psi} x_M \left( \frac{k}{2^s}, \frac{1}{2^s} \right).
\end{aligned}
$$

(5.18)

The dual wavelet \(\tilde{\psi}\) can be used to analyze a function \(x_M\) by computing its integral wavelet transform at a desired time-scale location, while \(\psi\) can be used to obtain its function representation at any scale. Therefore, we call \(\tilde{\psi}\) an analyzing wavelet, while \(\psi\) is called a synthesis wavelet.

Of course, if we have orthogonal decomposition \(A_s \perp W_s\) with orthonormal bases \(\{\phi, \psi\}\), then the analyzing and synthesis wavelets are the same. Observe that when we say orthonormal (o.n.) wavelets, this implies that the wavelets are orthogonal with respect to scale as well as with respect to translation in a given scale. But orthonormal scaling function implies that the scaling functions are orthonormal only with respect to translation in a given scale; not with respect to the scale because of the nested nature of the MRA.

A question that arises is, Why do we need biorthogonal (b.o.) wavelets? One of the attractive features in delegating the responsibilities of analysis and synthesis to two different functions in the biorthogonal case as opposed to a single function in the orthonormal case is that in the former, we can have compactly supported symmetric analyzing and synthesis wavelets and scaling functions, something that a continuous o.n. basis cannot achieve. Furthermore, o.n. scaling functions and wavelets have poor time-scale localization.

In some applications to be discussed in later chapters, we need to interchange the roles of the analysis and synthesis pairs, \(\{\phi, \psi\}, \{\tilde{\phi}, \tilde{\psi}\}\), respectively. In b.o. decomposition, we cannot do so easily since \(\phi\) and \(\tilde{\phi}\) generate two different MRAs, \(A\) and \(\tilde{A}\), respectively. For such an interchange, we need to map the given function \(x \mapsto \tilde{x}_M \in \tilde{A}_M\), and then we can use \(\psi\) as analyzing and \(\tilde{\psi}\) as synthesizing wavelets.

In addition to b.o. and o.n. decomposition, there is another class of decomposition called *semiorthogonal decomposition*, for which \(A_s \perp W_s\). Since in this system, the scaling function and wavelets are nonorthogonal, we still need their duals, \(\tilde{\phi}\) and \(\tilde{\psi}\). However, unlike the b.o. case, there is no dual space. That is, \(\phi, \tilde{\phi} \in A_s\) and \(\psi, \tilde{\psi} \in W_s\), for some appropriate scale \(s\). In this system it is very easy to interchange the roles of \(\phi, \psi\) with those of \(\tilde{\phi}, \tilde{\psi}\) .
