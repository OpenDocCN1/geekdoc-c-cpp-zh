
$G_1(z) = -z \overline{G_0(-z)}$
(6.38) $\Rightarrow g_1[k] = (-1)^k g_0[1-k];$

(6.39) $\Delta_{G_0 G_1}(z) = G_0(z)G_1(-z) - G_0(-z)G_1(z) = z$

$H_0(z) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{G_1(-z)}{z} = \frac{1}{2} \times \overline{G_0(z)}$
(6.40) $\Rightarrow h_0[k] = \frac{1}{2} g_0[-k];$

$H_1(z) = -\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{G_0(-z)}{z} = \frac{1}{2} \times \overline{G_1(z)}$
(6.41) $\Rightarrow h_1[k] = \frac{1}{2} (-1)^k g_0[k+1].$

As an example, for a Haar scaling function and wavelet (Figures 5.3 and 5.4),

$g_0[0] = g_0[1] = 1;$

$g_1[0] = 1, g_1[1] = -1;$

$h_0[0] = h_0[-1] = \frac{1}{2};$

$h_1[0] = \frac{1}{2}, \quad h_1[-1] = -\frac{1}{2};$

$g_0[k] = g_1[k] = h_0[k] = h_1[k] = 0 \quad \text{for all other } k.$

**Remarks:** One of the most important features of the orthonormal bases is that all of the decomposition and reconstruction filters are FIR and have the same length. This helps tremendously in the decomposition and reconstruction algorithm discussed in Chapter 7. One of the disadvantages of orthonormal wavelets is that they generally do not have closed form expressions, nor does a compactly supported orthonormal wavelet has linear phase (no symmetry). The importance of linear phase in signal reconstruction will be discussed in Chapter 7. It has also been shown [2] that the higher-order orthonormal scaling functions and wavelets have poor time-frequency localization.

## 6.4 ORTHONORMAL SCALING FUNCTIONS

In this section we will discuss the commonly used orthonormal wavelets of Shannon, Meyer, Battle-Lemarié, and Daubechies. We will derive expressions for only the sequence $\{g_0[k]\}$ since other sequences can be obtained from the relationships of Section 6.1.

### 6.4.1 Shannon Scaling Function

The Shannon sampling function

$$
\phi_{SH}(t) := \frac{\sin \pi t}{\pi t}
\tag{6.42}
$$

is an orthonormal scaling function with $\hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega) = \chi_{[-\pi, \pi)}(\omega)$. Proving the orthogonality of (6.42) in the time-domain by the relation

(6.43) $\langle \phi_{SH}(t-\ell), \phi_{SH}(t) \rangle = \delta_{0, \ell}$

is cumbersome. Here it is rather easy to show that the Riesz bounds are 1; that is,

(6.44) $\sum_k \left| \hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega + 2\pi k) \right|^2 = 1.$

The sequence $\{g_0[k]\}$ can be obtained from the two-scale relation

(6.45) $\frac{1}{2} \sum_k g_0[k] e^{-jk\omega/2} = \frac{\hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega)}{\hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega/2)}.$

Since the left-hand side of the expression is a $4\pi$-periodic function, we need a $4\pi$-periodic extension of the right-hand side. In other words, $G_0(z)$ is nothing but a $4\pi$-periodic extension of $\hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega)$ (see Figure 6.4)

(6.46) $G_0(z) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_\ell g_0[\ell] \exp\left(-j \frac{\omega \ell}{2}\right) = \sum_\ell \hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega + 4\pi \ell).$

**FIGURE 6.4:** $4\pi$-periodic extension of $\hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega)$.

![](img/f73ae67732328dc36596b27ffe8d0152_235_0.png)

From (6.46) we can get the expression for the coefficients $\{g_0[k]\}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
g_0[k] &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{4\pi} \sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega + 4\pi\ell) \exp\left(j \frac{\omega k}{2}\right) d\omega \\
&= \frac{1}{2\pi} \sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{4\pi\ell}^{4\pi(\ell+1)} \hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega) \exp\left(j \frac{\omega k}{2}\right) d\omega \\
&= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega) \exp\left(j \frac{\omega k}{2}\right) d\omega \\
&= \hat{\phi}_{SH}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.47}
$$

By using (6.42), we get

$$
g_0[k] = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1 & \text{for } k = 0 \\ (-1)^{(k-1)/2} \frac{2}{k\pi} & \text{for odd } k \\ 0 & \text{for even } k \neq 0. \end{array} \right.
$$

### 6.4.2 Meyer Scaling Function

The Shannon scaling function $\phi_{SH}(t)$ has poor time localization ($\Delta \phi_{SH} = \infty$). The reason for this is that in the frequency domain, $\hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega)$ has a discontinuity at $-\pi$ and $\pi$. Consequently, in the time domain, as given by (6.42), the function decays as $1/t$ and hence its rms time window width (4.3) is $\infty$. To improve it, Meyer [3, 4] obtained the scaling function $\hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\omega)$ by applying a smoothing function near the discontinuities of $\hat{\phi}_{SH}(\omega)$ in such a way that the orthogonality condition

(6.48) $\sum_k \left|\hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\omega + 2\pi k)\right|^2 = 1$

is satisfied. In (6.48) the index $m$ indicates the degree of smoothness—that is, the $m$th order corner smoothing function $S_m(\omega)$ is $m$ times continuously differentiable. To satisfy the orthogonality requirement (6.48), these corner smoothing functions should have the following properties:

$$
\begin{cases} 
S_m(y)+S_m(1-y)=1, & 0 \le y \le 1; \\ 
S_m(y)=0, & y < 0; \\ 
S_m(y)=1, & y > 1. 
\end{cases}
$$

Examples of corner smoothing functions are given below.

$$
S_0(y) = \begin{cases} 
0, & y < 0; \\ 
y, & 0 \le y \le 1; \\ 
1, & y > 1. 
\end{cases}
$$

$$
S_1(y) = \begin{cases} 
0, & y < 0; \\ 
2y^2, & 0 \le y < 0.5; \\ 
-2y^2 + 4y - 1, & 0.5 \le y \le 1; \\ 
1, & y > 1. 
\end{cases}
$$

Let $S_m(y)$ be a desirable corner smoothing function. Then

$$
\hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\omega) = \begin{cases} 
\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} S_m\left(\frac{3}{2\pi}|\omega|-1\right)\right), & \frac{2\pi}{3} \le |\omega| \le \frac{4\pi}{3}; \\ 
0, & |\omega| \ge \frac{4\pi}{3}; \\ 
1, & |\omega| \le \frac{2\pi}{3}. 
\end{cases}
$$

The scaling function in the time domain then becomes

(6.49) $\phi_{M,m}(t) = \frac{2}{3} \frac{\sin \frac{2\pi t}{3}}{\frac{2\pi t}{3}} + \frac{2}{3} \int_0^1 \left[ \cos\left[ \frac{\pi}{2} S_m(\xi) \right] \cos\left[ \frac{2\pi}{3} (1+\xi) t \right] \right] d\xi$

For a linear smoothing function $m = 0$, the above integral can be easily evaluated. The result is

(6.50) $\phi_{M,0} = \frac{2}{3} \frac{\sin \frac{2\pi t}{3}}{\frac{2\pi t}{3}} + 4 \frac{4t \sin \frac{2\pi t}{3} + \cos \frac{4\pi t}{3}}{\pi \frac{9-16t^2}{3}}$

For higher values of $m$, the integral in (6.49) needs to be evaluated numerically. In Figure 6.6 we show the scaling function and wavelet of Meyer for $m = 0$ and 1.

As done before, the two-scale coefficients $\{g_0[k]\}$ can be obtained by a $4\pi$-periodic extension of $\hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\omega)$. An example of such an extension is shown in Figure 6.7.

$$
\frac{1}{2} \sum_k g_0[k] e^{-jk\omega/2} = \frac{\hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\omega)}{\hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\frac{\omega}{2})} = \sum_k \hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\omega + 4\pi k).
$$

Meyer's wavelets can be obtained by using the two-scale relations. Since the scaling functions have compact support in the frequency domain, Meyer wavelets are related to the scaling function in a more direct way as shown below.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\psi}_{M,m}(\omega) &= G_1(z) \hat{\phi}_{M,m} \left( \frac{\omega}{2} \right) ; \quad z = \exp(-j\omega/2) \\
&= -z \overline{G_0(-z)} \hat{\phi}_{M,m} \left( \frac{\omega}{2} \right) \\
&= -z \left[ \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\omega + 2\pi + 4\pi k) \right] \hat{\phi}_{M,m} \left( \frac{\omega}{2} \right) \\
&= -z \left[ \hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\omega + 2\pi) + \hat{\phi}_{M,m}(\omega - 2\pi) \right] \hat{\phi}_{M,m} \left( \frac{\omega}{2} \right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.52}
$$
