
(6.94) \(\phi_0(t) = \delta(t)\).

and setting \(\phi_0(n) = \delta(n) = 1\), after upsampling by 2, the sequence is convolved with the \(g_0[k]\) sequence to give \(\phi_1(n)\). This sequence is upsampled and convolved with \(g_0[k]\) again to give \(\phi_2(n)\), and so on. In most cases, the procedure usually converges within 10 iterations. For biorthogonal wavelets, convergence time may be longer. Once the scaling function has been obtained, the associated wavelet can be computed and displayed using the two-scale relation for the wavelet

\[
\psi(t) = \sum g_1[k] \phi(2t - k).
\]

### 6.6.2 Spectral Method

In this method, the two-scale relation for the scaling function is expressed in the spectral domain

\[
\hat{\phi}(\omega) = G_0\left(e^{\frac{j\omega}{2}}\right) \hat{\phi}\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right), \quad z = e^{\frac{j\omega}{2}},
\]
\[
= G_0\left(e^{\frac{j\omega}{2}}\right) G_0\left(e^{\frac{j\omega}{4}}\right) \hat{\phi}\left(\frac{\omega}{4}\right)
\]
\[
\vdots
\]
\[
= \prod_{k=1}^N G_0\left(e^{\frac{j\omega}{2^k}}\right) \hat{\phi}\left(\frac{\omega}{2^N}\right)
\]
(6.95)

\[
= \prod_{k=1}^{N \to \infty} G_0\left(e^{\frac{j\omega}{2^k}}\right) \hat{\phi}(0).
\]
(6.96)

Since \(\hat{\phi}(0) = 1\), we may take the inverse Fourier transform of (6.96) to yield

\[
\phi(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \left[ \prod_{k=1}^{N \to \infty} G_0\left(e^{\frac{j\omega}{2^k}}\right) \right] e^{j\omega t} d\omega.
\]
(6.97)

To compute (6.97), the user has to evaluate the truncated infinite product and then take the FFT.

### 6.6.3 Eigenvalue Method

The eigenvalue method converts the two-scale relation into an eigen-equation. Let us consider the two-scale relation by setting x = n to yield the following matrix equation:

\[
\phi(n) = \sum_k g_0[k] \phi(2n-k)
\]
\[
= \sum_m g_0[2n-m] \phi(m)
\]
\[
= [g_0(n, m)] \phi(m)
\]
(6.98)

where the matrix element \(g_0(n, m) = g_0(2n - m)\). In matrix form, we write (6.98) as

\[
\begin{bmatrix} \ddots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \ddots \\ \dots & g_0[0] & g_0[-1] & g_0[-2] & \dots & \dots \\ \dots & g_0[2] & g_0[1] & g_0[0] & \dots & \dots \\ \dots & g_0[4] & g_0[3] & g_0[2] & \dots & \dots \\ & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \ddots \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \vdots \\ \phi(0) \\ \phi(1) \\ \phi(2) \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \vdots \\ \phi(0) \\ \phi(1) \\ \phi(2) \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}
\]

The eigenvalue of this eigenvector is 1, so we can compute \(\phi(n)\) for all integer n. This procedure can be repeated for a twofold increase in resolution. Let \(x = n/2\) , and the two-scale relation becomes

\[
\phi\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) = \sum_k g_0[k] \phi(n-k).
\]
(6.99)

By repeating this procedure for \(x = n/4, n/8, \cdots\), we compute the discretized \(\phi(t)\) to an arbitrarily fine resolution.
