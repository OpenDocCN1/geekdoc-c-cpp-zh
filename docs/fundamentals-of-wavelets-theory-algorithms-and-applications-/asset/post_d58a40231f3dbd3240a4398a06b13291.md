### 3.9.1 Discrete Fourier Basis and Discrete Fourier Series

For a given periodic sequence with periodicity $N$, we have

(3.91) $f_p(n+mN) = f_p(n), \quad m \in \mathbb{Z}$.

The Fourier basis for this periodic sequence has only $N$ basis functions, namely,

(3.92) $\mathbf{e}_k(n) = e^{\frac{j2\pi}{N}kn}, \quad k=0, 1, \dots, N-1$.

We can easily show the periodicity of the basis set

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{e}_{k+N} &= e^{\frac{j2\pi}{N}(k+N)n} \\
&= e_k \cdot e^{j2\pi n} \\
&= e_k
\end{aligned}
$$

since $e^{j2\pi n} = 1$ for integer $n$. Therefore, the expansion of $f_p(n)$ is in the form of

(3.94) $f_p(n) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \alpha_k \mathbf{e}_k(n)$

$= \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \alpha_k e^{\frac{j2\pi}{N}kn}$,

(3.95)

and then we can compute the coefficients by

(3.96) $\alpha_k = \left\langle f_p(n), e^{\frac{j2\pi}{N}kn} \right\rangle = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f_p(n) e^{-\frac{j2\pi}{N}kn}$.

Equations (3.94) and (3.96) form a transform pair for discrete periodic sequences and their discrete spectra. It is quite easy to see from (3.96) that the Fourier coefficients $\{\alpha_k\}$ are also periodic with $N$.

$\alpha_k = \alpha_{k+mN}, m \in \mathbb{Z}$.

### Example 1.

Find the Fourier series coefficients for the sequence

$f(n) = \cos(\sqrt{5}\pi n)$.

Solution: The given sequence is not a periodic sequence since we cannot find an integer $N$ such that $f(n + N) = f(n)$. Consequently, $f(n)$ does not have a discrete Fourier series representation.

### Example 2. Find the Fourier series representation of

- (a) $f(n) = \cos \frac{n\pi}{5}$, and
- (b) $f(n) = \{1, 1, 0, 0\}$.

Solution: (a) Instead of directly computing the coefficients using (3.96), we may represent the cosine function in its exponential form

(3.97) $f(n) = \frac{1}{2} \left\{ e^{j\frac{2\pi n}{10}} + e^{-j\frac{2\pi n}{10}} \right\}$.

The periodicity of this sequence is seen as $N = 10$. Since (3.97) is already in the form of an exponential series as in (3.95), we conclude that

(3.98) $\alpha_k = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} & k=1, \\ \frac{1}{2} & k=9, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$

(b) We compute the Fourier coefficients using (3.96) to obtain

$\alpha_k = \frac{1}{4} \left( 1 + e^{-j\frac{2\pi k}{4}} \right), \quad k=0, 1, 2, 3$.

We have

$$
\alpha_k = 
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{2} & k=0, \\
\frac{1}{4}(1-j) & k=1, \\
0 & k=2, \\
\frac{1}{4}(1+j) & k=3,
\end{cases}
$$

(3.99)

### 3.9.2 Discrete-Time Fourier Transform (DTFT)

If a discrete signal is aperiodic, we may consider it to be a periodic signal with period $N = \infty$. In this case, we extend the discrete Fourier series analysis to DTFT similar to the extension in the analog domain. In DTFT, the time variable $(n)$ is discretized while the frequency variable $(\omega)$ is continuous since

$\Delta\omega = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{2\pi}{N} \to \omega$.

The DTFT pair is explicitly given by

(3.100) $\hat{f}(\omega) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} f(n)e^{-jn\omega}$,

(3.101) $f(n) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \hat{f}(\omega)e^{jn\omega} d\omega$.

Example.

Determine the spectrum of the exponential sequence

$f(n) = a^n, \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{Z}^+ := \{0, 1, \cdots\}, \quad |a| < 1$.

Solution: Using (3.100),

(3.102)
$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{f}(\omega) &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n e^{-jn\omega} \\
&= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (ae^{-j\omega})^n \\
&= \frac{1}{1 - ae^{-j\omega}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

We pointed out in Chapter 2 that the DTFT can be obtained from the z-transform by replacing the variable $z$ with $e^{j\omega}$. For this example, the z-transform is

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(z) &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n z^{-n} \\
&= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (az^{-1})^n \\
&= \frac{1}{1 - az^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, replacing the variable $z$ with $e^{j\omega}$ yields

$F(z)|_{z=e^{j\omega}} = \frac{1}{1 - ae^{-j\omega}} = \hat{f}(\omega)$.

The z-transform $F(z)$ and the DTFT, $[\hat{f}(\omega) = F(z)|_{z=e^{j\omega}}]$, will be used interchangeably in future derivations and discussions on wavelet construction.

## 3.10 DISCRETE FOURIER TRANSFORM (DFT)

The integral in the inverse DTFT discussed in Section 3.9 must be evaluated to recover the original discrete-time signal. Instead of evaluating the integral, we can obtain a good approximation by a discretization on the frequency ($\omega$) axis.

Since the function $f(t)$ is band limited (if it is not, we make it so by passing it through a low-pass filter with sufficiently large width), we need to discretize the interval $[-\Omega, \Omega]$ only, namely,

(3.103) $\omega_n = \frac{2\pi n}{Nh} \quad n = -\frac{N}{2}, \dots, \frac{N}{2}$.

The integral in equation (3.17) can now be approximated as a series sum, namely

(3.104) $\hat{f}(\omega_n) = h \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} f(k) e^{-j\omega_n k} = h \hat{f}(n)$

where

(3.105) $\hat{f}(n) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} f(k) e^{-\frac{j2\pi kn}{N}}$.

We can easily verify that the evaluation of the discrete Fourier transform using (3.105) is an $O(N^2)$ process. We can compute the discrete Fourier transform with an $O(N \log_2 N)$ operation with the well-known algorithm of *fast Fourier transform* (FFT). One of the commonly used FFT algorithms is by Danielson and Lanczos, according to which, assuming $N$ to be such that it is continuously divisible by 2, a DFT of data length $N$ can be written as a sum of two discrete Fourier transforms, each of length $N/2$. This process can be used recursively until we arrive at the DFT of only two data points. This is known as the Radix-2 FFT algorithm. Without getting into many details of the algorithm, which the interested reader can obtain from many excellent books available on these topics, we simply mention here that by appropriately arranging the data of length $N$ where $N$ is an integer power of 2 (known as decimation-in-time and decimation-in-frequency arrangements), we can compute the discrete Fourier transform in an $O(N \log_2 N)$ operation. If $N$ is not an integer power of 2, we can always make it so by padding the data sequence with zeros.
