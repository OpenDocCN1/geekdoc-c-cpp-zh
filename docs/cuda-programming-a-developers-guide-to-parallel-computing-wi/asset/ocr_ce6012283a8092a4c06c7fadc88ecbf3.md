# Hardware acceleration

The Intel AES-NI extension to the x86 processor instruction set is available on most Intel Sandybridge 15 and 17 processors as well as the Westmere-based 17 Xeon processors and their successors. The AES-NI instruction set consists of the following instructions:

+   - AESENC (cipher data, round key)—Standard round of encoding completely in hardware.
+   - AESENCLAST (cipher data, round key)—Last round of encoding completely in hardware.
+   - AESKEYGENASSIST (round key, cipher key, round number)—Assist in the generation of the round keys.
+   - AESDEC (cipher data, round key)—Standard round of decryption in hardware.
+   - AESDECLAST (cipher data, round key)—Last round of decryption in hardware.

Thus, the entire AES encryption and decryption process can be done entirely in hardware. Special 128-bit xmm1 and xmm2 registers are used to contain the operands in single registers. We see that in practice when such AES-NI is used with real applications, there is something in the order of a 2× or more performance improvement over a nonaccelerated processor (Toms Hardware, “AES-NI Benchmark Results: Bitlocker, Everest, And WinZip 14,” http://www.tomsguide.com/uk/clarkdale-aes-ni-encryption-review-31801-7.html). Of course with handwritten assembly and optimal scheduling conditions over many cores, it’s possible to get significantly more. This, however, gives us a feel for the likely benefit of coding such a solution and therefore it seems worth the effort.
