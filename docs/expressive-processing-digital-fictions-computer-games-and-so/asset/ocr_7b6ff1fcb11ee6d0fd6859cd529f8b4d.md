More expensive houses have stronger positive impacts on the need meters of the Sims. In turn, Sims that leave for work in a better mood perform better in their jobs, leading to promotions, leading to more income, leading to the ability to buy better things, build job skills more effectively, have a bigger house, have more friends over, maintain larger social networks, be happier, perform better at the job, and so forth. Yet there never seems to be enough time. At the start of the game jobs must be found—and then it’s a challenge just to get the Sims fed, showered, and urinated before the carpool comes to pick them up. If you don’t they’ll never get promoted, and if they miss the carpool they’ll get fired, but the minutes of the day click by far too quickly. It all starts to feel like a familiar Dialect(c).

Of course, this is what makes the game a challenge to play—and games without challenges don’t tend to be best sellers. But Wright also considers the game more reflectively, as a comment on our time-stretched lives and even a parody of consumer culture (Wingfield 2006).9 Frasca (2001) and others are unconvinced.10 The very rules of the simulation enforce the roles of commodity capitalism, so what does it matter if the in-game catalog descriptions, or the game’s designer, poke gentle fun at its structures?
