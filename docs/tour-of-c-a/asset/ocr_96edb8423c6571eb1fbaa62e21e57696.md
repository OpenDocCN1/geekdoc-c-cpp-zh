C++ was designed primarily by my friends and I would not have to program in assembler, C, or various non-fashionable high-level languages. Its main purpose was to make good programs easier and more pleasant for the individual programmer. In the early years, there were no C++ paper design; design, documentation, and implementation went simultaneously. There was no “C++ project”, neither a “C++ design committee.” Throughout, C++ evolved to cope with problems encountered by users and by a set of discussions among my friends, my colleagues, and me.

The first design of C++ included function declarations with argument checking and implicit conversions, with the public/private distinction between the interface and the implementation, derived classes, constructors and destructors. I used macros to provide primitive parametrization [Stroustrup,1982]. This was in non-experimental use until mid-1980. Later year, I was presented to a set of languages supporting a cohort of programming styles. In retrospect, I consider the introduction of constructors and destructors most significant. In the terminology of the time [Stroustrup,1979].

A “new function” creates the execution for the member functions; the “delete function” returns.

Soon after, “new function” and “delete function” were renamed “constructor” and “destructor.” Here is the root of C++’s strategies for resource management (causing a demand for exceptions) and the key to many techniques for making code short and clear. If there were other languages at the time that supported multiple constructors capable of executing general code, they didn't (and don't) have many destructors. We were new to C++..

C++ was released in October 1985. By then, I had added inlining ($1.3, $5.2.1), consts ($1.6), function overloading ($1.3), references ($1.7), operator overloading ($5.2.1, $6.4), and virtual functions ($3.4). Of these features, for run-time polymorphism in the form of virtual functions was by far the most controversial. I knew its worth from Simul but found it impossible to convince most people in the systems programming world of its value. Systems tended to view indirect function calls with suspicion, and people acquainted with other languages supporting object-oriented programming had a hard time believing that virtual functions could be fast enough to be used in systems. Conversely, many programmers with an object-oriented background (and many still) had a hard time getting to use virtual function calls only to express a choice that must be made at run. The resistance to virtual functions may be related to a resistance to the idea that you can get better through more rigid structure of code supported by a programming language. Many C programmers convinced that reality remains is flexible and creative individual crafting of every detail of a program. My view was (and is) that every bit of help we get from languages and the inherent complexity of the systems we are trying to build at the edge of what we can express.
