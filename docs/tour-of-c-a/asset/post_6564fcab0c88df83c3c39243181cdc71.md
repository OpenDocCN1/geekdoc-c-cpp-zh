## 19.1.3 The ISO C++ Standards

The explosive growth of C++ caused some changes. Sometimes during 1987, it became clear that formal standardization of C++ was inevitable and that we needed to lay the groundwork for a standardization effort [Stroustrup,1994]. The result was a conscious effort to maintain contact between implementers of C++ compilers and their major users. This was done through electronic mail and face-to-face meetings at C++ conferences and elsewhere.

AT&T Bell Labs made a major contribution to C++ and its wider community by allowing me to share revised versions of the C++ reference manual that could be seen. Because many of people worked for companies that were also competing with AT&T, the significance of this contribution should not be underestimated. an enlightened committee could have caused major problems of language fragmentation simply by doing nothing. As it happened, around a hundred individuals from dozens of organizations commented on what became the generally accepted reference manual and the document for the ANSI C++ standardization effort. Their names can be found in the Annotated C++ Reference Manual ("the ARM") [Ellis,1999]. The X316 committee of ANSI was created in December 1989 at the initiative of Hewlett-Packard, DEC, and IBM by users. ANSI (American national) standardization of C++ became part of an ISO (international) standardization effort for C++. From 1990 onwards, these joint C++ standards committees have been the main forum for the evolution of C++ and the refinement of it. They served on these committees throughout. In particular, as chairman of the working group for extensions (later called the evolution group) from 1990 to 2014, I was responsible for handling proposals for major changes to C++ and the addition of new features. An initial draft standard for public review was produced in April 1995. The first ISO C++ standard (ISO/IEC 14882-1998) [C++,1998] was ratified by a 22-0 vote in 2003, so sometimes people refer to C03, that is essentially the same language and standard as C++98.

C++, known for years as C++0x, is the work of the members of WG21. The committee worked under increasingly onerous self-imposed processes and procedures. These processes probably led to a better (and more rigorous) specification, but they also limited innovation [Stroustrup,2007]. An initial draft standard for public review was produced in 2009. The second C++ standard (ISO/IEC 14882-2011) [C++,2011] was ratified by a 21-0 vote in August 2011.

One reason for the lag between the two standards is that most of the committee (including me) were under the mistaken impression that the ISO rules required a "waiting period" after a standard was issued before starting on new features. Consequently, serious work on new language features did not start until 2002. Other reasons included the increased size of modern languages and their foundations libraries. In terms of pages of standards text, the language grew by about 30% and the standard library by about 100%. Much of the increase was due to more detailed specification, rather than new functionality.

a new C++ standard obviously had to take care not to compromise older code through incompatible changes. There are billions of lines of C++ code in use that the committee must not break. Stability over decades is an essential “feature.”

C++11 added massively to the standard library and pushed to complete the feature set needed for a programming language that is a synthesis of the “paradigms” and idioms that had proven successful with C++98.

The overall aims for the C++11 effort were:

- Make C++ a better language for systems programming and library building.
- Make C++ easier to teach and learn.

The aims are documented and detailed in [Stroustrup, 2007].

A major effort was made to make this model {18.1} and support for lock-free programming. This was the work of Hans Boehm, Brian McKnight, and others in the concurrency working group. On top of that, we added the threads.

After C++11 there was wide agreement that 13 years between standards were far too many. Herb Sutter proposed that the committee adopt a policy of shipping on time at fixed intervals, the “train model.” I argued for a short interval to minimize the change of delays because someone insisted on extra time to allow inclusion of “just one more essential feature.” We agreed on an ambitious 3-year schedule that we would alternate between minor and major releases.

C++14 was deliberately a minor release “completing C++11”. This reflects the reality that with a release date, there will be features that we know, but can’t deliver on time. Once, in widespread use, gaps in the feature set will inevitably be discovered.

C++17 was meant to be a major release. By “major,” I mean containing features that change the way we think about the structure of our software and how we design it. By definition, C++17 was at a minimum release. lot of minor extensions, the features that would have had dramatic changes (e.g., concepts, modules, and coroutines) were either not ready or made in controversy and lack of design direction. As a result, C++ programmer who had already absorbed the lessons of C++11 and C++14.

C++20 offers long-promised and much-needed major features, such as modules {3.2.2}, concepts ({8.2}), ranges ({18.6}, {14.5}), and many minor features. It is as a major upgrade to C++ as C++11. It became widely available in late 2021.

The ISO standards committee, SC22/WG21, has about 350 members, out of which about 250 attended the last pre-pandemic face-to-face meeting in Prague C++20 was approved by a unanimous 79-0 that was ratified by a national body vote of 22-0. Getting that degree of agreement among a large and diverse group is hard. Dangers include “Design by committee,” feature bloat, lack of consistent style, and short-sighted decisions. Making progress toward simpler and more coherent language is very hard. The committee is aware of and trying to counter it; [Wong, 2020]. Sometimes, we succeed and it is very hard to avoid complexity creeping in from “minor features,” fashion, and the desire of experts to serve rare special cases directly.

## 19.1.4 Standards and Style

A standard says what will work, and how. There are significant differences between understanding the technical details of programming language features and using them effectively in combination with other features, libraries, and tools to produce better software. By "better" I mean "more maintainable, less error-prone, and faster." We need to develop, popularize, and support coherent programming languages. Further, we must support the evolution of code to these more modern, effective, and coherent styles.

With the growth of the language and its standard library, the problem of popularizing effective programming styles became critical. It is extremely difficult to make large groups of programmers depart from what works for something. There are still people who see C++ as a few minor additions to C and people who regard 1980s Object-Oriented programming styles based on massive classes the hierarchy of development. Many are still struggling to use C++ well in environments with lots of C++ code. On the other hand, there are many who enthusiastically use novel facilities. For example, programmers are convinced that only code using massive amounts of metaprogramming is true C++.

What is C++? In 2015, I set to answer this question by developing a set of coding guidelines with rationales. I found that I was not alone in grappling with that, and together with many people from the world, notably from Microsoft, Red Hat, and Facebook, started the "C++ Core Guidelines" project [Stroustrup,2015]. This is an ambitious project at complete type-safety and complete resource-safety as a benefit, faster, and more maintainable code [Stroustrup,2021b]. In addition to coding rules with rationales, we back up the guidelines with static analysis tools and a tiny support library. I see something like that moving the C++ community at large forward to benefit from the improvements in language features, libraries, and supporting tools.

## 19.1.5 C++ Use

C++ is now a widely used programming language. Its population grew quickly from one in 1979 to 400,000 in 1991; that the number of users doubled about every 7.5 months for more than a decade. Naturally, the growth rate slowed since that initial spurt, but by my best estimate is there about 4.5 million C++ programmers in 2018 [Kazakova,2015] and maybe a million more today. Which of that growth happened after 2005 when the exponential explosion of processor speed stopped that language performance grew in importance. This growth was achieved without formal marketing or an organized user community [Stroustrup,2020].

C++ is primarily an industrial language; it is more prominent in industry than in education or programming language research. It grew up in Labs inspired by the varied and stringent needs of telecommunications and systems programming (including device drivers, networking, and embedded systems).
