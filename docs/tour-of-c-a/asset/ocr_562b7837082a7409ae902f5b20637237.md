# 19.1.3 The ISO C++ Standards

The explosive growth of C++ caused some changes. Sometimes during 1987, it became clear that formal standardization of C++ was inevitable and that we needed to lay the groundwork for a standardization effort [Stroustrup,1994]. The result was a conscious effort to maintain contact between implementers of C++ compilers and their major users. This was done through electronic mail and face-to-face meetings at C++ conferences and elsewhere.

AT&T Bell Labs made a major contribution to C++ and its wider community by allowing me to share revised versions of the C++ reference manual that could be seen. Because many of people worked for companies that were also competing with AT&T, the significance of this contribution should not be underestimated. A enlightened committee could have caused major problems of language fragmentation simply by doing nothing. As it happened, around a hundred individuals from dozens of organizations commented on what became the generally accepted reference manual and the document for the ANSI C++ standardization effort. Their names can be found in the Annotated C++ Reference Manual ("the ARM") [Ellis,1999]. The X316 committee of ANSI was created in December 1989 at the initiative of Hewlett-Packard, DEC, and IBM by users. ANSI (American national) standardization of C++ became part of an ISO (international) standardization effort for C++. From 1990 onwards, these joint C++ standards committees have been the main forum for the evolution of C++ and the refinement of it. They served on these committees throughout. In particular, as chairman of the working group for extensions (later called the evolution group) from 1990 to 2014, I was responsible for handling proposals for major changes to C++ and the addition of new features. An initial draft standard for public review was produced in April 1995. The first ISO C++ standard (ISO/IEC 14882-1998) [C++,1998] was ratified by a 22-0 vote in 2003, so sometimes people refer to C03, that is essentially the same language and standard as C++98.

C++, known for years as C++0x, is the work of the members of WG21. The committee worked under increasingly onerous self-imposed processes and procedures. These processes probably led to a better (and more rigorous) specification, but they also limited innovation [Stroustrup,2007]. An initial draft standard for public review was produced in 2009. The second C++ standard (ISO/IEC 14882-2011) [C++,2011] was ratified by a 21-0 vote in August 2011.

One reason for the lag between the two standards is that most of the committee (including me) were under the mistaken impression that the ISO rules required a "waiting period" after a standard was issued before starting on new features. Consequently, serious work on new language features did not start until 2002. Other reasons included the increased size of modern languages and their foundations libraries. In terms of pages of standards text, the language grew by about 30% and the standard library by about 100%. Much of the increase was due to more detailed specification, rather than new functionality.
