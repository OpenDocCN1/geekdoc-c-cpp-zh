Issue of Sampling Let us now consider the sampling consequences of low pass filtering an image. Nyquist sampling criteria says that the minimum samples required to sample an image are double the highest frequency contained in the image. As an image undergoes low pass filtering, its frequency content decreases (f3 < f2 < f1 in Figure 3.12). This means that the minimum number of samples required to adequately sample the low pass filtered image goes down too. This says that the low pass filtered image can be at a smaller size than the original image. Or, as we progressively increase the size of the low pass filter, we do not need to have the image at its original size, but we can resample and store them at a much smaller size, just adequately large to sample the highest frequencies in them.

This property is used to build a pyramid of progressively low pass filtered images called the Gaussian pyramid. For this, we resample the original image to the size of 2^n × 2^n. This forms level 0 of the pyramid. 2 × 2 pixel blocks of this image are low pass filtered to create a single pixel of the image at the next level of the hierarchy providing a 2^{n-1} × 2^{n-1} image. When using a box filter, this amounts to just averaging every 2 × 2 blocks of pixels in level n to create each pixel of level (n + 1) × (n + 1). Note that since the image at level i + 1 is a low pass filtered from the image at level i, the lower resolution is adequate to sample this image with lower frequency content. This process if progressively continued creates n levels of the pyramid with the last level being a single pixel which can be considered to be an image of 2^{n-n} × 2^{n-n} = 1 × 1.
