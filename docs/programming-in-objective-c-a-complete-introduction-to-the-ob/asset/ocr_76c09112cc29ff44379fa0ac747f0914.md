Let's go back to the previous list and write everything in this new syntax. Before you do that, you need to get your new car. Go to the factory for that, like so:

```
yourCar = [Car new];      get a new car
```

You send a new message to the Class (the receiver of the message) asking it to give you a car. The resulting object (which represents your unique car) is stored in the variable yourCar. From on, *yourCar* can be used to refer to your instance of the car, which you got from the factory.

Because you wanted to get to the factory for your car, the method is called a *class method*. The rest of the actions on your new car will be instance methods because they apply to your car.

```
[yourCar prep];      get it ready for first-time use
[yourCar drive];      drive your car
[yourCar wash];       wash your car
[yourCar getGas];     put gas in your car if you need it
[yourCar service];    service car
```

```
[yourCar topDown]; if it's a convertible
[yourCar topUp]
currentMileage = [yourCar odometer];
```

This example shows an instance method that returns information—presumably, the current mileage, as indicated on the odometer. Here we store that information inside a variable in our program called *currentMileage*.

Here's an example of where a method takes an *argument* that specifies a particular value that may differ from one method to call the:

```
[yourSetSpeed: 55];      set the speed to 55 mph
```

Your sister, Sue, can use the methods for her own instance of a car:

```
[suesCar drive];
[suesCar wash];
[suesCar getGas];
```

Applying the same methods to different objects is one of the key concepts of object-oriented programming, and you'll learn more about later.

You probably won't need to work with cars in your programs. Your objects will likely be computer-oriented things, as windows, rectangles, pieces of text, or maybe even a calculator or a playlist of songs.
