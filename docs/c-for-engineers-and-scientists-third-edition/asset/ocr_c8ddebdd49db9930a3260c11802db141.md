Formally, a model is a representation of a problem. The first step in creating an object-based model is to begin "thinking about objects." For example, if you want to know the result of tossing a coin hundred times, you can certainly do so by tossing a real coin, however, if a coin could be modeled you could find by writing a program to simulate a coin toss. Objects, such as coins, cards, and more make complicated objects, which are well suited to a programming representation because they can be modeled by two basic characteristics: attributes and behaviors. **Attributes define** the properties of interest, and **behaviors define how the object reacts to its environment.** When designing and developing an object-oriented program, you need to follow these two steps:

+   1. Identify the required objects.
2. For each object:
   a. Identify the attributes of interest.
   b. Identify the behaviors (operations) of interest.

To make this more tangible, think about a coin-tossing experiment. Step 1 tells you to identify the required objects. For this experiment, the object is a coin. Step 2 tells you to identify the relevant attributes and behaviors. In attributes, a coin has a denomination, size, weight, color (arnished or worn), country of origin, and side (head or tail). If you want to collect coins, you need all these attributes but for a coin, however, the only attribute that's of interest is the side; it doesn't matter whether the coin is a penny or a quarter, copper or silver, in terms of modeling a coin for a coin-tossing experiment, the only attribute you must consider is what visible when the coin is tossed. It's important to understand the significance of the choice of attributes. Very few models include every aspect of the problem

Having the attributes to use in modeling a coin, the next step requires identifying the behavior this object should exhibit. In this case, you must have a means of simulating a toss and determining the side that faces when the toss is completed.
