Consider a non-programming example. If I deposit $1,000.00 dollars into a bank, the bank has custody of my money. It is still my money, so I theoretically reclaim it at any time. The bank stores money in its safe, and my money is in the safe as well. Suppose I wish to withdraw $100 dollars from my account. Since I have $1,000 total in my account, the transaction should be no problem. What is wrong with the following scenario:

+   1. Enter the bank.
2. Walk past the teller into a room that provides access to the safe.
3. The door to the safe is open, enter the safe and remove $100 from a stack of $20 bills.
4. Exit the safe and inform a teller that you got $100 out of your account.
5. Leave the bank.

This is not a normal bank usage to handle withdrawals. In a perfect world where everyone is honest and makes mistakes, all is well. In reality, many customers might be dishonest and intentionally take more money than they want. Even if faithfully counted out my funds, some of the bills in the wallet may stuck to each other and I made an honest mistake by picking up $20 bills instead of five. If I place the bills in wallet with money that is already there, I may never detect the error. Clearly a bank needs a more controlled procedure for handling customer withdrawals.

When working with programming objects, in many situations it is better to restrict client access to the internals of an object. Client code should not be able to change directly bank account objects for various reasons, including:

+   * Federal laws dictate that deposits above a certain amount should be reported to the Internal Revenue Service, so a bank would not want customers to be able to add funds to an account to circumvent this process.
* An account number should not change for a given account for the life of that account.

How do we protect the details of our account objects and permit customers to interact with them in a well-defined, controlled manner? The trick is to hide completely from the object's fields and provide special functions, called methods, that have access to the hidden fields. These methods provide the only means available to clients of changing the object's internal state.
