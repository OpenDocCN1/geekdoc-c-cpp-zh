The Department of Educational Sciences and Early Childhood Education, especially the Laboratory of Science, Mathematics and ICT Education of the University of Patras in Greece, was one of the twenty-four Twin Centres responsible for training educators on how to plan their teaching following the enquiry-based approach. # 2.3. Professional development

Educators attended a three-hour training led by the researchers of this study. The training was planned in two parts. In the first part, educators were familiarised with the principles of Inquiry Science-Based Education with an emphasis on robotics and tangible programming. Programming concepts were approached through maths (spatial reasoning, measurement and patterns/iteration) to show how they are embedded within the national curriculum. In addition, this part provided introductory information on the robotic tools, focusing mainly on the tangible robot Bee-Bot and its affordances in early childhood education to teach programming. In the second part, educators participated in hands-on play with the robot. They were introduced to the scenario-based design in early childhood, the pedagogical principles and methodology, and this was based on and how these were formulated in activities. Teachers were given the opportunity to implement the scenario as it was planned for their classrooms, understand how to implement the research instruments and their importance for data’s validity, share possible drawbacks and propose solutions altogether as a group. Furthermore, teachers were given hard copies of the scenario, the assessment instruments, and the kit for implementation (robots, cards of commands-pseudocode, mats, pictures and other additional resources). The training slides, the scenario and assessment instruments were also provided in a digital form. Educators were given ongoing professional development and support through in-person assistance and role-modelling from researchers in their classrooms or phone calls and meetings. Additionally, a log was created to keep track of which classes delivered assessments and when and when they were planned to finish the implementation and organise the rotation of kits’ distribution.

# 3. Procedure

# 3.1. Instruments and Data collection

The present study followed the design-based research (DBR) methodology, which aims to improve educational practices through systematic, flexible, and iterative review, analysis, design, development, and implementation, based upon collaboration amongst researchers and practitioners in real-world settings leading to design principles or theories (The Design-Based Research Collective, 2003; Wang & Hannafin, 2004). Thus, an iterative model of a developmental DBR approach through a three-year implementation, analysis, and redesign was developed. It involved multiple collaborative iterations (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012). It used multiple case studies to collect qualitative and quantitative developmental data (Kelly et al., 2008) within the same methodological framework without a distinction to be made. It was considered to be included under the case study method (Yin, 2009). These are approached from mixed research methods and involve three iterations, collaborative partnership between researchers and educators and tactic impact on the educational process. Hence, as the DBR approach supports, this research applies mixed methods from quantitative and qualitative data and instruments (Fig. 2). A concurrent triangulation approach is applied, which ‘provides quantitative statistical results followed by qualitative quotes that support or disconfirm the quantitative results’ (Creswell, 2009). This study utilised the Logo-like tangible robot Bee-Bot and a scenario-based design with three scenarios (teaching sequences), following pedagogical and developmentally appropriate methodological criteria to organise the learning in programming through three different learning areas of the curriculum: i) spatial reasoning, ii) measurement, iii) memory/iteration. Different research instruments were developed and introduced to children through the distinct phases of an educational scenario concerning the data collection and analysis techniques (Fig. 2). All these instruments were tools for educators to record and evaluate the learning process. Mainly, all the data emerged from the post-test interviews, which were structured interviews with two different instruments reporting on mental representations and subject knowledge (Appendices 1-4). The interviews were used individually to assess (prior) and evaluate (post) the transformation of children’s mental representations and consolidation of CT’s skills. Furthermore, data on debugging was derived from the richness and range of the programming profiles that emerged in this study since no teaching activities or assessment of the debugging process were not included in the scenarios implemented.

# 3.2. Scenario-based design for teaching and learning programming

At this point, we should describe what a scenario-based design is. A scenario-based design for teaching and learning alongside the tangible robot Bee-Bot is used to develop children’s abilities of CT (Misirli & Komis, 2014; Komis & Misirli, 2015; Komis et al., 2017). The conceptualisation of a scenario-based design is based on constructionism pedagogical approaches such as project-based learning, child-centred learning, problem-solving learning environment, collaborative learning, and scaffolding and reflection process. It includes seven parts: 1.Identification of the teaching subject, 2.Children’s prior mental representations and subject knowledge, 3.Learning goals, 4.Teaching activities, 5.Artifacts and material, 6.Children’s post mental representations and subject knowledge, and 7.Documentation. This model constructs a linear model for designing a full teaching intervention based on robotics and presents different planning and implementation instances (Misirli & Komis, 2014).

Especially for part 4 (Teaching activities), the idea was to have two sections. The first section of activities was designed to be focused on robotics concepts (function, control and operation of the robot), around two basic programming strategies: i) ‘step-by-step’ and ii) automated. The first strategy is highly recommended to help students in synchronise their thinking better with the actual movement of the robot and the program being executed (Nusen & Sipitakiat, 2011). The gridded mats we designed supported this strategy as the length of each square corresponds to the distance Bee-Bot moves on one linear step. Hence, children, by moving the robot on the grid from one location to the other, formulated in an abstractive way the program meant to be built. The second section integrated these concepts into activities but focused on mathematical and programming concepts (spatial reasoning, measurement and memory/iteration). In addition, material (gridded mats of 15 cm squares, cards/set of commands, themed pictures) was developed and provided to educators in a kit.
