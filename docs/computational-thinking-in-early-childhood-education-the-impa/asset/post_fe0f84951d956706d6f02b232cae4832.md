They added new design aspects to the robots they developed and provided clues that helped children better understand, locate, and solve the problems by showing how step-by-step control allowed children to better articulate and pinpoint errors in the program. Thus, we adopted for our study Klahr & Carver’s debugging framework, which approaches debugging through five distinct and interrelated phases (Fig. 1):

- 1. Test and evaluate a program
- 2. Identify bug
- 3. Represent program
- 4. Locate bug
- 5. Correct bug

In the first phase, the user tests the program and checks by comparing the initial design of the program with its performance in the robotic system. If a discrepancy between design and performance is observed in the behaviour of the particular robotic system, then the user should proceed to Phases 2–5. In that case, the user should first recognise that there is an error (bug), then represent the program to investigate the possible location of the error (bug) and thus lead to the detection of the error (bug) and finally correct it.

As stated by Papert (1993) ‘the process of debugging is a normal part of the process of understanding a program’ (p. 61). In this study, we identify how novice programmers’ debugging process matches the troubleshooting framework proposed by Klahr and Carver (1988) and how we use that framework to explain debugging. Specifically, we aim to answer the following questions:

- 1. How do children identify the existence of error and locate it?
- 2. What types of errors emerge when coding?
- 3. What type of error is complex for novice programmers?
- 4. What strategies are developed by novice programmers to debug effectively?
- 5. What types of knowledge do novice programmers construct by debugging the tangible robot Bee-Bot?

Our work contributes to the field of CT and tangible programming by demonstrating how debugging can be analysed in classifications and play an essential role in the learning process of fostering CT abilities. This study provides typologies of debugging practices focused on developing syntactic and semantic/logical knowledge and their effectiveness. Furthermore, results demonstrate the importance of classified debugging strategies and behaviours to increase preschool children’s exposure to coding and build upon an understanding of the debugging process more systematically. Moreover, the study suggests teaching developmentally appropriate robotic and programming-based activities that further promote the development of computational concepts and thinking in tangible programming.

## 2. Material and methods

### 2.1. Participants

Participants in this study were 526 young children from urban and suburban public schools and local mainstream preschools, with a mean age 5.18 years (4.3–6.5, SD = 0.4655). The sample comprised 256 males and 270 females, with 167 younger (4–5 years old) and 359 older (5–6 years old), given that in the Greek educational system, both ages are blended in each class.

Thirty (30) preschool educators from mainstream public schools trained to implement robotics and programming in their classes following a teaching approach based on a scenario-based design with additional research instruments to collect data. They were instructed to assess children’s prior and post-mental representations and subject knowledge of robots and programming by interviewing them individually following the script of open-ended questions (Appendix 1). That was highly innovative for educators’ theoretical knowledge about summative assessment then. According to those reports, children had no prior knowledge of robots and programming. Though these results are not included in the present paper, we can report algorithmic thinking and programming ability evolution. The ‘Instruments and Data collection’ section provides more details regarding the research design and instruments used.

### 2.2. Recruitment

The study was part of the FP7 European Project ‘The Fibonacci Project-Disseminating an Inquiry Based Science and Mathematics Education (ISBME)’ which had the ambition to contribute to the dissemination of ISBME throughout the European Union. The Fibonacci project defined the dissemination process starting with 12 Reference Centres and 24 Twin Centres. The Department of Educational Sciences and Early Childhood Education, especially the Laboratory of Science, Mathematics and ICT Education of the University of Patras in Greece, was one of the twenty-four Twin Centres responsible for training educators on how to plan their teaching following the enquiry-based approach. To achieve that, the Twin Centre of the University of Patras established its initial Teacher-Network by organizing an open meeting where the philosophy and the usefulness of the project, as well as the actual work plan, were presented to preschool teachers and other stakeholders. In that event, the educators who were willing to participate in the Teacher-Network got familiar with the enquiry-based approach in 3 different scientific areas: i) Science, ii) Mathematics, and iii) ICT and were meant to be trained to integrate them into their teaching practice through a series of professional development pieces of training and workshops that the project offered. Moreover, they were supported by the research team during the implementation phase in their classrooms. At the same time, they, in turn, contributed to the preparatory phase of the new members of the Teacher-Network by sharing with them their experiences and expertise in the meetings.

### 2.3. Professional development

Educators attended a three-hour training led by the researchers of this study. The training was planned in two parts. In the first part, educators were familiarised with the principles of Inquiry Science-Based Education with an emphasis on robotics and tangible programming. Programming concepts were approached through maths (spatial reasoning, measurement and patterns/iteration) to show how they are embedded within the national curriculum. In addition, this part provided introductory information on the robotic tools, focusing mainly on the tangible robot Bee-Bot and its affordances in early childhood education to teach programming. In the second part, educators participated in hands-on play with the robot. They were introduced to the scenario-based design in early childhood, the pedagogical principles and methodology, and how these were formulated in activities. Teachers were given the opportunity to implement the scenario as it was planned for their classrooms, understand how to implement the research instruments and their importance for data’s validity, share possible drawbacks and propose solutions altogether as a group. Furthermore, teachers were given hard copies of the scenario, the assessment instruments, and the kit for implementation (robots, cards of commands-pseudocode, mats, pictures and other additional resources). The training slides, the scenario and assessment instruments were also provided in a digital form. Educators were given ongoing professional development and support through in-person assistance and role-modelling from researchers in their classrooms or phone calls and meetings. Additionally, a log was created to keep track of which classes delivered assessments and when they were planned to finish the implementation and organise the rotation of kits’ distribution.

## 3. Procedure

### 3.1. Instruments and Data collection

The present study followed the design-based research (DBR) methodology, which aims to improve educational practices through systematic, flexible, and iterative review, analysis, design, development, and implementation, based upon collaboration amongst researchers and practitioners in real-world settings leading to design principles or theories (The Design-Based Research Collective, 2003; Wang & Hannafin, 2004). Thus, an iterative model of a developmental DBR approach through a three-year implementation, analysis, and redesign was developed. It involved multiple collaborative iterations (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012). It used multiple case studies to collect qualitative and quantitative developmental data (Kelly et al., 2008) within the same methodological framework without a distinction to be made. It was considered to be included under the case study method (Yin, 2009). These are approached from mixed research methods and involve three iterations, collaborative partnership between researchers and educators and tacit impact on the educational process. Hence, as the DBR approach supports, this research applies mixed methods from quantitative and qualitative data and instruments (Fig. 2). A concurrent triangulation approach is applied, which ‘provides quantitative statistical results followed by qualitative quotes that support or disconfirm the quantitative results’ (Creswell, 2009). This study utilised the Logo-like tangible robot Bee-Bot and a scenario-based design with three scenarios (teaching sequences), following pedagogical and developmentally appropriate methodological criteria to organise the learning in programming through three different learning areas of the curriculum: i) spatial reasoning, ii) measurement, iii) memory/iteration. Different research instruments were developed and introduced to children through the distinct phases of an educational scenario concerning the data collection and analysis techniques (Fig. 2). All these instruments were tools for educators to record and evaluate the learning process. Mainly, all the data emerged from the post-test interviews, which were structured interviews with two different instruments reporting on mental representations and subject knowledge (Appendices 1-4). The interviews were used individually to assess (prior) and evaluate (post) the transformation of children’s mental representations and consolidation of CT’s skills. Furthermore, data on debugging was derived from the richness and range of the programming profiles that emerged in this study since no teaching activities or assessment of the debugging process were included in the scenarios implemented.

### 3.2. Scenario-based design for teaching and learning programming

At this point, we should describe what a scenario-based design is. A scenario-based design for teaching and learning alongside the tangible robot Bee-Bot is used to develop children’s abilities of CT (Misirli & Komis, 2014; Komis & Misirli, 2015; Komis et al., 2017). The conceptualisation of a scenario-based design is based on constructionism pedagogical approaches such as project-based learning, child-centred learning, problem-solving learning environment, collaborative learning, and scaffolding and reflection process. It includes seven parts:

- 1. Identification of the teaching subject
- 2. Children’s prior mental representations and subject knowledge
- 3. Learning goals
- 4. Teaching activities
- 5. Artifacts and material
- 6. Children’s post mental representations and subject knowledge
- 7. Documentation

This model constructs a linear model for designing a full teaching intervention based on robotics and presents different planning and implementation instances (Misirli & Komis, 2014).

Especially for part 4 (Teaching activities), the idea was to have two sections. The first section of activities was designed to be focused on robotics concepts (function, control and operation of the robot), around two basic programming strategies: i) ‘step-by-step’ and ii) automated. The first strategy is highly recommended to help students synchronise their thinking better with the actual movement of the robot and the program being executed (Nusen & Sipitakiat, 2011). The gridded mats we designed supported this strategy as the length of each square corresponds to the distance Bee-Bot moves on one linear step. Hence, children, by moving the robot on the grid from one location to the other, formulated in an abstractive way the program meant to be built. The second section integrated these concepts into activities but focused on mathematical and programming concepts (spatial reasoning, measurement and memory/iteration). In addition, material (gridded mats of 15 cm squares, cards/set of commands, themed pictures) was developed and provided to educators in a kit. However, when using the Bee-Bot, the children’s programme was not visible as it was stored internally in the device. Hence, we introduced the set of commands in cards, which worked as a ‘pseudocode’ (Fig. 3). Initially, the ‘pseudocode’ is a written list of actions in a numbered sequence.
