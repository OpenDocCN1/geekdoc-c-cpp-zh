We followed similar steps with Sipitakiat and Nusen (2012) and Rich et al. Thus, we adopted for our study Klahr & Carver’s debugging framework, which approaches debugging through five distinct and interrelated phases: 1) Test and evaluate a program, 2) Identify bug, 3) Represent program, 4) Locate bug and 5) Correct bug (Fig. 1). In the first phase, the user tests the program and checks by comparing the initial design of the program with its performance in the robotic system. If a discrepancy between design and performance is observed in the behaviour of the particular robotic system, then the user should proceed to Phases 2–5. In that case, the user should first the user should recognise that there is an error (bug), then reproduce the program to investigate the possible location of the error (bug) and thus lead to the detection of the error (bug) and finally correct it.

As stated by Papert (1993) ‘the process of debugging is a normal part of the process of understanding a program’ (p.61). In this study, we identify how novice programmers’ debugging process matches the troubleshooting framework proposed by Klahr and Craven (1988) and how we use that framework to explain debugging. Specifically, we aim to answer the following questions: 1) How do children identify the existence of error and locate it? 2) What types of errors emerge when coding? 3) What type of error is complex for novice programmers? 4) What strategies are developed by novice programmers to debug effectively? and 5) What types of knowledge do novice programmers construct by debugging the tangible robot Bee-Bot?

Our work contributes to the field of CT and tangible programming by demonstrating how debugging can be analysed in classifications and play an essential role in the learning process of fostering CT abilities. This study provides typologies of debugging practices focused on developing syntactic and semantic/logical knowledge and their effectiveness. Furthermore, results demonstrate the importance of classified debugging strategies and behaviours to increase preschool children’s exposure to coding and build upon an understanding of the debugging process more systematically. Moreover, the study suggests teaching developmentally appropriate robotic and programming-based activities that further promote the development of computational concepts and thinking in tangible programming.

# 2. Material and methods

# 2.1. Participants

Participants in this study were 526 young children from urban and suburban public schools and local mainstream preschools, with a mean age 5,18 years (4.3 – 6.5, SD=0,4655). The sample comprised 256 males and 270 females, with 167 younger (4–5 years old) and 359 older (5–6 years old), given that in the Greek educational system, both ages are blended in each class.

Thirty (30) preschool educators from mainstream public schools trained to implement robotics and programming in their classes following a teaching approach based on a scenario-based design with additional research instruments to collect data. They were instructed to assess children’s prior and post-mental representations and subject knowledge of robots and programming by interviewing them individually following the script of open-ended questions (Appendix 1).
