# 4.7. Phase 3–5: Represent program, locate bug and correct bug

In these three phases, the user should represent the program to investigate the possible location of an error, in other words, to detect the error and then take action to correct it. This process requires changing and replacing one or more errors and retesting the program. Consequently, a programmer should detect, decode and verify if one or more errors occur. Our study considers all three phases described by two Variables, V5 and V6. Phase 3 regards the representation of a program, so in our study, this phase is met by the program’s visual representation with the ‘pseudocode’. The teaching sequences of educational scenarios ensured that all children would visualise and reflect their verbal algorithmic designs by ‘pseudocode’. To that end, each child could easily recognise when an algorithmic design was unsuccessful due to error(s) by matching each robot’s movement with each command card. Hence, they were led to detect and locate a bug as suggested in Phase 4, the most demanding cognitive process in debugging. At this point, the child must have identified the location of the error and selected the appropriate command to correct the program effectively and successfully solve the algorithm, Phase 5. Children initially process the solution on an algorithmic design

Then, 122 out of 526 children (f=122, rf=23.19) belong to the category where their programs show errors without corresponding debugging.

In contrast, 120 out of 526 children (f=120, rf=22.81) identify and locate the error and consequently successfully correct it, so in this case, it is called effective debugging. However, out of the whole set of children who proceeded to debug (126/526), only 6 children (f=6, rf=1.14) ultimately failed to modify the inaccurate program while attempting its correction. So they do not complete the process and would have to continue again by going through Phases 3–5 of the debugging model.
