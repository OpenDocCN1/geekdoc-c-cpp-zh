# ABSTRACT

The present study investigates the debugging process of 526 children in preschool (4-6 aged) when programming a tangible robot. When a child is involved in programming, it is needed to identify and correct errors, or in other words, debug a program. Debugging is a high-level thinking skill and is an essential component closely related to developing Computational Thinking in early childhood education. Though the debugging process is a cognitive function in programming investigated by many researchers in recent decades, most studies are conducted either in primary, secondary education, or higher education concerning beginners or experts in programming. Very few reviews, and some focus on preschool education. Our study set out the following objectives: 1) How do children identify the existence of error and locate it? 2) What types of errors emerge when coding? 3) What type of error is complex for novice programmers? 4) What strategies are developed by novice programmers to debug effectively? and 5) What types of knowledge do novice programmers construct by debugging the tangible robot Bee-Bot? The study follows an iterative model of a design-based research approach. It uses multiple case studies to collect qualitative and quantitative data. The programming intervention was implemented by 30 educators in their classrooms and was based on a scenario-based teaching design. The debugging process was analysed using Klahr & Craven's framework (1988): 1) test and evaluate program, 2) identify bug, 3) represent program, 4) locate bug and 5) correct bug. The main finding is the construction and development of syntactic and semantic knowledge.

# 1.1. Computational thinking in early childhood education

The development of Computational Thinking (CT) is included in computer science and, extensively, is combined with educational robotics due to the common goals and objectives they share. In recent years, the term CT has been introduced in the computer science research community and the computer science teaching community by Wing who suggested that CT skills are not only beneficial in computer science but also in many other fields as a skill that is as fundamental as being able to read, write, and do arithmetic (Wing, 2006). From the emergence of the concept to the present day, an extensive literature has been developed which studies the development of this term and the context in which it can be developed. The concept of CT extends the term 'algorithmic thinking' closely related to programming processes (Wing, 2006). Some years later Guzdial in his viewpoint on Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) proposed that CT is the new literacy of the 21st century, which drawing methods from diverse disciplines would improve teaching computing in higher education (Guzdial, 2008). Potentially, CT's context of skills and abilities find application in all scientific areas due to the applicability of different problem-solving strategies and multiple levels of abstraction depending on the context in which they are embedded (Barr & Stephenson, 2011).

Finally, from a psychological point of view, debugging is received as a diagnostic test for ‘symptoms’ displayed on a programme, which would help the programmer to their identification and fixation (Hoc et al., 1990, p.55). Especially, the Logo environment was designed to cultivate debugging as a cognitive function (Solomon & Cambridge, 1986). The emphasis is much more on debugging a child’s ideas than on debugging the actual code (Solomon et al., 2020). Detecting errors in a program is a significant learning and self-regulation activity based on observing differences between expected and observed results. Therefore, debugging process through the pedagogical lens of Logo the has a broader dimension. It is not just about composing correct programs but aims to develop the cognitive aspects of debugging as a fundamental learning and self-regulation activity based on observing differences between expected and observed results. Throughout the debugging process learners develops behaviour patterns such as perseverance and self-regulation (Bers, 2018a).

On the other hand, in the context of teaching programming the detection and correction of errors (mainly logical and less syntactic errors) holds a dominant position: finding and correcting errors is a necessary cognitive and teaching process approached by many researchers but initially is focused on older children and students and tried to compare or highlight debugging strategies established either by novices or between novices and experienced programmers (Kurland et al., 1989; McCauley et al., 2008; Murphy et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2018).

Other researchers are more focused on how debugging could be taught in higher education (Whalley et al., 2021). A considerate volume of work investigated student’s misconceptions in relations to the reasons the bugs occur and how programming course can be improved towards the teaching of debugging (Luxton-Reilly et al., 2018; Rich et al., 2019; Swidan et al., 2018; Albrecht & Grabowski, 2020; Emerson et al., 2020; Johnson et al., 2020). In order for educators to understand how novice programmers interpret debugging process in Logo they need to take into account the level of programmers’ knowledge of the syntactic structure (the rules for combining elements into commands and the rules for combining commands into programs or program segments), and the semantic structure of a programming environment (operations, objects and locations) (Fay & Mayer, 1988).

The programming activity and behaviour of early childhood children during the design of an algorithm and writing of a program (code) is directly related to the process of ‘debugging’. The process in computer science, particularly in computer programming, is called ‘debugging’ and is a high-level thinking skill. Debugging is a fundamental concept even when early childhood students learn programming, and it will be of great value if CT starts in early childhood education (Wing, 2010). Kong shows in a review conducted in 24 senior primary school education studies that testing and debugging are suggested in seven studies’ core CT aspects (Kong, 2019). While much of recent scholarly work deals with the knowledge and CT of novice programmers, there has been less emphasis on how very young children learn to debug. At an early stage, Cuneo examined the debugging strategies of young children in a turtle logo-like programming environment without previous experience in computer programming. Although the feedback and practice children had received in the immediate conditions, they presented difficulty in fixing incomplete programs (missing commands) than fixing a program in an appropriate form (sequence or suitability of commands) (Cuneo, 1986). Despite the fact that early learning environments increasingly incorporate coding curricula and CS standards, there is little about programming and debugging knowledge in early childhood education (Wang et al., 2021). However, we can identify a few studies in early childhood education (ages 4–8) focused on debugging and robotics education either using tangible or visual-block programming or Logo-based computer programming (Bers et al., 2014; Fessakis et al., 2013; Flannery & Bers, 2013; Heikkila & Mannila, 2018; Newhouse et al., 2017; Pugnali et al., 2017; Strawhacker et al., 2018; Sullivan & Bers, 2013). Firstly, Fessakis et al. reported that children could be introduced to basic computer programming concepts using a Logo-based computer environment (Fessakis et al., 2013). Applying a hybrid programming environment with concrete robots and computer visual-block programming Flannery & Bers pointed out distinctive differences of debugging strategies in early childhood children’s reasoning as this was categorised in three cognitive development measures with older ones to demonstrate more sophisticated programming expertise (Flannery & Bers, 2013). Nevertheless, when children use tangible robots in free play, no organised problem-solving behaviours could be developed that could result in a concrete understanding of programming and debugging (Newhouse et al., 2017). Furthermore, Pugnali et al. emphasised that debugging is the most challenging concept amongst other concepts of CT but can be highly mastered with tangible robotic kits such as KIBO rather than visual-block graphic programming environments such as ScratchJr (Pugnali et al., 2017). However, Strawhacker et al. (2018) reported that children ages 5–8 indicated mid-to-high levels of ScratchJr programming comprehension on assessment tasks on programming concepts and skills of symbol decoding, sequencing, debugging, and goal-orientated programming. Especially for the debugging concept it was observed younger children displaying poorer comprehension than older ones.

Although Gomes et al. (2018) used digital games to examine the effects of their interactional affordances for teaching computing concepts on children’s aged 5–7 the interesting evidence regarding debugging was that children developed the strategy of erasing the entire program and starting from scratch rather than trying to fix the program. Furthermore, Relkin et al. (2021) found that the group of students who did not receive the CAL curriculum, which includes the aspect of debugging for the development of CT, scored higher than the CAL group; it is assumed that is more likely that problem-solving skills was learned through another school subject that increased NO-CAL group’s debugging skills.

Teaching debugging using tangible robots and coding toys is suggested as an appropriate developmental context for early childhood education (Angeli & Valanides, 2020; Bers, 2018a; Flannery & Bers, 2013; Silvis et al., 2021). For young children, debugging using tangible toys involves solving problems at the program level and physical materials (Fields et al., 2016). However, most of these devices use a logo-like programming language, which encompasses a complexity of syntactic knowledge of external modalities (buttons, tiles, blocks) that children encounter in their learning to code and debugging (Silvis et al., 2021). Similar evidence is reported by Bers et al. (2014) in a tangible-graphical programming environment where the discovery of errors may lead children to either decide to keep their original goal or switch to an appropriate alternative and in this stage children’s ability was reported higher in activities for simpler than complex modalities of a robotic system (Bers et al., 2014). Furthermore, concurrent physical and programming bugs present opportunities for young children to learn about the broader computational system in which they are learning to construct learning models of their debugging strategies and the types of bugs they encounter (Silvis et al., 2021). In line with this evidence is Nusen & Sipitakiat’s work, when introduced the tangible robot Robo-Blocks which allowed children to step through each instruction and observe the resulting action, thus making programming accessible to young children (Nusen & Sipitakiat, 2011).
