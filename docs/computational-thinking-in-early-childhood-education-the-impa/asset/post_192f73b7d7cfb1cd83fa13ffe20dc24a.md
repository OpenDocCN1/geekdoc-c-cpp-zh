## 4.1. The different cognitive phases of a debugging ‘journey’

The analysis that follows is made according to Klahr and Carver’s (1988) framework that includes different cognitive phases of the debugging process.

## 4.2. Phase 1: Test and evaluate program

By observing the behaviour of all children when coding, the majority follow the process: a) writing a program, b) executing the program, c) debugging the program, and d) retesting and evaluating the program. So we define Variable 1, which records the number of times a child coded a program and inputted and executed it by the robot. Though some children were led to an effective program by their first attempt, using a combination of the taught programming strategies, variable V1 enabled

us to examine the number of attempts as a result of an ‘error’ to test a program and complete it successfully an algorithmic design. In variable V1, children’s efforts are recorded by a scaling and the following categories are created: a) one (01) to two (02) times, b) three (03) to four (04) times, and c) more than four times. The underlying idea was to rank the categories on a scale of one (01) to four (04) attempts to classify two types of programming behaviour: a) incidents that exhibited the programming strategies with potentially more than one attempt and b) incidents that exhibited errors. Obviously, in each category, both types of behaviour appear together. As illustrated in Table 1, 449 of the 526 children (f = 449, rf = 85.36) attempted up to two times to write and execute a program to complete an algorithmic design. As this number of children represents a significant proportion of the sample, it implies interpretations such as that a large proportion of children: a) were led to implementing the corresponding programming strategy (or strategies) or b) identified the error relatively easy, thus were led to an effective debugging. On the other hand, these interpretations apply to a smaller proportion of children, 40 out of 526 (f=40, rf=7.6), who led to coding three to four times. The distribution shows similar behaviour for 37 out of 526 children (f=37, rf=7.03) who show a more significant number of attempts more than four times.

## 4.8. Locate and correct bug

Mapping the cognitive process in Phases 4 and 5, we created Variable V5 (V5_EvalProg_ProgramDebugging). Its subcategories are assigned when a child is led to identifying and debugging errors (syntax, semantic/logical, or both) after executing a program or debugging the code. Mainly, it involves four subcategories that capture children’s behaviour and cognitive process when they locate and correct one or more errors. ## 4.7. Phase 3–5: Represent program, locate bug and correct bug

In these three phases, the user should represent the program to investigate the possible location of an error, in other words, to detect the error and then take action to correct it. This process requires changing and replacing one or more errors and retesting the program. Consequently, a programmer should detect, decode and verify if one or more errors occur. Our study considers all three phases described by two Variables, V5 and V6. Phase 3 regards the representation of a program, so in our study, this phase is met by the program’s visual representation with the ‘pseudocode’. The teaching sequences of educational scenarios ensured that all children would visualise and reflect their verbal algorithmic designs by ‘pseudocode’. To that end, each child could easily recognise when an algorithmic design was unsuccessful due to error(s) by matching each robot’s movement with each command card. Hence, they were led to detect and locate a bug as suggested in Phase 4, the most demanding cognitive process in debugging. At this point, the child must have identified the location of the error and selected the appropriate command to correct the program effectively and successfully solve the algorithm, Phase 5. Children initially process the solution on an algorithmic design

Most of the children, 278 out of 526 (f=278, rf=52.85), presented complete and functional programs, so they did not debug. In the same category fall those children who exhibited the ‘step-by-step’ programming strategy as when in this strategy they didn’t come up with errors. Then, 122 out of 526 children (f=122, rf=23.19) belong to the category where their programs show errors without corresponding debugging.

In contrast, 120 out of 526 children (f=120, rf=22.81) identify and locate the error and consequently successfully correct it, so in this case, it is called effective debugging. However, out of the whole set of children who proceeded to debug (126/526), only 6 children (f=6, rf=1.14) ultimately failed to modify the inaccurate program while attempting its correction. So they do not complete the process and would have to continue again by going through Phases 3–5 of the debugging model.

In particular, it includes four (04) subcategories that record children’s cognitive process to correct an error.
