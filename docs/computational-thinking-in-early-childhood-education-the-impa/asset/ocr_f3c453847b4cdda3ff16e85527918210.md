# 5. Discussion

This study provides empirical evidence from a large-scale study that teaching preschool students (ages 4–6) to code through the tangible robot Bee-Bot can accelerate the acquisition of one of CT’s practices, such as debugging. The current study uses developmentally appropriate educational intervention and is the first to document and report on typologies of debugging behaviours linked to syntactic and semantic knowledge. Our research demonstrates the debugging behaviours young preliterate children exhibit while processing programming without having prior experience but trying to understand if a program is fixable (Papert, 1993, p. 23) when programming the tangible robot Bee-Bot and what processes are emerging in order to identify, locate and correct errors. The children were not aware of what steps a debugging process consists of, nor were they taught so. Thus we may suggest that children were led to debug intuitively and as a result of their logical process to make their program fixable to fulfil their algorithm and solve the problem. Although Cuneo (1986) suggested otherwise in a graphic-programming environment, in our study, the children managed to debug their incorrect programs even though they were not meant to do so, and those programs were more complex than three commands. However, most proba-

bly, tangible programming facilitated and supported this process. In this programming context, we applied Klahr’s framework for assessing debugging abilities and describing strategy aspects to provide indicators of stages of the debugging process matched to preschoolers’ development of CT. The types of errors are categorised in syntactic and semantic knowledge, and the individual strategies children develop when they recognise the existence of an error are highlighted. We have shown how ‘step-by-step’ control allowed children to better articulate and pinpoint errors in the program, as suggested by Sipitakiat and Nusen (2012). We designed the ‘step-by-step’ function to allow children to slow down the execution process and have more time to observe and compare the code simultaneously during the robot’s execution. Without this function, children had to choose whether to pay attention to the floor robot or the ‘pseudocode’ set in a program. The former was usually the case, which often made it difficult, especially for children aged 4–5, to associate the observed problem with the bug in the program. On the other hand, when using the ‘step-by-step’ function, students pushed the execution button to run one command at a time, so they minimised error risk-taking and articulated the algorithmic design to resolve a problem at their own pace.

# 5.1. Identification of an error and reflective process

Firstly, regarding the research question about the process children follow to identify the existence of error and locate it, we gather evidence from Variable 1. As shown, most children debugged the same program more than once but up to two times. The same evidence is shared by Gould and Drongowski (1974), so we can suggest that it is common for this age group to attempt two tries for debugging.
