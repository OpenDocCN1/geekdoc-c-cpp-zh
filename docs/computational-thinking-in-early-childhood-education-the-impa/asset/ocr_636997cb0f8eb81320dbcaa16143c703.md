# ABSTRACT

The present study investigates the debugging process of 526 children in preschool (4-6 aged) when programming a tangible robot. When a child is involved in programming, it is needed to identify and correct errors, or in other words, debug a program. Debugging is a high-level thinking skill and is an essential component closely related to developing Computational Thinking in early childhood education. Though the debugging process is a cognitive function in programming investigated by many researchers in recent decades, most studies are conducted either in primary, secondary education, or higher education concerning beginners or experts in programming. Very few reviews, and some focus on preschool education. Our study set out the following objectives: 1) How do children identify the existence of error and locate it? 2) What types of errors emerge when coding? 3) What type of error is complex for novice programmers? 4) What strategies are developed by novice programmers to debug effectively? and 5) What types of knowledge do novice programmers construct by debugging the tangible robot Bee-Bot? The study follows an iterative model of a design-based research approach. It uses multiple case studies to collect qualitative and quantitative data. The programming intervention was implemented by 30 educators in their classrooms and was based on a scenario-based teaching design. The debugging process was analysed using Klahr & Craven's framework (1988): 1) test and evaluate program, 2) identify bug, 3) represent program, 4) locate bug and 5) correct bug. The main finding is the construction and development of syntactic and semantic knowledge.

# 1. Introduction

# 1.1. Computational thinking in early childhood education

The development of Computational Thinking (CT) is included in computer science and, extensively, is combined with educational robotics due to the common goals and objectives they share. In recent years, the term CT has been introduced in the computer science research community and the computer science teaching community by Wing who suggested that CT skills are not only beneficial in computer science but also in many other fields as a skill that is as fundamental as being able to read, write, and do arithmetic (Wing, 2006). From the emergence of the concept to the present day, an extensive literature has been developed which studies the development of this term and the context in which it can be developed. The concept of CT extends the term 'algorithmic thinking' closely related to programming processes (Wing, 2006). Some years later Guzdial in his viewpoint on Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) proposed that CT is the new literacy of the 21st century, which drawing methods from diverse disciplines would improve teaching computing in higher education (Guzdial, 2008). The educational benefits of CT involve the generalisation of CT to various contexts outside of computer science while strengthening and improving intellectual abilities (Wing, 2010). Potentially, CT's context of skills and abilities find application in all scientific areas due to the applicability of different problem-solving strategies and multiple levels of abstraction depending on the context in which they are embedded (Barr & Stephenson, 2011).

In the US, many organisations working in parallel or exclusively for education for the last decade, have published and reported on the aspects of CT and its development in education. For example, the International Society for Technology in Education (ISTE), advocates that CT is an essential literacy for all students, combining four pillars — problem decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction and algorithms. It involves expressing solutions as a series of steps to automate a process and is linked to programming and coding because CT is the highest thinking order of problem-solving.
