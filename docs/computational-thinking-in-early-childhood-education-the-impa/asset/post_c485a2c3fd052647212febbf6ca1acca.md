## 5. Discussion

This study provides empirical evidence from a large-scale study that teaching preschool students (ages 4–6) to code through the tangible robot Bee-Bot can accelerate the acquisition of one of CT’s practices, such as debugging. The current study uses developmentally appropriate educational intervention and is the first to document and report on typologies of debugging behaviours linked to syntactic and semantic knowledge. Our research demonstrates the debugging behaviours young preliterate children exhibit while processing programming without having prior experience but trying to understand if a program is fixable (Papert, 1993, p. 23) when programming the tangible robot Bee-Bot and what processes are emerging in order to identify, locate and correct errors. The children were not aware of what steps a debugging process consists of, nor were they taught so. Thus we may suggest that children were led to debug intuitively and as a result of their logical process to make their program fixable to fulfil their algorithm and solve the problem. Although Cuneo (1986) suggested otherwise in a graphic-programming environment, in our study, the children managed to debug their incorrect programs even though they were not meant to do so, and those programs were more complex than three commands. However, most probably, tangible programming facilitated and supported this process. In this programming context, we applied Klahr’s framework for assessing debugging abilities and describing strategy aspects to provide indicators of stages of the debugging process matched to preschoolers’ development of CT. The types of errors are categorised in syntactic and semantic knowledge, and the individual strategies children develop when they recognise the existence of an error are highlighted. We have shown how ‘step-by-step’ control allowed children to better articulate and pinpoint errors in the program, as suggested by Sipitakiat and Nusen (2012). We designed the ‘step-by-step’ function to allow children to slow down the execution process and have more time to observe and compare the code simultaneously during the robot’s execution. Without this function, children had to choose whether to pay attention to the floor robot or the ‘pseudocode’ set in a program. The former was usually the case, which often made it difficult, especially for children aged 4–5, to associate the observed problem with the bug in the program. On the other hand, when using the ‘step-by-step’ function, students pushed the execution button to run one command at a time, so they minimised error risk-taking and articulated the algorithmic design to resolve a problem at their own pace.

### 5.1. Identification of an error and reflective process

Firstly, regarding the research question about the process children follow to identify the existence of error and locate it, we gather evidence from Variable 1. As shown, most children debugged the same program more than once but up to two times. The same evidence is shared by Gould and Drongowski (1974), so we can suggest that it is common for this age group to attempt two tries for debugging.

### 5.2. Types of errors

Regarding the research question for the types of errors that emerged when coding it, an interesting finding is the presence of the two main types of errors: i) syntax and ii) semantic/logical. The former type records most children exhibiting a complete programming behaviour, in which syntactic errors are absent. However, a small proportion of children exhibit semi-complete syntactic knowledge; possibly this relates to difficulty building the memory command’s position and perhaps its function. It would be helpful for future reference to relate this finding to the relative age group and cognitive development. The latter type of error is the predominant category of semantic/logical errors belonging to the high-level cognitive process. It found that this type was likely more complex for children to build. The qualitative data analysis shows that the semantic errors relate to a misunderstanding of the location or numbering of the commands.

In contrast, the logical errors relate to the missing programmable robot’s control commands. The number of errors in a program ranges from one (1) to three (3); for debugging them, novice programmers end up with appropriate strategies. Each child appeared to identify one error for each program debugging attempt. Most children show to form semantic knowledge, so we attribute them a well-organised and consolidated programming behaviour.

The number of children presented with the highest semantic/logical errors distribution was divided into the categories ’Semantic/Logical Error_TurnOriDir’ and ’Semantic/Logical Error_NumDir’. This number of children had difficulty selecting a combination of two (2) commands (orientation and direction) in structural parts of a program. Evidence suggests that children tended to delete more commands than needed or even compose a new program to fix the error (Misirli & Komis, 2020). This evidence suggests the error was either made by their difficulty in spatial reasoning or an error in counting (Shumway et al., 2021), even though the scenarios proved effective in constructing spatial reasoning (Misirli et al., 2019). Thus, this semantic/logical error type seems to be the most difficult for novice programmers.

Additionally, we have not observed "initialisation bugs" taking place in the physical, tangible domain where they represent failures to re-set the robot itself on the proper trajectory (Silvis et al., 2021). That would be causing semantic/logical errors as the scenario-based pedagogical design had predicted this kind of device complexity and anticipated adequate activities. Overall, bugs in the programs most often involved turn errors and missing codes (Silvis et al., 2021), but not those involved controller errors.

### 5.3. Strategies of debugging and types of knowledge

Furthermore, regarding the strategies developed by novice programmers to debug effectively, children having already represented a program on ‘pseudocode’ might have been helped to move the debugging process without an effective result. As Ahn et al. (2021) suggest, the learning experience in varied forms (plugged and unplugged) that lead to experiential and meaningful learning can increase accessibility to programming education.

As shown in our study, half of the children implemented the debugging strategy by composing a ’new program’ to complete their debugging process. A ’new program’ is a twofold process of identifying and correcting an error in the original code or writing a new code by scratch to accomplish their algorithmic design.

From the above, we conclude that in Phase 3–5, the preschool children have already represented a program since it was a prerequisite of the teaching intervention, moving some of them to the debugging process without being led to an effective process. As it is shown, half of the children in the sample design and compose successful programs in solving an algorithm. However, a significant number still present difficulties in syntactic and semantic knowledge, made to identify some of the errors they present (syntax and semantic/logical) and are led to appropriate correction and modification of the program. Nevertheless, a significant number of children continue to show syntactic knowledge difficulties. It might be the programming environment of tangible robotics that may not be adequate for consolidating syntactic knowledge. However, the graphical programming such as Scratch, prevents syntax errors as students can drag and drop the code to build programs without making syntax errors (Resnick et al., 2009; Maloney et al., 2010; Qian & Lehmann, 2017), and also is reported that can help novices build a better understanding of some programming concepts (Weintrop & Wilensky, 2018; Chou, 2019). It would be interesting to examine the development of syntactic knowledge in a graphical programming environment such as Scratch using our scenarios for teaching and compare results to the PTD curriculum and children’s lower scores in debugging (Pugnali et al., 2017), as in both teaching frameworks the sole focus was not on debugging. Furthermore, this study shed light to the importance of teachers’ well-designed professional development that needs to focus explicitly on how to use tangible robots and develop programming skills to promote computational thinking whilst providing a developmentally appropriate pedagogical design on planning to teach as suggested by Chalmers (2018).

## 6. Limitations and future research directions

This study was conducted in public schools during their regular school timetable. We were aware that no matter how dedicated teachers might be and successfully teach these contents in their classroom, they had different teaching styles and approaches, although we had tested all scenarios before delivering the professional development in robotics education. Thus, as with any study in a school setting, we acknowledge that this study faced several environmental limitations. Even though each participating teacher taught the same scenario, controlling all factors from different school settings was not feasible; this may have influenced the results. For example, absences for each child were not recorded, which might be one factor that influenced some children to develop segmented programming skills. In addition, while some teachers followed a more constructivist approach in their teaching, as was implied in the scenarios, others more likely followed a teacher-led mode of implementation.

Furthermore, another element to examine is follow-up cases, even though a small number occur, and discuss their programming profiles towards the evolution of syntactic and semantic knowledge. That would probably provide great insight into debugging or other aspects of CT that might emerge along with differences between novices and experienced programmers. Also, investigating pair debugging (McCauley et al., 2008) would be another possibility to expand on the findings of this study in regards to debugging strategies and their evolution. Further analysis would examine a correlation between gender and the two age groups to highlight CT skills and abilities that emerge even though they were not initially planned. Ideally, a focus group of teachers who participated in this professional development would provide more insight into their confidence and subject knowledge in CT education with young children (Chalmers, 2018; Wang et al., 2021).

Despite the limitations of the study described in this paper, post-study evaluation data collected from the teachers show the success of robotics in education. All the teachers mentioned they would participate in this project again. Furthermore, most of them purchased robots for their classrooms or borrowed them from us to continue teaching programming after the end of the European project. Nevertheless, it was not only teachers supporting this positive feedback; post-study evaluation data from children drew attention to the success and enthusiasm reported and asked for exploring more modalities on the robot like the pause button. Therefore, this feedback highlights this process’s widespread pedagogically adequate and educational nature.
