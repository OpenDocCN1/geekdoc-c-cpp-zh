Passionate developers love to have a playground where they can try new ideas, technologies, and techniques. Ask around. See who has a project or is interested in starting one. # Engage with External Communities

Although we have many ways to establish a culture of learning in our organizations, we shouldn't stop there. Why limit ourselves to our small group? Check if there are any technical communities or user groups in your town. Invite your colleagues to go to their meetings. Get insight into how you can contribute.

Exposing yourself and colleagues to so many people and amazingly talented people from the external communities can be a great way to motivate you. In addition, participating in external communities can give you and your colleagues many ideas of what to do in your own CoP.

# What if Others Don't Want to Join In?

Different people, on different projects, in different companies, behave in different ways. Persuading people to change their behavior and to care about something is no simple task. Some even say that it is impossible. If you are going to change an entire organization with your enthusiasm and the knowledge you got from a few books, forget it. That's not going to happen. However, you can help people to discover their path and how great it is to be a developer.

You don't need to change everyone to make the learning more effective or at a better place. That's where the majority of the attempts go to creating a culture of learning. Attempting to change the whole organization (or the department, or the team) in one goal will be bring frustration for those leading. Frustration leads to lack of motivation, which in turn will make you give up. Don't let them.

The following sections provide advice on how to help aspiring software craftsmen to create a culture of learning.

# Be an Example

“It's really annoying,” claims a developer. “I would love to talk to TDD but no one in my team wants to do it.” This is an excuse, not the attitude of a software craftsman. How do you persuade people to do something if you don't do it? The most effective way to inject passion into a team, and help them embrace different ways of working (or different technologies), is to lead. Many developers are not comfortable in working hours, even if they feel it could be a good idea. Having someone who is not necessarily experienced but has enthusiasm leading the way what might be needed to ignite a whole team.

# Focus on Who Count

Not everyone will be moved by your enthusiasm and willingness to change. Focus on the ones who are willing to join. Pair with them. Write tests and review each other's code. In good conversations: share ideas and information. Have with them. A few people are having working well together, and using different techniques and approaches, more will want to join.

# Don't Force

Forcing developers to attend any of the learning initiatives will make things worse. Keep them from the invitations (as you do for all the developers), making sure they feel welcome to join in whatever they want. Never let people who don't want to be in the sessions squash the fun for people who want to be in the session. If people don't want to participate, then OK. At that least you are not discouraging others to participate in future sessions because of a bad experience.

Don't get disappointed or angry with developers who don't want to join. Keep your own motivation, regardless of how many people turn up. As one of the open space guiding principles says, “Whoever comes is the right people.”

# Don't Try to Change Everything

If you are on a team of ten where no one is doing TDD or pairing (whatever you want the team to do or adopt), and after a few months three or four are doing it, be will happy about it. You don't need to change everyone. Big improvements can be made by a small number of people.

# Avoid Consensus Delays

Trying to reach a consensus of when and where to run sessions can be one of the biggest reasons not to have the sessions at all. Although we should always try to accommodate everyone, we just need to say: "Guys, John and I will be in the meeting room Wednesday at 12:00 p.m. playing with Scala (or whatever technology). You are more than welcome to join us."

Many I have seen teams discussing for weeks, trying to reach consensus. It is sometimes just not possible to accommodate everyone. "I want to go to the gym at time," says one. "Wednesdays work from home," says the other. Just pick a day and run the session.

# Don't Ask for Authorization

You don't need authorization from your boss to learn and practice on your own time. This is true in many companies, even during working hours if you don't. You just need to be responsible. No sensible manager will complain when the team is practicing and getting better.

Find an empty room and have the meeting at lunchtime, early in the morning, or late in the afternoon. Just keep a healthy balance between your learning activities and the things you promised yourself. Be sensible.

# Don't Complicate

"Our office layout is not suitable for group meetings. Our room does not have a projector. What about our teams in other locations? How can we include them? These are common challenges I hear from developers who are trying to create a culture of learning at their workplace. Because many of these problems cannot be resolved quickly, the sessions never happen.

You dont say. If your office is not good enough, go to a coffee shop. If you don't have Internet access, ask people to install whatever they need on their laptops before coming to the meeting. If some of your teams are in different geographic locations, have the different teams running sessions locally.

Aim for sessions that don't require too much organization or preparation. If you choose to have a group discussion, there is nothing you need to book a room and calling everyone; the people attending the meeting can choose the topics when they get there.

# Establish a Rhythm

The secret to a healthy community, and keeping the sessions regularly is to have a rhythm. It is much easier for people to come to the sessions regularly. Our open space will be every Wednesday at 5 pm in the board room. Regular meetings are also easier to organize. Find a meeting room and schedule a recurring meeting inviting everyone. Just make sure you find someone to run the meeting when you are not in the office. It is important that meetings happen even if you are away.

It is not difficult to build a culture of learning into an organization. The only thing needed is a passionate developer to start it. Stop making excuses and be this developer.

# Summary

Creating a culture of learning is not only a manager's or Agile coach's job. It's everyone's, including developers. Lack of time or an appropriate place for learning is not an excuse. With no support from management, developers can easily create a culture of learning, environment where they can learn and share with each other. Don't have a place? Go to a local coffee shop. Don't have time? Use your lunchtime or do it before or after working hours. It's not possible to do it every day? Do it once week.

Despite what many companies or managers think, creating a culture of learning is cheap and easy. Hire passionate developers and provide them with a good environment and support, and they will figure out many different ways in which they can learn, share, and bring innovation to the company.

# 14. Technical Changes

How do I integrate technical practices to my team? How do I convince my team to Test-Driven Development (TDD)? How do I convince my manager to let us pair programming? How can I introduce another new language or framework in my current project? These are questions that always pop up in conversations with other developers. Over the years of finding other developers to adopt technical practices or to change their outside is far harder than convincing managers. Many developers suffer when Kent Beck calls it. They think nothing is worth it. From developers to managers, and architects, how to do it is essential.

# Identifying Skepticism Patterns

The first thing that any craftsman needs before trying to change is to identify the types of skepticism. In this chapter we look at different types of skepticism and how to convince them. They tend to fall under:

- The Uninformed: The reason why they don't use tools or practices is lack of awareness. They simply don't know that tools or practices exist. Because of natural tendency to fear change, when new ideas are presented, they are usually rejected without much consideration. Developers in this group usually don't spend much time reading.

- The Herd: They feel they are not experienced enough; they leave decisions to others. They could easily adopt practices or tools if someone else asked for it, but not leaders.

- The Cynic: They like to be argumentative just to prove they are smarter than everyone else. Previous events have made them so frustrated that they just can't escape. Many developers believe knowing is more important than being smart. One of the reasons for them not to adopt practices or tools is that they don't know how to something. It is easier to find ways to undermine and avoid new ideas than to embrace them.

- The Time-Crained: They are always busy and have no time for changes. Developers in this group are usually busy and unable to see the long term. They don't have time to do things right but they have time to do the same things over and over. For them, if they take TDD as an example, writing tests will take too much—it is an extra thing they need to do. But they can't see the amount of time that is spent manually trying to change the code without tests, and all of it, not making any improvements for the long term system. The only thing they don't have time to change is the way they work.

- The Boss: If the boss is not a technical person, she doesn't understand what you are talking about and will fail to sell all the arguments for what you are proposing. The boss managers have development problems. Instead of "creating tools to automate something," you need to say "reducing project costs." If the boss is a developer, one who wrote code age ago, you might even have a bigger problem. "When we need developers, we need managers."
