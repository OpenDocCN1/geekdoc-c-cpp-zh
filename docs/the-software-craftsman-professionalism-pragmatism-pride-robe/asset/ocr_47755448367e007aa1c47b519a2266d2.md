There is also a general perception that recruitment processes take too long. Companies constantly complain that they need between 10 to 30 developers to operate in, giving them the impression that the vast majority of the developers are not suitable for the job. "Developer suitability" is not the only problem. Interviewers do not know what they are looking for or how to find it. Interviewers rarely realize how bad they are at interviewing, and when hiring good developers, they would complain about hiring bad developers. They would be saying that they need a cultural change.

When working for a large company I used to visit teams in different departments and even countries. During these visits, I would work with the developers, run technical sessions, pair-program, and then help them adopt more efficient practices. After that I would discuss my experience with high-ranking managers, who were responsible for the teams. A common question was, "How are they?" Because interviewing takes forever to learn, "I don't really know what to do." I would tell them which ones are the worst or if we can do something about it, or I would give them a list of the ones to replace. One of the managers really pushed me off, for he had to give some names to replace the developers. My answer was, "Sorry but I can't tell you. You called me to help the developers and not to find them. I came back a few days later with a list of developers the same time they became close to. I won't betray them. It's not on I here. Fault of none of them are they. Regardless of how good or bad they are, they didn't just make me applicable here. They sent their CVs, went through their selection process, and were rejected. Whoever is responsible for it. The selection process must be changed and whoever is behind it should be fired.

The identity and culture of an organization are defined by its people and not by its chief executive officer (CEO). Delegating recruitment to HR, recruitment agencies or vendors means delegating the identity and culture of your organization to companies that do not understand or care about it.

# Too Busy to Interview

The main mistake that makes interviewing so easy is that they say to interview: "We can't spend too much time interviewing. We have work to do."

We spend more time with our colleagues than we spent with our ones—which makes it even more important that we trust and have relationships with them. The truth is that if you can't allocate enough time to interview, you'll be responsible for building a great team.

Because the majority of businesses rely heavily on developers, developers play a big part in the success or failure of a business. Developers are the ones who maintain the machine that enables businesses to stay alive.

Since hiring developers is key, the quality of the interviewers is extremely important. Job candidates are always judged from a biased perspective: they will be considered good or bad developers according to the interviewer's own views, values, and technical abilities. People who do not value themselves and believe in hierarchies will not hire anyone better than themselves. On the other hand, people who always seek to do things in a better way will always look for people who are better or at least good as them.

Although we need to allocate time to interview, the whole recruitment process—from collecting CVs to interviewing—can take weeks or months. If not, we wasted a lot of time looking at all-the-other CVs and interviewing many unsuitable candidates.

Time-consuming activities or defining a formal recruitment process is not the solution. The wasted interviewing and wrong candidates is the problem we must address. Hiring interviewing to find four or five is not viable, and delegating the recruitment activity is too risky; the only alternative is to be more specific with our filtering criteria.
