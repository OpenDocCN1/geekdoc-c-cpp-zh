There is a lot we can get from a pair-grouping interview:

- How well the developer decides on tests to write—that shows experience
- How familiar is she with the tools (the integrated development environment [IDE]), language, testing and mocking frameworks, usage of shortcuts, etc.)
- Choice of names for classes, methods, and attributes
- How clean and expressive the code is
- How the developer reacts when the interviewer makes suggestions or comments
- Her thought process
- Her care in not solving the problem for but also how she is solving the problem

However, pair-grouping interviews can be very tricky to conduct and choosing a good problem to work on can be quite challenging.

A common approach is to choose one of the katas publicly available or to create our own kata. Katas are great when we want to use TDD and general clean-code principles.

Another approach is to take an existing code from our own code base, especially one that is badly written and without tests, and ask the candidate to write and make it better. This is valuable for projects with loads of legacy red skills the candidate would need if using it.

It is important to have realistic expectations when conducting pair-grouping interviews. We need to remember that, as interviewers, we already know the domain the candidate doesn't know. We shouldn't expect the candidates to get certain solutions strategies. When we know something, tend to forget how much time we spend learning.

If we provide tools (Computer, IDE, testing and mocking frameworks), we need to bear in mind that the candidate may not be familiar with them. This is not a bad thing. Every mainstream language has many testing frameworks and is supported by many different IDEs and editors. It's common for candidates getting stuck when they don't know the expected environment or IDE provided during the interview. Pay attention to how the candidate reacts to that, and whether they need help. Since this is a pair-grouping exercise, the interviewer should show what the candidate can do in the right direction.

Whatever we do as an interviewer, we should not leave the candidate to struggle too long. We should wait for maximum of three or four minutes, and then ask for suggestions. The candidate is already under pressure, and making her struggle too long will just make things worse. As we can mentally keep track of the points where the candidate struggled, we should give them feedback at the end of the interview.

Pair programming means pair programming and not standing over someone's mistake and applying unnecessary pressure. That's not how we usually work with our colleagues and there is no point to benefit this way with a candidate.

Once I interviewed a very experienced guy. On my machine I had Eclipse, JUnit, and Mockito (a Java mocking framework). He told me he was more familiar with IntelliJ and Mockk. He never used Mockito, so he's at least learning it quickly.

He quickly became frustrated because he didn't know the Eclipse shortcuts. "I'm really stupid using the mouse so often," he repeated, he said. "What's the shortcut for running?" I answered and then he took notes on a piece of paper. He then started asking me about other shortcuts and took notes of all of them. Fifteen minutes later, had on, piece of paper, some of the basic shortcuts and started to feel more comfortable.

He also struggled with Mockito. "If you tell me what to do, I can do it for you," he said. "I want to make this class and that constant every time the public method is invoked," I then wrote the code according what he wanted to do. That helped him to understand how Mockito worked. By the end of the interview, he was using Eclipse and Mockito quite well.

The only thing I regretted about that interview was we were not fast enough to get back to him and he got a job somewhere else.

# Bring Your Computer

Each candidate may feel comfortable with different tools and frameworks. Asking the candidate to bring her laptop to the interview is a good way to see her working with her own favorite tools, and it can save precious interview time.

An interesting exercise is to ask the candidate to clone a project from GitHub—provided that the candidate can connect to the Internet—and work on it. This approach can tell us a lot about the candidate. The candidate familiar with Git? Does she have it installed? How is her computer configured? Which tools does she prefer? Can she start a project quickly? Does she have all the testing and coding frameworks installed and configured? Developers who code outside hours will always have all tools installed and ready to go.

As we are interested to see the candidate's technique—how clean is the code, how she writes the tests, how she approaches the problem—it doesn't really matter which tools or languages she used.

# Tailor-Made Interviews

Before interviewing any candidate, we should ask: what are our values? What sort of attitude do we expect from team leader? The answers to these questions form the acceptance criteria we use to hire them. But that doesn't mean we shouldn't explore others. It's always good to verify how versatile the candidate is, as we don't reject a candidate for things that are not important to us.

I was once contacted for an opportunity at a major startup in the UK. The role was for a server-side Java developer and the project had no experience. The project seemed interesting and I was comfortable with the vast majority of the technologies listed. I decided to apply.

The interview process consisted of two phases: an online multiple-choice test and an interview with the project manager leader. That was a very bad start because believe online tests are stupid and totally inefficient. One of that, didn't have to talk to any of the developers during the selection process, there would be no way for me to judge their knowledge, code values, and the real situation of the project. I don't want to continue with the process.

A couple of hours after I finished the test, the HR department called me. They told us we still debating whether we would call me or not for a face-to-face interview, I said that I scored 88 percent (26/30) but the team would only interview candidates who scored at least 90 percent. "Not a problem at all," I said "Please tell them."

The preselection process is extremely important. That is where you filter candidates according to your core values. Judging candidates by online-choice tests is definitely not how you do it.

# Taking a Punt

Once I interviewed a candidate in his early twenties. He had two years of experience and was still working for his first employer. His CV had no mention of TDD or Agile—things that are common nowadays. His CV was quite with almost nothing in it. Less two years of experience at a single company. It was a CV that I wouldn't normally pay attention to. However, there was one line that really made me curious. He had been a member of a Linux user group since he was 16.

I invited him for an interview and, as we spoke, he told that he was working for an organization where Agile and Extreme Programming were not allowed. He also mentioned he was trying to TDD as a project. "How many projects do you have? I asked. "Five," he said. We spent the rest of the interview with him about his projects, his approach to TDD, the challenges, things he would do differently, and so on.

Although he didn't have experience in the technologies we used and had basic TDD skills, I could see how he passed to new things. He was using his own time to learn things he could not do during working hours. He was a good guy, given the right environment and opportunity, could really shine and become a great developer.

We were looking for more experienced developers but I could not let him. One year after he joined our team, he became one of the best developers we had.

When interviewing, we should look for talent, attitude, passion, and potential, not knowledge of specific technologies.
