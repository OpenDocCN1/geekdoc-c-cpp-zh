# Good Interviews

A good interview is like a good and informal chat between passionate developers. It's an exchange of information: a good debate about techniques, tools, challenges, and approaches to software development.

My first interview was at an investment bank, the type of company I could never work for,. The interviewer said, "I read some of your blog posts and I liked the topics you covered. However, I didn't agree with certain things you wanted to discuss."

We spent more than one hour discussing the things I had written, tried to find scenarios where things could not be applied, and the challenges to some approaches, given different contexts. It was interesting to see the things that we agreed on, things we didn't agree on, and most importantly, the things that none of us had ever thought before.

There is no way to prepare for an interview like that. Either you have experience on the topics discussed or you. Googling for some stuff before the interview or reading a few articles is not enough to prepare a candidate for an open conversation like that. And that's exactly the point of interviews: showing how the candidate really is.

# The Right Focus

Don't waste interview time talking about things that are not relevant. On what is important and more valuable to the company.

# Mind-Mapping a Conversation

One technique I find very useful when conducting interviews (or should I say conversations) is mind-mapping. I use a few pieces of paper and a pen, and I start the interview with an open question. What is well-crafted for you? What are the mind maps in a software project? In these examples, well-crafted software could be the root of my mind map.

Open questions that lead to be answered with a list of things like maintainability, testability, readability, performs well, satisfies requirements, and so on. Each item in this list becomes a node, hanging off the mind map root well-crafted software. With that, we can start exploring one of the nodes and mapping the conversation against it, creating new nodes for what is said by the interviewer or the candidate. Whenever I'm happy with the conversation about one of the topics I can expand it to nodes that are hanging off the mind map. That was back, you also mentioned that. Could you expand that?

If the conversation deviates in a way that I don't find useful, I can look at the map and choose another node (topic) to explore. If I want to have a conversation about a completely different topic, I create a brand-new map on a different piece of paper, or other node under the root node.

If I want to be more specific, because the conversation doesn't get to where I want it to, then I ask another more direct question: "What do you think about TDD?" This will lead to another interesting conversation captured by the mind map.

Once the interview is finished, I have the entire conversation mapping, which helps me to remember every conversation I had with each of the candidates.
