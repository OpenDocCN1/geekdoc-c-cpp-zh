
# Focus and Determination

Vojra said, "You've got to be careful if you don't know where you're going because you might not get there." We software craftsmen, value and control our careers. We understand that a career is a life-long journey, depending on the choices we make, which may or not lead us to mastery.

Defining the direction we want to go in in our careers is important. However, this is a long-term goal and many things can change. Think about your career if you are managing a multilayer project. Once we understand the project vision—the ultimate goal—we take the most important requirements, break them down into smaller pieces, and iterate. While working on small iterations, we also re-evaluate the project goals and adapting when necessary. It's the same with our careers.

# But If We Don't Know Where We Are Going?

It sounds simple but we do not always know where to go next. Actually, defining the direction we want to take our careers can be an extremely difficult thing to do. We don't decide it once and for all. We need to constantly evaluate our career decisions based not just our passion but also all the events in our personal and professional lives.

We need to be where we are in relation to our goals and our dreams. We need to create situations where opportunities can be presented to us. No one will knock on our doors and offer us an opportunity if we keep ourselves isolated from the rest of the world, locked inside our houses and offices. No one will offer us a opportunity if they don't know who we are, what we do, and most importantly, how we work. We need to go out and speak to people. We need to show other people what we can do. We need to be comfortable with technical practices, and to talk about what we want to do next, create an environment where other people and companies can present us with some options. When we have a few doors, and a few options available to you, you will be in a better place to decide what to do.

Here are a few things we can do in order to create opportunities:

- Expand our technical knowledge, studying things that are out of our comfort zone, like new languages and technologies.
- Attend user groups and community events.
- Network with other developers and businesspeople.
- Blog about what we are learning and doing.
- Contribute to open source projects.
- Develop projects and make them publicly available.
- Attend conferences.
- Present at a conference.

The things described above create situations where we can receive options that we were not considering before, and this could help us decide what to do.

# Job as Investment

The definition of a great career in software development is very personal: each of us have a different dream, aspiration, and different person has it. However, the process of career aspirations is very similar. The most important thing is to treat every job as an investment, a step toward a bigger goal. Software craftsmen value their ever-increasing knowledge, passion, dedication, and professional attitude in every job they do. Therefore, for like any other type of work, we need to be clear about what we expect as a return, and we need to measure it regularly throughout the length of the investment.

After a few months of interviews and networking, I finally accepted a job in an investment bank. For many days, then I doubted myself that investment banking was totally compatible with Agile and Software craftsmanship principles, making them a no-go for me. I also thought that I would be totally constrained by their bureaucracy and their hierarchies. However, I realized I could help them adopt technical practices and bring new ideas into the existing ones. I was looking for a job where I could find some of the gaps that I had in my career. The return on investment I was looking for was experience in developing software at a very scale, with teams all around (the world I was not familiar with), and opens doors in an industry that was closed to me—usually you need to have previous experience or a financial background to be hired as a high in an investment bank. I wanted to see how effective of all my preferred software development projects and techniques would work. Exposing myself to an unfamiliar environment allowed me to learn and discover different ways of doing things. That was the return on investment I was expecting from this job.

> Note

Expecting a return on investment should not be mistaken for the selfish and unprofessional attitude as "CV building," where developers don't care about their jobs and are to improve their CVs, choosing technologies and methodologies for personal reasons and not because they are fit for a specific purpose. Also, software craftsmen who care about and respect their customers.

Knowledge is the most common reason for investment in a job. Developers choose jobs according to what they want to learn, and they leave jobs either when they are not learning or whatever it is to learn is not aligned with their careers.

Our lives also play an important role in our decisions. We want just more money, and that's OK. If our career can be significantly improved with more money, we should invest in a job that can give us a better income. Some want just more stability and security, if we are starting a new family or our family is about to grow. We are tired of traveling all the time. Sometimes we need a job where we don't work extra hours, so we can spend more time with family and honing our craft

When it comes to our career, is it right or wrong? But there is something worth keeping in mind: knowledge is forever; money, stability and security are not. If, whatever reason, our job is terminated, knowledge and experience are the only things we will take with us. If we always focus on learning and becoming better craftsmen, jobs that can provide us with a good income, stability and security will be much smaller task than if we have spent our careers focusing on getting more money.

# Autonomy, Mastery, and Purpose

In Drive: The Surprising Truth What Motivates Us, Daniel Pink says—that what makes a job of the table—workers are motivated by three things: autonomy, mastery, and purpose.

- Autonomy: it is when we are in control of what we do, how we do it, and when we do it. That will always be the case in a true Agile context
- Mastery: it is when we are constantly learning and evolving—becoming better professionals and better human beings
- Purpose: it is when we feel that our job is important and we are contributing to making things—instead of just doing what we are understanding why.

The assumption of money being off the table is not important. When our basic needs are not satisfied, it is very difficult to focus and do what we love. The feeling of being undervalued always leaves a sour taste in our mouths. Even when we work for the company, we are just questioning whether we are fairly treated or being undervalued.

Software craftsmen choose jobs where they have autonomy, mastery, and purpose. These are key aspects that help any person to have a sense of satisfaction. Choosing jobs for the money that can bring our careers to a halt, making it impossible to put it in motion.

For me, autonomy and mastery are absolutely non-negotiable and deal-breakers. If I felt that I wouldn't have one of them, I wouldn't accept the job. If I had a good job and felt I lost one of them, I would leave the job.

Purpose is not as cut-throat. It's to work on projects where we know how things will be better once the project is finished or realized and where we are motivated to feel that we are contributing to a greater good. However, I've been on projects where the entire project, no features or technologies, was questionable. No one could really explain why we were doing it. Businesspeople were too busy to speak to us and no one knew the real users it was.

# Career Inside Companies

It's very exciting to start new jobs when we choose them carefully. I was sure would have a great and long career in each of the jobs I started. From the outset, all the companies looked great. One way or another, I always ended up with a disappointment and had to leave.

There were a few exceptions. Some companies that I worked for were really great, but unfortunately, after a few months there was a mismatch between what I wanted for my career and what they could offer me.

Our careers will be more important than any specific job or company. Pursuing a career inside a company is not the same thing as pursuing our others. Our careers are like long staircases and the jobs are the steps. They are not even. Some steps (or jobs) have different heights. Others are longer or shorter. Some steps are longer and higher. It depends on how well we do on our homework before de joining a company. It may also depend on changes in our personal situation or aspirations.

If we are working for a company where we have autonomy, mastery, purpose, and the job is aligned with our own aspirations, then pursuing a career inside company is the right thing to do.
