
# The Problem with the Manifesto

There were a few criticisms related to the Software Craftsmanship Manifesto. The main one is that, unlike the Agile Manifesto, the Software Craftsmanship Manifesto is almost impossible to disagree with. In the Agile Manifesto, the contrast between the left and right side is clear, indicating what is being proposed. A person who believes that documentation is very important struggles to accept 'working software over comprehensive documentation.'

Companies that are used to fixed price and detailed contracts would struggle with 'customer collaboration contract negotiation' in the Software Craftsmanship Manifesto. There is no contrast between the left and right sides. The right side is an evolution of an already accepted left side. A person who agrees that working software is better than comprehensive documentation will never disagree with the value of well-crafted software. Companies that are better in collaboration will never disagree with productive partnership.

This is a fair point. I don't think anyone who believes in Agile values can really disagree with what is written in the Software Craftsmanship Manifesto. However, if no one disagrees with the Software Craftsmanship Manifesto, it is fair to assume that everyone agrees with it. If that is the case, the question we should ask ourselves is do we always work and behave to what is written in the manifesto? Are we really practicing the things on which we agree?

In my view, the manifesto should not be read as if it was just a bunch of lines put together. We must go deeper to fully understand.

# Summary

Software Craftsmanship is about professionalism in software development. It's about software developers striving to always do their best and provide good service to their clients. Software craftsmen seek productive partnerships with their clients and employees and not an employer/employee relationship. Software craftsmen understand that being a professional means having a good reputation, and for that they need to constantly delight their clients by delivering successful projects.

Software Craftsmanship is a mindset—a lifestyle that many professional developers adopt. Software craftsmen live and breathe software. They see software as a craft and are committed to do whatever they can to master their craft.

# Who Owns Your Career?

What if the company we work for does not buy us? If the company never sends us to any training or courses? Or will it be that we could learn new things ourselves? Does it really mean the company is bad?

Imagine that you have a problem to solve in your house. Imagine that you need a lawyer for any legal issues, or a doctor or a dentist when you have an aching tooth. Normally you go to these professionals when you have a problem, so imagine turning them back and saying, "Can you make me a book? Can you send me on a course?" What would you think about? To make things even worse, imagine that you, for some reason, decide to buy the book or send them on a service, and they acquired the knowledge you gave them, they come back and charge you for their services. How does it sound to you?

These professionals need to invest in their own careers so they can do a good job, satisfy their customers, and hopefully receive other clients. They need to satisfy their current clients. Those that don't end up losing clients, receive fewer rewards forever, and will slowly be forced out of their business.

On the other hand, factories work, for example rely on training. Factories need to train their employees to use new machines and perform work. Factory workers have no say in what machines the factory should buy; they are just to be told what to.

Clients pay with the expectation and experience of receiving a good service. They pay professionals to solve the problems in the best way possible. Clients don't pay for learning. Clients pay for their knowledge and skills. Professionals are expected to provide solutions, viable alternatives, and ideas to their clients. Professionals are expected to help their clients achieve whatever they want to achieve in the best way possible, and that is how they build their trust and reputation.

We have all wanted to be respected as professionals before we realized that we need to start behaving like professionals. That means that we should use our own time and money to get better at what we do. We should show our own careers and what we learn and what we earn. We should be in a position that we can help clients and employees to achieve their goals. Those who only rely on their company knowledge are not professional software developers. They are just factory workers in disguise.

> "Companies should not be investing in their own people?" you may be asking. No, that is not what I mean. Companies should invest in people but software professionals do not see that as their obligation. That should be a bonus, a win-win situation. Companies that provide time to get better at what they do are much better and can become far more efficient. Passionate developers will always favor these companies when choosing who to work for.

Our industry moves faster than any other. Languages, frameworks, practices, and processes are constantly evolving. Keeping up to date and constantly getting better at what you do is key for a successful career as software craftsmen.

# Books, Many Books

Having our own library, or electronic, we are very lucky to be in an industry where so much information is produced. However, there are many types of books, and choosing which ones to read is a difficult task.

- Technology-specific books are very valuable but they expire. They are essential for immediate need when we want to learn a framework, language, or any other software we need to use. They are great at giving us deep understanding and how knowledge can be used immediately. They are also great when we are planning our next projects. They can give us details of how to use technologies that may be between our current job and desired job. However, many of the technology-specific books get old quickly. When a new version of the technology is released, or a different way of doing things happens, they will not be as valuable as before. Examples would be books about Hibernate, Nodejs, or Code.
- Conceptual books are those that give us the foundation in our careers. These are the books that get us introduced to concepts, patterns and practices. The knowledge we acquire through this type of book cannot always be applicable; it may take a significant amount of time to digest the information and become proficient. Often a quote or language may be used to explain technical concepts but the knowledge we get can be applied broadly. Books covering topics like Test Development, Domain-Driven Design, object-oriented design, functional programming, or modeling with NoSQL databases are examples of such books. The knowledge we get in this category is to be far more helpful than learning a specific technology, and it may take many years before we get comfortable with them.
- Behavioral books are the books that make us more efficient when working in teams and better professionals in their field. They help us learn how to deal with people, clients, decisions, team members, and so on. By knowing programming languages, frameworks, and practices is not enough if we want to be a good professional. We also need to learn how to deal with everything that is not related to our job but is also part of a software project or organization. Books in this category will cover the more human and professional side of software development, including topics like Agile methodologies, Software Craftsmanship, Lean software development, philosophy, and management.
