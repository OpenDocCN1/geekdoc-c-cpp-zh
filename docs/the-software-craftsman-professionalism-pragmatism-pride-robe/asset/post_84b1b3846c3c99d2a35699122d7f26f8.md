
Agile changed the way we work. The old, hierarchical, and very specific segregation of roles within a software project team started fading away. Software professionals realized that writing code is just one of the things they need to be good at. Developers, who before were just understood the importance of getting involved with business and delivering value to customers—the realization that the team as a whole was a game changer—made software professionals evolve. Instead of being specialists who could write some decent code, developers started taking an active role in planning, estimation, requirements, team organization, analysis, architecture, production readiness, prioritization, demands, and collecting feedback from users and stakeholders on a regular basis.

## People Empowerment

Software teams become flat in hierarchy. No more team leads and segregation of duties. Instead of managers assigning tasks to identify and specify the amount of time that it should take to be done, now we have a backlog of tasks that is prioritized by sponsors and product owners of the team. The team decides which task to work on next, according to priorities, and who is going to do what, how it is going to be done, how long it is to take. This all happens as part of an iterative process where the feedback loop for all people is constant.

## Professional Vision

To fit this way of working, software professionals had to evolve. Instead of just being specialists, like in the past, companies started to hire specialists. Being good at writing is the minimum skill expected from software professionals. Testing, analysis, to understand the business, good communication, and a more extroverted personality became part of the skill set software professionals need today.

## Agile Manifesto

The following is an excerpt from Agile Manifesto.

> We are uncovering better ways of developing software by doing it and helping others to do it. Through this we have come to value:

- Individuals and interactions over process and tools
- Working software over comprehensive documentation
- Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
- Responding to change over following a plan

That is, while there is value in the right, we value the items on the left more.
Besides the Manifesto, the origins of Agile also came up with twelve principles.

## Principles Behind the Agile Manifesto

The twelve principles include the following:

- 1. Our highest priority is to satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software.
- 2. Welcome changing requirements, late in development. Agile processes harness change for the customer's competitive advantage.
- 3. Delivering software frequently, from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference to the shorter time scale.
- 4. Businesspeople and developers must work together daily throughout the project.
- 5. Build projects around motivated individuals. Give them the environment and support they need, and trust them to get the job done. (translation error in source but kept as is).
- 6. The most effective and effective method of conveying information to and within a team is face-to-face conversation.
- 7. Working software is the primary measure of progress.
- 8. Agile processes sustainable development. The sponsors, developers, and users should be able to maintain a constant state indefinitely.
- 9. Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility.
- 10. Simplicity—the art of maximizing the amount of work not done—is essential.
- 11. The best architectures, requirements, and designs emerge from self-organizing teams.
- 12. At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, it tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly.

## The Agile Transformation Era

Agile was a revolution in our industry and, bit by bit, companies adopted it. Consultancy companies and individuals became specialized in helping companies go through the Agile journey. The role of the Agile coach emerged. Agile coaches to companies; analyze their problems, processes, and people; and help them to change in order to become more agile.

During Agile transformations. Scrum, combined with a few other specific techniques, was normally the methodology of choice. What was a great entry point into Agile world. The notion of self-organizing teams was all for many companies. Accepting the idea of having teams backlogs, defining and splitting stories, estimating with priority features at the end of each iteration was a massive step forward. Communication was also improved. Teams started to talk to each other and with their customers much more often—another agile thing in certain companies.

Providing an environment where people interact with each other helped companies to solve problems and understand weaknesses and strengths of teams and individuals. Agile processes helped unite team members, providing them with a common goal.

Agile processes definitely helped companies move forward, not only exposing their problems but also providing a feedback mechanism that allowed them to react and adapt.

## The Agile Hangover

For a few years, companies and teams were in this Agile transformation part. Companies were transformed. Meetings became stand-up meetings—although some companies have them in home, setting down for one hour. Burn-down charts, iteration backlogs, and release backlogs became the new project management tools. We became user stories. Project managers became Scrum Masters And Agile became the new name for their way of working. But some changes were negative. If you looked at offices from before and after the Agile transformation, the difference could be massive.

We often had ten or more people standing around a single computer, and whiteboards everywhere. Some companies have everyone with whiteboards, colored in color Post-Its. And what was Agile meant to many companies. The new color of Post-Its on a whiteboard became their true measure of Agile. The very Agile companies could be identified by their use of Post-Its, of course, or different thing. Anyway, everyone could see the color Post-Its—some moving with some time they could do something well, a bit faster, but not at all), and everyone had that feeling of freedom and empowerment. Freedom meant could do whatever he wanted or what she wanted—as long as he or her boss lasted. And one day, after a few months, or in years, some cases of Agile ended up with a massive Agile hangover.

They realized that after all this, they were still not delivering software with quality enough fast. In fact, many of the teams and companies still had the same problems they had before the Agile transformation. They were taking a long time to do anything and still producing what they needed. They had technical debt, even in projects that they started from scratch in Agile. Motivation was a big problem. The quality assurance (QA) teams were still needed. Many companies had a period of (a few days, to a few weeks) after each change in a sprint their code base became a mess and was slowing them down. The size of logs was not large enough. They still had applications that were not well documented and everyone was scared to touch it. Debugging and log analysis were their main techniques for figuring out what the hell was going on. Managers were still bad at deitting applications just a few weeks after they had been written, which was slowing the whole process.

The new applications in Agile, were also badly designed, complex, and with the help developed development teams were still needed to ensure the changes that were desperately needed by the developers. For example, they had a bad architecture and were not talking to each other constantly. In terms of the actual technical work they did, nothing had really changed. The same problems were still there: stagnant skill, low morale and motivation, mountains of technical debt, lack of unit tests, unscalable release process, system loss, discovery of bugs, unnecessary tests every long cycles, and requirements were not understood until they were started to ask. 'What went wrong?' 'That was not what we were.' At the end of the day, the main reason to change the whole process and adopt Agile was that they could have a better return on the investment in their software.

## A Partial Transformation

Over the years I have seen, and was part of many Agile transformation projects. I have seen many companies strongly that they wanted to adopt Agile but not much effort to actually be agile. They wanted a prescription: something that they could follow to magically make them better. Many companies and teams claimed that Agile does not work. They went through this Agile transformation and were not really much better than they were before.
