
## 5. Heroes, Goodwill, and Professionalism

In the 1990s, I got a job in a multinational company. After two years working for small softwares and a long selection process, I had the feeling that I had found my dream job. We were creating enterprise resource management system (ERP) similar to SAP, and our clients were governments and very large companies. The team was extremely talented and the project was massive compared to everything I had done before. I was quite intimidated by the skills of the developers on the team. They all seemed much better than me, making me feel like I had to do better.

With more than a hundred developers working on the codebase, the project felt enormous. At least the back-end was developed in a proprietary language and database running on mainframe, and the middleware was in C++. I could only understand a little of it. Also, my English was not good enough.

The project started more than ten years before I joined and the effort was so big part of the project from mainframe to Windows which started three years before I joined. There were also initial talks about moving the system to the web, a massive undertaking for the teams, since they were mainly developers that had nothing else to change the application that this would be the first large web project in department. All the pressure was on us to make this translation challenge.

The project was being developed in the typical waterfall fashion and during the two and half years I spent there, I never had to speak or meet any of our clients. I didn't know any of the developers I ever met. I had no idea who these people were or what we were doing. Although I never had access to the reals, I learned from conversations over coffee, that the clients complained about large number of bugs we had to fix, and the pace made me every time the client wanted a new installation or upgrade of our software.

The company employed two developers (myself included) who lived one hundred kilometers from Sao Paulo (the capital), and they provided us with a car for the commute. For that to wake up at 5 and be at peak point at 5:30 a.m., I would usually be back around 8 p.m. Because overtime was not uncommon we often had to de leave late and get home at midnight, or take my coach home, traveling by public transport and going home past midnight.

Things got really ugly. We had this crazy deadline to deliver one of our system to the last guest. Due to the time-difference and the level of desperation, we had to work late. I decided to work hard, 90 days with no time-off just to get on the track. The company didn't pay for my gas or tolls, as they were already providing the free. In two months we worked more than 24 hours in a row more often than I could count. Once, I had to be in the hotel for a few hours and I was too tired to do more than 30 minutes sleep with just sleeping in a sleeping bag. I woke up with the phone ringing in my car to ask what was the next. Of course we were never paid anything. Finally, after meetings with the company, they decided to pay for a hotel room near.

Many friends from the business school told me that what we were doing on the architecture was ridiculous, working so hard, sleeping in the car, and not even paid overtime. "What are you doing, Why do you put up with it?" they said. But in my head, I just said, I was single, living on my own and loving my job.

Here, I was learning a lot, enjoying the company of my experienced teammates, and I loved what I wanted most-massive and good quality code.

Today, I look back and I understand why that work was not important. We never tried to understand what the clients really wanted or what could potentially offer different alternatives. Although we never had access to the clients, we questioned the requirements they either. We never said it would be impossible. We just got on with our jobs because we thought we were developers.

Deep down we wanted a lot of recognition and fame. We wanted to be like the guys who saved the project. The guys who did the impossible. Wells. Well, in the end, all that effort was for nothing. The deadlines were missed and no one in the team really cared how hard we worked. We didn't become famous. We were just a bunch of low-pay developers. We managed to leave the company for some reasons, which they explained, they decided to move on, working normal hours during the time. The developers were the only ones working like us to make people we didn't know good luck. With a top-down, command and control structure, most decisions were impossible to us. We were just the factory workers doing the heavy lifting. But we really felt good about that. Our team was awesome and it was a pleasure to work with many talented guys.

Today, I know what we did was wrong. It hurts me to remember how we were treated. It hurts me to know that this situation is still very common, in many countries that offshore work. It frustrates me to know that we didn't professionally–we never knew what we were doing or what the real problem was. We tried to provide an alternative. Allowed ourselves to be treated as factory workers. We behaved like factory workers and made mistakes during long hours, exhausting the entire process. We were unprepared with a very poor understanding of the real issues and did not create situations where we could provide solutions. We allowed ourselves to be deprived of a personal life and even ended discriminating against those who decided not to leave in the park or just 12 hours a day. We were not acting professionally because we said NO.

## Learning to How So

More than not, we have tight deadlines and a lot of pressure from our managers to meet them. Many of these deadlines are unrealistic and imposed on developers by managers who were very aggressive in trying to make what they are delivered on time. Developers, the younger ones, tend to succumb to pressure and say they will get things done by the imposed date, because they are afraid to go against their managers or because they want to avoid conflict. When they are unable to deliver all the features they imposed, developers end up ending with conflict.

The outcome of this is usually very bad. Systems are riddled with full of bugs, and people are unhappy and full of distrust. After working hard for many days, and sacrificing time with their loved ones, developers feel undervalued by some manager who pushed them to do what they knew impossible, while he was at home enjoying his weekend with his family, they blame their incompetence. "How the hell did you break the sign-up page? Why isn't the system performing well? How on earth is the system not able to process 10,000 transactions per minute? How could you forget this edge case?

## An Epic Failure

I worked for a large marketing company. The team wanted to launch this brand-new mobile portal we were developing. They wanted to launch it in three different countries and this estimation was a small percentage of our customers would use the application from day. Even being a small percentage, the company had 20 million customers in.

The deadline approaching, and with so much work already into it, we said, "Sure, we will do it." The final day was a Monday morning and the application went live. Our last deployment to production had been a few hours before. We were tired and wanted the whole thing to be over but at the same time we were extremely proud of ourselves, knowing that we saved the project. We would be considered heroes. We had few conversations in the middle of the night, during a coffee break, asking for raises and promotions.
