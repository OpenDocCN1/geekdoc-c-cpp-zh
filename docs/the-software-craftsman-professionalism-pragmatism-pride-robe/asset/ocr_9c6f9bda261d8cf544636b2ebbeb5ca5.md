I worked with few teams in Eastern Europe—my mission there to disseminate the Software Craftsmanship ideology and attitude, and also to introduce the teams to Extreme Programming (XP) practices. Helping developers to get better at writing code, one of my main goals was to insert some passion into the teams and help them to create a culture of learning.

While working with them, "What are your career aspirations? Where do you want to be in a few years?" "I want to become an architect. Another want to be a manager," one said. Other developers said head of department, product owner. No one said, "I want to be a developer." I said, "Why not a developer?" I asked them. An awkward minute of silence with a strong accent, one of the guys said, "Here, if you are a developer when you are over 30. I am 35," I said loudly, and I felt very proud to be a developer.

From that onward, the developers seemed freer. They were enjoying what they were doing, pairing with each other, making a good effort to test tries, trying to make their code run well, internal sessions, really getting better at writing code. They realized that you could have a successful (and a lot of fun) career that you love.

Unfortunately, that was not a situation I encountered only in Eastern Europe. I see similar situations in Asia, Brazil, and also in the UK and the US. We have companies that treat developers like second-class professionals, who are just there to type off on the keyboard. Developers, on the other hand, think that is how they work, and they do want to be up in their careers, from development, so they can be treated as professionals.

Fortunately, this mentality is changing. Many there recognize how important software developers are for their businesses, and they offer excellent packages to attract the best ones. Today, every company is a software company, and companies that fail to understand will struggle to compete in the future. Companies that fail to recognize developers as high-skilled professionals will never have craftsmen for their team.

# Different Leaders

Every person has a career leader. Software development is: we are at the start and move upward as we become more experienced.

Climbing up the software development ladder is a challenge. The skills needed to become a great manager are not necessarily the same ones needed to become a great developer (and vice versa). Developers, for one reason or another, decide to take roles as managers or architects are not climbing the software development; they are switching developers.

# Roads and Milestones

There is a difference between following a career within a company and following your own career as a craftsman. A successful software craftsman carefully plans her career; she looks for opportunities where she is to learn the things she wants to learn in order to become a better professional. Different people have different aspirations and these aspirations require planning the next steps is key for a successful career.

Moreover, looking for a new job. "I will offer my career knowledge and time in exchange for the highest salary I can get." That's very shortsighted. For professionals who are interested in leading and successful careers, a job is for more than money. It's a constant investment in their careers.

Besides the things for what we do, we should also value all our dedication, passion, commitment, and what we acquire. Working outside hours to make the workplace a better place—where everyone can live and learn. Investing in working out a better environment creates more opportunities for enriching our owners.

I never focused on just what was specified in the job I was listing. "That's not my job." In fact, I don't ever look back at any job description or consider before being hired. I tried for hours to get more of, to do more, and to make sure everyone around me happy as much as I could, regardless of what role or position was. This is what I my investment. It was an investment not only in my job, but also in my individual career, but for my personal life, and for the world that I worked in. Some people value certain technologies or industries; experience with different types of projects; opportunities to develop skills, different roles, and have different types of responsibilities; or even more money were all that at some point in my career I wanted a start. Money was not my priority—good software professionals will always have money when they need to earn a little. I always did my best to provide as much as I could—the company's t-shirt, as we say back home—and got my return back.

I changed some jobs by time during my career, spending two years in each company. There were a few exceptions though. There was one company where I stayed for five years, and another company where I stayed for three months.
