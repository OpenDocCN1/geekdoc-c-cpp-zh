## A's Perspective

It is vital that developers learn how to use interviews as an opportunity to learn more about the company and the people they will work, potentially, working with.

There are many things to ask. Who is the interviewer? Is she a project manager, a developer, a team leader, or an architect? How many people are we being interviewed by? Is it a single-phase or multiphase interview process? What are the questions of us being asked? Is the interviewer following a script with predefined questions or having a conversation? Are the questions specific or open? Is the interviewer more interested in binary answers to questions or more interested in knowing us?

It's important to filter out all the potential expectations we have. Every company is great when we talk to recruiters, Human Resources, and interviewers, paying attention to details can help us see what the situation reality is.

Our first hint that the management does not trust developers, and that formal hierarchies govern the government, is when we are asked to make decisions. So whether developers are asked to make decisions or if a few at higher positions take all the decisions.

Single interview processes always make me a bit weird. It is because when the company is in a hurry and cannot find the right people. A multiphase interview process usually means the candidate is analyzed in different aspects of the candidate's profile and are more about finding a more well-rounded.

Analyzing the questions asked during an interview can be very valuable and accurate. The questions asked explain what the interviewer is looking for. When we join the team, it is common to hear that if there is a chance, nothing wrong with asking how relevant the questions are to the actual role offered-as long as they are relevant, and not in a confusing manner. The interviewer will feel relaxed and respond to this question. Some interview will be annoyed by the question. We should be reluctant, and so will be able to explain it. Some well will help you naturally and explain the reason for the questions asked. The interviewer behavior will show what happens when they react. What would you work with? Would you feel comfortable to make decisions?

If the interviewer is asking very specific and short questions, then one after another, one of paper that shows she is not willing to explore new ideas and engage in a debate. She already has a perception of what a good developer should look like. It shows that a process, even when it does not make sense clearly is not the best approach. An undirected conversation would make me think that the whole may not be open to different ideas.

Judging developers by what they have an exact answer—APIs, tools, technologies—is wrong to do. Instead of having an open conversation about real and concrete scenarios, exploring different approaches and solutions, and of code review, the best way is to determine a candidate experience.

When visiting one of our teams, we were invited to be observers in one of their interviewers. The interviewer has a spreadsheet with a few questions that seemed not to match to the letter while interviewing the candidate. The questions were very specific and with each answer, not leaving any for openings.

It was quite robotic, with just no real interaction between the interviewer and the candidate, quick success of short questions and answers. Each question answered would mark a mark on the printed paper. The spreadsheet could tell me if the candidate was passionate about his profession, or if he had experience to do the job.

When the interview was over, the only thing we knew about the candidate was familiarity with Java APIs and frameworks, but we had no idea if the candidate could use them to write well-crafted code. Any inexperienced developer who had read a Java book or for a certification in the first few months would pass with flying colors.

As we walked back to the desk, the interviewer asked me not to tell anyone the questions he asked. He didn't want the questions to leak.

If we are scared people know what we are going to talk about the interview, probably it is because we are doing something wrong. If the interview is composed of questions that any person could answer correctly after a quick search on Google, or after reading the APIs of a language or the documentation of a tool, the questions have absolutely no value in identifying good or bad developers.

Interviews like that show the lack of creativity and openness of the team. It shows that, for them, there is just a small set of tools they can use to develop.

## Good Interviews

A good interview is like a good and informal chat between passionate developers. It's an exchange of information: a good debate about techniques, tools, challenges, and approaches to software development.

My first interview was at an investment bank, the type of company I could never work for. The interviewer said, "I read some of your blog posts and I liked the topics you covered. However, I didn't agree with certain things you wanted to discuss."

We spent more than one hour discussing the things I had written, tried to find scenarios where things could not be applied, and the challenges to some approaches, given different contexts. It was interesting to see the things that we agreed on, things we didn't agree on, and most importantly, the things that none of us had thought before.

This was my first interview out of five, but after it I already knew I would like to work for that company. There is no way to prepare for an interview like that. Either you have experience on the topics or you google for some stuff before the interview. And that's exactly the point of interviews: showing how the candidate really is.

## The Right Focus

Don't waste interview time talking about things that are not relevant. On what is important and more valuable to the company.

## Mind-Mapping a Conversation

One technique I find very useful when conducting interviews (or should I say conversations) is mind-mapping. I use a few pieces of paper and a pen, and I start the interview with an open question. What is well-crafted for you? What are mind maps in a software project? In these examples, well-crafted software could be the root of my mind map.

Open questions that lead to answered with a list of things like maintainability, testability, readability, performs well, satisfies requirements, and so on. Each item in this list becomes a node, hanging off the mind map root. With that, we can start exploring one of the nodes and mapping the conversation against it, creating new nodes for what is said by the interviewer or the candidate. Whenever I'm happy with the conversation about one of the topics I can expand it to nodes that are hanging off the mind map. That was back, you also mentioned that. Could you expand that?

If the conversation deviates in a way that I don't find useful, I can look at the map and choose another node (topic) to explore. If I want to have a conversation about a completely different topic, I create a brand-new map on a different piece of paper, or other node under the root node.

If I want to be more specific, because the conversation doesn't get to where I want it to, then I ask another more direct question: "What do you think about TDD?" This will lead to another interesting conversation captured by the mind map.

Once the interview is finished, I have the entire conversation mapping, which helps me to remember every conversation I had with each of the candidates.
