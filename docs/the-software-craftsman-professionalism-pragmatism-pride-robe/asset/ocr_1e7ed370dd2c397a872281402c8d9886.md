# Constrained by Lack of Motivation

Every company has every single employee, without effort, could easily come up with a big list of things they wished were better. But, if employees know what to be changed and how things could be improved, why don't they do anything about it? Here are the most common reasons, in no particular order:

+   * They don't feel empowered to do it.
* They don't want to be the ones pushing it.
* Nah! Too much hassle.
* They don't believe things could be changed.
* No one agrees on what a better thing is.
* They don't care. It's just a job.

The reason why many companies fail to improve the way they work is low motivation. There is no way we can change an organization when people are not motivated and don't care about their jobs. Before trying to convince people to work in a different way, ask yourself: are they motivated to do a good job? Do they really care about improving the way they work? Are they constantly trying to get better at what they do? Do even believe in like what the company does?

# Injecting Passion

Embedding craftsman in a team, either hiring them or bringing in external craftsmen for a period of time is the most effective way to motivate developers. The differences between regular development and software is that craftsmen are on a mission. They are on a mission to make things better, to deliver value to their clients, and to inspire people around them. They are not afraid to lead the way and develop. For software craftsmen, striving to always do their best is just a natural state of mind.

There is nothing more annoying for a developer than having a manager or an architect dictating what she should do. Instead of telling others what to do, software craftsmen can sit and pair with other developers, sharing their knowledge, experience, and passion. Besides getting the work done, they are always keen to act mentors to other developers. Software craftsmen are always looking to share their ideas with the team to help them do better.

While working in an unfamiliar bank, we joined many other things, to inject passion into the team. We were a few senior developers but many of them were not really excited about their jobs. They were coming to the office doing what they were asked to do, and going home. Although we were in the middle of an Agile transformation, they had loads of bugs in their systems, and we were deploying software only once or twice three years, and had very little innovation to develop.

We have done many things over the last years since I was there, but there is no book on Agile transformation and change. I'll focus on some simple ones that ended up having a very good result.

One of the first things we did was hire a craftsman to join us. The bringing of a few craftsmen to the team that we were all allergic to really helped to inject passion and bring innovation.

We used Scrum and at the end of our daily stand-up meeting, we started: 'Has anyone learned anything from our stand-up meeting?' In the first week one developer would answer and yes then shared something we read in a book or blog post, some we discussed and we tried the right approach, or some code we had written. As weeks went by, more and more developers became motivated to learn and share in our daily stand-ups. We shared blog post links, videos, and books. All of them, and some manner of conversations about software, techniques, practices, and some possible solutions to some of our problems. We experienced the new craftsman and they wanted to be part of it.

I bought a copy of Seven Languages in Weekend by Bruce A and started going through the first chapter (Ruby) and spent a few times during our stand-up, coffee breaks, and pairing sessions. At some point it the developers asked if they could join me when I was ready to start the language (Prolog). We then agreed that we would meet every Wednesday at 12 pm and spend our lunch together. After each session, we were excited and wanted to talk about it. More and more developers got interested and at one point we had 12 developers to coming to the sessions and some of them joined us.

Things like completely changed our environment. Developers were excited by technology again and were keen to get better. They were all more inclined to pair-program and try new things. Many innovations were brought to our department, many technical problems were fixed, the number of bugs reduced dramatically, and we started deploying production more often. The general mood was so great that even product owners, project managers, and production services noticed it. We were happy to work with the developers as well.
