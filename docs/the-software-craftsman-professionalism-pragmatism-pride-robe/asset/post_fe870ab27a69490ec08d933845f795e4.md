## A Job Is Not Just a Job

For great developers, their job is not just a job; it's their hobby and passion. They feel fortunate that we pay well to do what they love. Since a job is more than just a job, great developers will work for companies that think in the same way and provide them with an environment where they can shine and have loads of fun.

## Recommendations

Recommendations should be encouraged but not financially rewarded. Attaching a monetary amount to the recommendation system triggers more motivation—which invalidates the whole process. Developers want to work with good developers and learn from them, and for that is the only thing that matters. They recommend the recommendation system as an opportunity to bring ideas and learn from them, which is great for the company and also for their professional growth.

Software craftsmen understand that, by recommending others, they put their reputation on the line. When a software craftsman makes a recommendation, it is implied that the recommended developer is a software craftsman, and that he she shares the same passion, values, and dedication.

## Community Involvement

The best way for companies to hire talented developers is to be in contact with the wider community. Supporting users groups and technical communities is a great way to attract and approach great developers.

The chief technology officer of a small profitable company in the financial sector came to one of the first Software Craftsmanship roundtables we ran as part of the London Software Craftsmanship Society (LSCC). He came to demonstrate the company's testing framework, which is now open. Later months the company became one of the LSCC sponsors, honoring one of our monthly meetings. We also sponsored the London User Group (LSUG).

It had a few advantages in sponsoring LSCC and LSUG:

- **Exposure to developers**: Many here directly, sharing their time and money.
- **Opportunity for developers to learn from**: Many of them attend our hands-coding sessions, where we pair-program different solutions, and they also attend our Software Craftsmanship roundtables, where we discuss various topics in an open-face fashion.
- **Free technical advice**: Their developers have the opportunity to talk to other developers about how to solve their problems.
- **Cheap investment**: It takes just a few hours and office space once or twice a month.

One of the advantages of actively supporting the community is that we can spot talent and approach directly. The team is there for developers. They can analyze the culture of the company, the developers working there, the types of applications they work on, and how they work. Because user groups and technical communities meet on a regular basis, it is easy for both developers and companies to identify opportunities together.

For months, if not years, developers and companies have the opportunity to know and help each other. A close contact is mutually beneficial—it can lead to business partnerships, employment agreements, and a great deal of knowledge sharing.

## Defining Effective Criteria

Relying on recommendations and community involvement is not always enough. Which means that we need to use the conventional channels, like job boards and job descriptions. When that is the case, we are back to our original problem: not enough time to interview.

Large organizations and well-known companies tend to receive CVs every time they advertise a job position. It is totally impossible to interview every developer, so we need to create filters to filter out those who have a better chance of satisfying their needs. The filter criteria can be quite vague and non-specific. HR or recruiters, when in charge of the filtering, will do it by a combination of keyword matching and some random criteria like years of experience or academic background. When hiring managers, they analyze the candidates' previous experience in each role, the companies and industries, and all the experience written by the candidate describing her role in each of the previous CVs.

Once I was recruiting for an investment bank. We were looking for an experienced developer to join our team. The agencies and vendors for us sent some CVs, and at some point, had more than 50 CVs that looked exactly the same. They satisfied all the items in the investment bank job description—similar to the first job described earlier in this article. Because each interview may take a few days, if not.

I started to think about what I really valued about a developer and what type of culture we were trying to create. We wanted software craftsmen. Passion was the most important criterion, even when compared to technical and programming languages. I wanted people who were constantly learning, who took the time to learn new things, who loved to share what they think, and who were active in their communities.

There is no job description that could describe what I wanted: "I want you to find passionate and talented developers." They kept me to push them, segmenting them, and I replied, "If you want keywords to match a CV, I'll give you one: 'passion.'" The majority of them never got back to me.

Whatever filter criteria we apply, we will always filter good developers. If we say we filter out loads of talented developers who have never worked for an investment bank. It would be if we asked for bachelors' degrees in computer science, experience with a specific framework, or a certain number of years of experience.
