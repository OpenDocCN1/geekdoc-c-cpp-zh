
When it comes to driving change, especially if the change is about attitude and practices TDD, nothing will help you more than the ability to lead by example. There are certain things for you cannot just find a manual, read it, and start doing it. TDD is one of those things. Software design, clean code, motivating people, inspiring people, with pressure, working with customers—managers, business, or external clients—are not things you can just point out with a few internet links to your team and expect them to be good at. Disciplines and practices take years to master, or at least a few months before you can reach a point where you are comfortable with them.

Developers may reject certain practices because they are too difficult to master or become comfortable with. On the other hand, hand-worrying about deadlines and productivity drop—“our lack of expertise” as an excuse not to support and promote certain practices.

We can help developers overcome initial fear by sitting down and writing code with them. Developers will feel more comfortable adopting a practice if they know they can count on us to help them. From a manager's perspective, one will have to tolerate something if it feels the team (or at least a few individuals) has the skills, keep a constant velocity, and deliver code with the same (if not superior) quality.

If you want to change the way we work with a technology or to practice, first make sure that you do not doubt yourself. Leading by example will help you deal with the majority of skeptics—they would have a hard time rejecting your proposals if you successfully demonstrate your technique and method them.

## Choose Your Battles

> If I had the power, and could work without disturbing me, and could hire and fire whoever I wanted, I would change this entire organization in a very short period of time. This is what crossed my mind many times during my career. However, this is not reactive feeling. And above all, it's not going to happen. One-man army won't win a war, so choose your battles carefully, one at a time.

After a few weeks of working in that environment, I had a list of things that I wanted to change. The entire list was quite a lot given the effort required to tackle them. I wanted to change small pieces of code here and there, then throw away and rebuild applications, fix how we deal with teams and vendors, reduce bureaucracy, and many other things. The list of the changes we wanted to make would take months, if not years. Trying to do everything at the same time wasn't possible but also impractical.

One of our primary goals was to change the culture of our department, the first thing we changed was our recruitment process, making sure any new person joining our team was already aligned to the culture we wanted to foster.

The second (that happened in parallel with the first one) was to introduce TDD and our programming to it. This was a long battle, not only with some, but also with a few different managers. It took us all to be comfortable with it and to drive an improvement in our systems. In early proprietary black-box frameworks, which had years of investment, were thrown away and replaced with open source alternatives.

Certain changes had to be done before. We had to wait for some changes to be in place so we could start tackling the design and architectural issues, restactoring of our applications, introducing new technologies, changing our process, and many other things.

Before all these changes, we had to consider the impact of these changes—to speak of people, and answer the questions from the team. In order to build trust with our bullies, we had to be clear about what we wanted to achieve and our criteria. The criteria can be a bit fuzzy, quite often driven by gut feelings, but we tried to put some thought into it. Changes would bring us a list of benefits in a very short period of time. Others would bring us from in the future. We had to weigh the types of faces we were fighting. In a political and bureaucratic environment, being clear is not the only thing you need in order to drive changes.

If you don't manage to change the development process of an organization, large or small, if you don't prepare yourself for it, don't fight all the battles; they are not equally important. Prioritize the battles that your efforts on will bring you more value.

## Iterate, Inspect, and Adapt

One of the easiest ways to follow the new design you are proposing is to propose gradual changes. Instance of saying, for that, from now on ever you suggest that in the next sprint, and another developer will choose to try and a new way. After the story is finished, and demoted to everyone, the team can decide if they want to adopt the new design.

It is important to include people in the decision-making process and provide a feedback loop mechanism where everyone has an equal say. We can use the iteration boundary trick—“Try this approach for one iteration and then we resist.” When people feel they are included in the process, they will be far more inclined to collaborate than to fight each other. Proposing gradual changes reduces the amount of resistance and minimizes the risk—changes we done gradually, iteration, with a well-developed feedback loop. Another advantage of this approach is that the majority of the skeptics, with the exception of the Oriental, will be disarmed. Let's just try it for iteration, in a small part of our software. We can then have a discussion about something concrete, and if we don't like it, we revert. There is nothing decided (yet). No sensible person would go against that, especially when this discussion happens in front of the whole team.
