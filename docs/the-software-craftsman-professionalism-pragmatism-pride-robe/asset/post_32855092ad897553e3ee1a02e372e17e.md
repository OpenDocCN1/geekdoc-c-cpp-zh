## About This Book

After decades and many different methodologies, software is still failing. Why they fail, there are a few things that cannot be ignored; companies see software development as a production line; not how to manage projects and how to hire good developers, and many developers still behave like unskilled, unmotivated workers, providing very poor service to their employees and clients. With the advent of Agile methodologies, the software industry took a step forward, but the percentage of failing projects is still incredibly high. Why are we all failing? Why are we so bad at delivering successful projects? What is missing?

Although the term "Software Craftsmanship" has been around for over a decade, it was not until it emerged as a viable solution for many of the problems faced by the software industry today.

Software Craftsmanship proposes a very different mindset for developers and companies. Although Software Craftsmanship is not a methodology, it strongly recommends the adoption of certain technical practices and disciplines, mostly the ones defined by Extreme Programming. With a great synergy with Agile and Lean principles, Software Craftsmanship promises to take our industry to the next level. Professionalism, technical excellence, and customer satisfaction are the main focus of Software Craftsmanship. One of its main focuses is changing the perceptions that software developers are like workers on a production line and that software projects can be run as a factory. How can we become better developers? How can we deliver better projects? With real stories and practical advice for developers, this book is relevant to all developers and every professional involved in a software project.

# Part I. Ideology and Attitude

### 1. Software Development in the First Century

I remember my early career in the 90s. After those years, I considered myself a senior developer. I could code in at least four programming languages and most importantly, I could understand Windows interfaces (APIs)—that few developers were able to understand. That is how seniority was measured back then. If you couldn't understand a piece of code, normally it was because you were not senior enough. And without stupidity, it was common to hear managers and developers say—“We can't change this code right now, we need to wait for Henry to fix it," because no one else can understand.

After years in the industry, I was done being a developer. It was time to move on with my career. I didn't want to be called a programmer or any coder. From that point onward I would call myself a software architect. At that time I was working for a business and our projects were large Java Enterprise Applications (JEE). We were also using Rational Unified Process (RUP), and had to struggle with entities and sequences. After some time with this great company, I was promoted and moved to the architecture team, where we had to draw classes without using Design Patterns. Besides the Gang of Four (GoF) design patterns and JEE patterns, I knew Unified Modeling Language (UML) and Java certifications. I had more experience than skills and credentials to be called a software architect.

My job as an architect was to speak to business analysts, understand the functional and non-functional requirements, and create diagrams that specified what developers had to do. I also had to think hard—very often using my crystal ball and checking the direction with my finger—to come up with the right architecture. I had to predict the needs of all the features that would be added to the system in the future, and it was impossible to predict exactly how the system would grow. The solution to this problem was to add design patterns all over the place. Today we call this over-engineering and stupidity.

After a few months on the architecture team, I realized that I was far more connected to my teammates in the development team than the others. I was constantly having lunch with the developers, talking to them, and also showing them how they were using different parts of the application. I also realized I was spending far more time than not using code; now I know it was because I was not writing code at all and I missed writing code and debugging.

After one year just being decoupled from documentation, I had enough. My boss wanted me to move back to being a developer as a technical architect. You know how many developers would like to be in the architecture team? You know how many of them wanted to be like me? Of course, I was a developer before. I knew how many of them wanted to be architects. “This will be a very easy problem for those who want to be told,” they said.

You have many developers you can choose from to join me. One week later I was back in the development team. Life was good.

I realized how important that decision was. I spent weeks trying to figure out how the development team was the right thing to do. On one hand I had the “status” of being an architect in a large company, seniority (recognition you don't write code, you tell people what to do), superiority (compared to other developers), visibility (by management), career advancement, and responsibility (looking at the big picture). On the other hand I loved being a developer, creating diagrams, writing code, and discussing long-term tasks that very rarely satisfied the test of time. Then I made my decision: to choose happiness. Happiness was being able to write code every day I wanted. From that point, I promised that I would only choose jobs and positions that would make me to wake up in the morning and go to work.

Decisions can change our careers personally and professionally. If I had decided to remain on the architecture team, I would probably have struggled to become a developer again. The longer we stay away from coding, the harder it is to get back to it.
