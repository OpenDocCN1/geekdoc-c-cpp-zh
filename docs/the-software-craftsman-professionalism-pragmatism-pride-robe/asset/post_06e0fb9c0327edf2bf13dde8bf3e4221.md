Low morale can destroy a company. Unmotivated people have no energy to create and bring innovation. Embracing Agile processes and ideology is essential for providing the right environment for improving morale, but when it comes to developers, we need more than that. Managers and Agile coaches have a very low chance of motivating developers because they are not close enough to them. ## #13. Culture of Learning

No change can be made effectively if the people involved are not motivated or don't really care about their jobs. Over and over again I have seen managers trying to define strategies to make the organizations more efficient. And there they would go, with their PowerPoint presentations, telling everyone how change would be. They would also say, with a big smile, what everyone had to do with the new policy. "From now on, developers must do Test-Driven Development (TDD). Development teams must do Agile. Everyone is now expected to follow our new 'process'." Developers would ask themselves: "What does she know about writing code? Could she sit and pair with us? Does she understand the details well enough to test our work?"

But let's not underestimate people's ability to make things worse. Some people would comply with the new (and improved) way of working, managers would make that type of people's objectives and bonuses. However, when it comes to software development, this approach can lead to disastrous consequences.

In this chapter we discuss many different things we can do to create a culture of learning. All the things described in this chapter have been used in large financial companies and also in few smaller companies in various industries, with positive results in all of them.

## Motivation

There was a time when a well-known investment bank hired the consultancy firm for work. For they had a specific requirement: "We want some developers who can unit test their code to base." The consultancy asked me to be one of these developers. I was surprised by surprise, but on the bench; I couldn't really say no. For a few months colleagues and I wrote unit tests for the code without ever telling any of the developers who wrote it. We just said, "You need to write the tests. Your target is to get the test coverage up to 70 percent." However, we had no idea if the code had a bug or if the behavior we found was the desired behavior. And we tried to do it, but that was not part of their job and they were not interested in talking to us.

To make things even worse, the developers working for the bank were not keeping the tests up to date as they made changes to the code, not writing tests either. Every single time we updated with the latest version of the code, we found that many of our tests were broken.

After a few months of blindly refactoring tests, without having a clause if the code was fit for the purpose, we managed to keep the test coverage above 70 percent. A few weeks later, the bank was happy and the contract terminated.

We learned a lesson: we will never change an organization by forcing people to adopt a new process or something. ## Creating a Culture of Learning

Creating a culture of learning is one of the most efficient ways of injecting passion into a company. Not many developers have been exposed to environments where their senior colleagues were constantly looking for ways to improve themselves. Many developers started their careers in places where senior developers didn't like what they were doing or were not motivated. Due to a lack of role models, junior developers are left to believe that development is not an exciting career, and like theirs, they start planning how to become managers or architects; they believe this is their natural career progression. These young developers, as they join our industry, have to deal with much and unnecessarily complex code produced by their seniors that, for them, creating presentations on PowerPoint and spreadsheets on Excel seems more attractive than writing code.

On the other hand, if the same developers had been brought up in an environment where their seniors were always trying to get ahead as they do, getting excited every time they found a better way to do things or learning new tricks—these developers would have enjoyed their professions. With highly motivated developers who favor doing what they do and who are constantly looking outside their own cubes, companies could benefit immensely from all the innovations and efficiencies they would bring.

However, instead of many managers trying to improve how, when, and what developers should learn, companies should foster freedom which may lead to lack of interest. Let developers decide how, when, and what they want to learn. With this freedom, developers have a much better chance of creating and building a culture of learning. The better the developers, the more innovative and agile the company can be. If the company becomes a place where developers are happy, schedule the meeting.
