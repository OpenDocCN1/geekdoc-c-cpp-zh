# Summary

Driving technical change is hard. Very hard. To be successful, we need to understand how to deal with all the different types of people we will need to interact with and convince the way. These people are not stupid; they just have a different opinion and different reasons for believing what they believe. Their opinions may be based on previous experiences, access to information you don't have, lack of confidence, or even fear.

Being good at what we do, being able to communicate clearly, and, most important, being able to establish trust are essential skills for any developer willing to drive changes. Understand who you are speaking to. Understand the reasons for each person's thinking. Prepare yourself. Be brave. Drive.

# 15. Pragmatic Craftsmanship

There is an assumption that quality takes time. Managers and developers believe they need to choose between expensive well-crafted code that takes a long time to make or cheap code that is delivered faster. Unfortunately, cheap average code to deliver usually overwhelms developers over-crafted code.

In this chapter we bust the myth that quality is expensive. We will also discuss, with examples, what pragmatic craftsmanship means.

# Quality Is Always Expected

I've met a person who wouldn't want quality if quality were inexpensive or if quality didn't need to be a trade-off for earning whatever she judged important at some point in time—like time to market or cost. Managers expect bad quality regardless of what they say, they will always expect high value. When developers tell them how many times quality will suffer if they want to keep the code and deliver faster, they will not in mind but they will expect quality.

The main problem is that cheap and average code is good enough—maybe in the short term, but not in the and long term. For a project that usually uses average code, more often than not, average means very poor. As the big build of crud, cheap doesn't mean the number of bugs increases, but time to deliver features becomes more and more, and managers and sponsors don't see that. In software development, software is a service just like any other service they pay for. And any other service provider, developers are expected to deliver quality. They expect to deliver valued features.

Think about yourself when hiring a service. Imagine you are hiring a builder to extend your house or to renovate your house. The very least, you expect a well-educated guide and price range. You want to know how much it is going to cost before they start the project. You also want it to be clean and efficient. You want to know when the new kitchen will be finished as soon as possible. If the builders don't look that and cheap, you can't guarantee quality. "If you want us to do what we are asking us, you may have some problems in the future." What would you do? If you refused to say you would choose quality over time and price, then again, quality.

It's also reasonable to assume you want to get the quality you want but you have a limited budget. If the building company that you chose would not need to pay more for the quality you wanted, or the project would take two or three times longer, what would you choose? There are many circumstances that may make you between keeping quality high, even if you need to pay a premium or tight budget. Maybe you and your wife are having twins and the extension needs to be finished before they are. Perhaps you don't live in the house but need a new house so you can rent it out as soon as possible.
