Encourage simplicity. Your idea or proposal must be simple and clear. Organize your thoughts before proposing anything. It is very easy for people to understand; use examples whenever possible. The ability to communicate is key if you want to have your ideas accepted.

**Speak the language.** Depending on changes you are proposing, you may need to speak to owners, managers, architects, product owners, business analysts, or any other person who can be affected by them. Learn how to speak their language. Do not talk about details of your code or frameworks with managers and product owners; tell them about the benefits your proposal has for the project: reducing maintenance costs, more reliable and frequent releases, and so on.

**Do your homework.** Make sure what you are talking about. Research, theory, and practice. Think about what the skeptics will ask, and make sure you have an answer before you speak to them. If your proposal has any drawbacks, mention them before anyone does. If there are areas where you don't know enough to be clear about them, explain the drawbacks that you thought about the problem and increases your credibility.

**Be respectful.** Don't treat others like they were stupid. Disrespect and aggression will immediately put people into a defensive mode, making the task of convincing almost impossible.

**Learn to listen.** You may have a very good idea of how things should be, but make no mistake, you are not the only one with ideas. Everyone has an opinion and sees the same problems from different angles. Perhaps, there are many things you are not aware of. Listen and digest what everyone has to say before you make any judgment.

# Where Do We Start?

After a period of time in a new project or organization, ideas for starting pop into our heads like popcorn in a microwave. And once we identify all the different types of skeptics we face, it is hard to imagine how difficult it will be to push our ideas forward. So where do we start?

# Establish Trust

We can't change if people don't trust us. The best way to build trust (with fellow developers, delivery managers, clients) is by consistently delivering quality software. The trick here is to understand what quality means. For others, it may mean that we have written meaningful and readable tests, and that there are no duplicates—that is, if the code is easy to maintain. It may mean software that satisfies their requirements, has no bugs, and is delivered on time. For clients, it may mean software that is in production, saving them, saving money, or protecting their revenue.

Although essential, consistently delivering quality software is not enough to build trust. You can't change if you don't have passion. People need to recognize that you have something interesting to see. You need to be visible. You need demonstrate how passionate you are and how much you care about the project and what you are proposing. People are far more inclined to follow someone who is passionate than someone doesn't exhibit anything.

But showing your passion is not enough. Above all, you must establish trust, to be what they need. People need to feel that you can do it. They need to feel you can lead them.

# Gain Expertise

When changing, we will always lose teams (including managers, product owners, etc.) that the future will be much better place if we do the things we are proposing. While these things—adopting a new technology, changing the design of an application, or adopting TDD—will have an initial cost. Skeptics will immediately say that it is, it will cost too money, or it will take too much time.

Before proposing a new methodology, make sure you really understand it. Play with it, build something with it, talk to experts, and compare it to other methods. Make sure you can do what you need to do with it, and above all, can you teach it to others. You need to find some people out who want to use their time to become an expert in a new technology or to master a new practice. Most people don't want to learn them. You can speed up their experience by learning the learning curve yourself and then lead them.

In gaining experience, you can inform the Uninformed; The Herd will join you, then they will know what you are talking about; the Cynic can be disarmed if you answer questions with confidence and demonstrate a deeper knowledge. The more knowledge you have, the better it will be for you to understand what happened with theed. With this understanding you can identify where the energy was and where it went. Gaining expertise gives you more ammunition to engage in discussions with the Ivory-Tower Architect and the Fanboy. And if it all fails, you will have more knowledge than you had before, which makes you a better professional. For a software craftsman, this is a win-win situation.
