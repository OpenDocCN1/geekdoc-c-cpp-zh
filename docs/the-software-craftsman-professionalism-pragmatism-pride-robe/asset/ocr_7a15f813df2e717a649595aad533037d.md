# # Crossing Borders

On February 26, 2009, the first international Software Craftsmanship Conference was held in London. Also in London, in May 2009, Adewale Oshine held a seminar for the aspiring software craftsman, and Enrique Rieiesen started The Wandering Book, a book that travels from craftsman to craftsman, the current thinking, the zeitgeist of the Software Craftsmanship movement. Software around told the world about what it meant to be a software craftsman and mailed it to other craftsmen. The craftsmen writing in the book wanted to take pictures of their entries and post them on it. Unfortunately, after traveling around a few countries, the book was lost and went out. It is possible to find some information about the Wandering Book in a few blogs.

In October 2009, the first Apprenticeship Patterns: Guidance for the Aspiring Software Craftsman, by Adewale Oshine, published. This book was one of the first books to publish, with practical advice, software developers to look after their careers, purpose, and their profession.

From forward, Software Craftmanship Conferences have been held every year in the United States, Kingdom, and from 2011, in Germany as well.

# # Craftman Swan

In April 2009, Lyth and Olivia mentioned many of the details about the swap. The Chicago Tribune covered it on June 15, 2009, mentioning the mutual respect between the two companies, in terms of their technical capabilities, which made them believe they could learn a lot from other. Many, as a first reaction, would think this is utter but in reality it shows that the companies have a different way of seeing things. "I like not really competing against each other but against the people we there [whose] want is to get as much money as they can... just putting out freely without thinking how software has lived for a long period of time," - Cory Hames, who helped to organize the swap and was the originator of the idea.

> "Initially there were concerns from both sides on how it would work and we were all very excited," said Tyler Jenkins from Obama. "We experienced that they couldn't get anything else anywhere. We [send] someone who was both an experienced developer and someone we thought represented the Obama view."

The developers involved as normal developers, working with other developers and doing real work. They wrote code, in meetings, and behaved as if they were working for the other company-at least during that which helped them learn new patterns, new styles, new languages, and so on.

> "Experience - I know that the benefits to the company were not necessarily visible in the direct sense, but for the those who participated and the community," said Tyler. During the conversation I asked him if he would recommend other companies to do the same and if he had any advice." He said, "It makes a lot of sense for software development companies to do this, especially if they're not in direct competition. It works well especially if they are careful, thoughtful developers, a.k.a.'craftman, a.k.a.'crafts,' should swap regularly. There are many different ways to approach a problem successfully and it has been my experience that each group has things more than the others. Swap lets companies to take the best practices from each to improve their own."

In my view, this was a starting point for the industry as a whole. Initiatives like this software developers to get better and help companies to be more competitive. producing products and solutions.

# # Software Craftsmanship Communities

Although there was some noise about Software Craftsmanship since 1999, it was in 2009 that the field flourished. Besides communities and a few conferences, the Software Craftsmanship movement really gained traction with the creation of a global list and a few communities in the United States, London, and a few others in Europe.

In December 2009, Levi founded the Israel Software Craftsmanship Community. In August 2010, David Green and I founded the London Software Craftsmanship Community (LSCC).
