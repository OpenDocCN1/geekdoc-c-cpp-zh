## Software Craftsmanship Communities

Although there was some noise about Software Craftsmanship since 1999, it was in 2009 that the field flourished. Besides communities and a few conferences, the Software Craftsmanship movement really gained traction with the creation of a global list and a few communities in the United States, London, and a few others in Europe.

In December 2009, Levi founded the Israel Software Craftsmanship Community. In August 2010, David Green and I founded the London Software Craftsmanship Community (LSCC). SoCrates 2011, Craftsmanship and Testing conference in Germany, several Software Craftsmanship communities were founded: the Paris Software Craftsmanship Community was founded in October 2011, the same time the Softwarekammer started with Software Craftsmanship communities in Germany. The Germany are now more than than fifteen communities around the world. Many other communities have been created around the world since.

The LSCC is currently the largest and most active community in the world with over 2,000 developers, organizing around four to five events per month, and an annual Software Craftsmanship conference–SoCrates UK. The LSCC also serves as a menu to help others organize communities around the world they started.

## The Software Craftsmanship Manifesto

Trying to avoid out the anticlimactic aftermath of the 2002 Software Craftsmanship Summit, Micah Martin really wanted to come out of the Software Craftsmanship Summit. He wanted something to write down and a craft of some kind that he could call his own. They discussed, among other things, what it means to be a craft and what it means to be an apprentice. They also discussed the document who would be written for, what the contents of it would be, if the document already existed, and if it needed to exist at all. The ideas were captured on a whiteboard and they came up with lots of ideas, there was far too much and it was far too much shorten to a finished document. Before closing the summit, Micah asked everyone to sign on what they had on the whiteboard

Doug Bradbury went into action and proposed the outcome of the summit in the group and collected feedback. In February 2009, Doug made an email "The New Left Side," which started to get the values and was refined into the actual Software Craftsmanship Manifesto. There was a debate about the Agile Manifesto and the Software Craftsmanship Manifesto, which was critical in moving forward. The manifesto they discussed was "The New Left Side" by Doug Bradbury and "Side Revisited" by Scott Firth.

The discussion revolved around the question, "Why a Software Craftsmanship manifesto?" Cory Haines agreed saying, "By making a community, publishing a manifesto, beginning on establishing principles and schools of thought, we are creating a new type of developers who can see. Those who are really interested in apprenticeships, journeys, and programs. In some cases, this will need someone to foster these ideas, hopefully following their footsteps then they create a new manifesto for themselves."

It was a fascinating conversation which shaped the final language of the manifesto. The whole discussion could be read in the Software Craftsmanship group. After a few weeks, Doug presented an elegant manifesto based on the ideas discussed, with the structure of the Agile Manifesto. It was great, and the community in general approved it. Within another couple weeks they built and published a site where people could use "The Manifesto for Software Craftsmanship."

## The Manifesto

As aspiring Craftsmen we are raising the bar of professional development by practicing it and helping others learn the craft. Through this work we can come to the following value:

- Not only working software
- also well-crafted software
- Not only responding to change,
- but also adding value
- Not only individuals and interactions,
- but also a community of professionals
- Not only design and architecture,
- but also productive partnerships

That is, in pursuit of the items on the left we have the items on the right to be indispensable

In the manifesto, the essence of Software Craftsmanship is captured in its "Raising the Bar." The manifesto summarizes values, frustrations and aspirations of very experienced and talented developers. For them, projects had to stop failing because of poor management, ill-defined processes, and of course, code written well.

Developers are taking the matter into their own hands and are trying to change how the industry software development works. They do not just by proposing new revolutionary and processes but also by showing their customers what they can do. When they show that their customers want to work together with them in order to produce and long-lived software, helping them to achieve whatever they want
