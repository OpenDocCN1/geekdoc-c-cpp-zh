The problem with all the Agile transformations I have seen is that they were a partial transformation. Consultancies and Agile coaches were hired by companies, most often, to change their process. However, these consultancies and Agile coaches did very little, or nothing, to help companies be better at writing quality software. General, Agile transformations were focused on process but not on technical disciplines, which means that they did very little to make better Agile coaches did not improve the skill set of people working on software delivery services or QA. The technical side of Agile transformation was totally forgotten or dismissed. This was very amusing (from some Agile coaches)—that developers who were great at writing code and the only thing they really wanted was a better process, promoting communication and empowering people, and everything. The only thing they needed was a better process, promoting communication and empowering people, and everything they were. "Let's make a better process and everything will be OK." In many cases, the developers who said the same old habits had just producing amazing software. Code is easy and just a detail thing, right? We just need a better process and we don't understand it.

The Agile transformations focused on process; empowering people; reducing bureaucracy and waste; prioritization of work; and improving the flow. The result was that companies were far better when they were addressed and improved. Without fixing the problems, it was almost impossible to achieve a successful project. However the technical behind Agile were forgotten. Process became more important than technical excellence. Technical excellence, which is assumed to be in place by process-related Agile, is ignored by managers and ill-prepared Agile coaches.

Many people in the Agile community love to talk about Toyota and how successful they became by changing their process, reducing waste, working in progress (WIP), and all the great things they do. What is Agile coaches and consultancy companies like to tell them themselves: The Toyota Dream. Building cars is different from building software. We do not use a car if it is not totally finished. We do not go back to the manufacturer after buying a car and ask them to add a door or to move the engine to the front of the car.

When talking about Toyota, we say "What if the cars were not good? If no one wanted to buy them? What if they were good and we had to take them back to the garage every month? An important factor to Toyota's success, was a good process, the focus on the quality of their cars.

Effective processes work for the system. In order to achieve quality, we need to provide a good feedback loop, at all levels. For a better system, we need talented people and professional coaches who can hear what they are doing, raising their voices when they feel something is wrong or could be better. Focusing purely on the process and treating software development like a production line leads to 9-5 developers doing what they are told—they become factory workers. This approach makes the system inefficient, compromising the entire project.

## Agile Coaches

Every profession has good and bad professionals and this is no different for Agile coaches. There are the ones who fully understand the original Agile message and when advising companies and teams on their Agile adoption, they emphasize the importance of process and technical excellence. Although the majority focus on process, they will often deal with or replace other professionals who could help them with the technical disciplines. However the average Agile coach, due to a lack of technical knowledge, will overwhelm the process side (misleading the client) and, often will not mention the technical side at all.

It is far easier to sell process management than the importance of technical excellence and all the effort we need to make Agile. Senior managers really well fail to see the benefits of developers playing a part bigger than just writing code. They also fail to understand the importance of paying attention to the quality of the code produced by developers. As they look at how it works, they are happy.

Senior managers, coming from technical backgrounds, are easily misled by bad Agile coaches. After a few months, or years, of adoption, when things goes wrong, it is very easy for Agile coaches and managers to blame the developers. Sometimes they are at fault, but the question here is how will the Agile transformation help them to be?

## Rejecting Technical Practices

But there is the other side as well. There are very good Agile coaches and consultancy companies out there doing an excellent job advising and helping their customers. When it comes to XP days, they get ugly. Many customers immediately reject the idea of having two developers pairing together—think it is a complete waste of money. They immediately reject the idea of investing in tests upfront and the benefit of Test-Driven Development. "What about our QA team?” they ask. "Developers do not waste time for that. That's why we pay company in Asia."

Sometimes, the rejection comes from the developers themselves. They just cannot see the pairing. Some think they would be because of their lack of knowledge in certain areas. They do not want to. They cannot see the point of writing tests, since at the end of the day they are great developers and do not make mistakes. Mediocre developers need tests, they just want to.

And if you do not invest in tests, what is the point of having continuous integration? Just to make sure the code compiles? Simple design and collective ownership do not work for them. Managers' idea of a senior and experienced developer is by how many patterns she knows, how complicated the design and architecture can be, and being the only one who knows the system works.

In cases like that, the rejection comes from source management, they need a bit of education and stronger evidence showing how it is to pay attention to the quality of the software that is being built. When the rejection comes from developers, they need some education, often quite achieved by XP developers who could code along pair with them and lead by example. If that does not work, replacing some of your developers with fresh blood, the ones who are open to learn new techniques and practices.

## A Naive Approach to Software Projects

During all years I have seen many approaches to software projects. Some companies that have the important things for a successful software project were expert writing requirements, a technical leader (who doesn't code) drawing widgets and documents, and a manager to supervise (micro-management). When they have these roles in place, they were ready to hire a few cheap developers—normally delegating the task of hiring to them. They were then given a pile of requirements and technical documents—that were prepared in months in advance, and software comes out of the end. One year later, developers present the software to be used, and the bug-free software is deployed to produce a product. What a waste it is. It's a matter that it never works.

In all cases where I have worked in a software project there was no access to the real users, no collaboration with the users, and no quick feedback-loop that ended up being far bigger than the problems they could face together. I can feel the stress, unhappiness, and dissatisfaction. But I have always seen we. Even with a couple of very bad developers with offshore outsourcing. I really don't know how the managers were expecting to work with such people. They have no idea how they were hired, knowing about their skills, and they believe that you will provide them with software that will satisfy their needs. After a few years of bad developers, I wonder how much this project really was for them.

Another common problem I see is that the point of leading software projects are too far away from the software itself. Many are not technical or can even remember when they lose their line of code—ever. If not they rely on bureaucracy, and, employing people. A software project cannot succeed without collaboration with good software professionals, who, being skilled in crafting, can also help the business to achieve what they want to achieve, giving them options, feedback, and constructive criticism.

Every software process, Agile processes, will assume technical excellence. Without technical excellence, every software will be a painful, frustrating and expensive experience.

## It's Not All Bad News

Many companies have just a Agile transformation, it is fair to say that, at least, they are much better than before. Improving their projects and having quick feedback loops about their business and delivery capabilities allow them to identify all the problems that they have still. A quick feedback loop is key for any company aspiring to be agile. Reacting to the feedback they get quickly is what makes them truly agile.

By no am I suggesting that Agile processes are bad and that just being better at quality software is the answer. The point is that we need a good process but delivery capability will not make projects succeed. Having developers who can produce high quality code but having a process that does not work well will not make projects succeed.

The important thing is to identify where the problems are and act on them. It is too late to make things better—it becomes far more when you delay it.

## Agile Software Craftsmanship

A common misunderstanding is to think Software Craftsmanship and Agile mutually exclusive. That's not the case at all. They complement each other nicely. Agile, in its current form, provides a different mindset for organizations and people, Agile methodologies focus on understanding and prioritizing activities by value, and providing feedback loops. This enables the team to react quickly, becoming agile through feedback loops and allows them to do the right thing. Software Craftsmanship is about professionalism in software development. Software Craftsmanship is an ideology, which many developers have decided to adopt in order to get better at what they do. Besides promoting many techniques and practices in which developers can get better at writing and well-crafted code, it also promotes that the developers should do far more to help their clients than just writing code.
