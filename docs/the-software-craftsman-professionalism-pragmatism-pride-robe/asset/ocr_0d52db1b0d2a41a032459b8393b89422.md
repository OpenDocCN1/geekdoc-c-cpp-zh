These young developers, as they join our industry, have to deal with much and unnecessarily complex code produced by their seniors that, for them, creating presentations on PowerPoint and spreadsheets on Excel seems more attractive than writing code.

On the other hand, if the same developers had been brought up in an environment where their seniors were always trying to get ahead as they do, getting excited every time they found a better way to do things or learning new tricks—these developers would have enjoyed their professions. With highly motivated developers who favor doing what they do and who are constantly looking outside their own cubes, companies could benefit immensely from all the innovations and efficiencies they would bring.

However, instead of many managers trying to improve how, when, and what developers should learn, companies should foster freedom which may lead to lack of interest. Let developers decide how, when, and what they want to learn. With this freedom, developers have a much better chance of creating and building a culture of learning. The better the developers, the more innovative and agile the company can be. If the company becomes a place where developers are happy, many other good developers will be attracted to it.

Let's explore some of the things developers can do to create such an environment.

# Start a Book Club

Pick a book and tell everyone you will start. Tell them what the book is and check if anyone is interested in having a discussion about it at lunchtime—or any other suitable time. If at least one developer is interested, you are ready to start.

If one is interested, ask what other developers sort of book (or subject) they want to explore. Make a list of the topics, pick the one that is most interested in, do some research, and present them with a small list of books on the topic. As a group, choose one of them and schedule the first meeting.

If no one is interested, start reading the book, take the time to share what you are learning during informal conversations. Maybe some of the developers initially not interested will change their minds and join you.

# Have a Tech Lunch (Bag Session)

Ask your team, if they want, would like to have lunch together while talking about technical stuff. The meeting doesn't need to have anything; decide what you want to talk about when you get there. Although it could be beneficial to discuss improvements to the project you are working on, don't make this about Work. What about learning. Make about different technologies, approaches, techniques, or whoever else you want to share and discuss. If people do want to talk about work, then some of the ideas discussed could be used to improve the project you are working on, schedule a time to discuss it.

Tech lunches are easy to set up because there is no preparation or organization needed. You just need a place and bring people along. I recommend using a meeting room near a coffee shop or restaurant; that you can use whiteboards, charts and projectors

# Have Group Discussions (Roundtables)

Divide a wall or whiteboard in two. One side is for talks; the other side is for topics to be discussed in a group. Hang Post-its for all the participants (or them on the table). Ask participants to write the title of the talk they want to give (five minutes for the talk, two minutes for questions) or the topic they want to discuss as group.

Start with the talking. Giving people talk can stand up and speak. You can use the whiteboard or projector if they want. Make sure you control the talk. If there are many questions after the talk is finished, interrupt with time and moving the talk Post-it to the group discussion. Tell everyone that they will have the opportunity to discuss that topic if they want to.

Once the listening (if any) are over, move on to the group discussion. Read the first talk out loud and whoever it wrote a 30-second summary about what they want to discuss. Do it for all the talks. Once all the talks are explained, check if anyone has any questions. In a single discussion: make sure people collaborate and are happy about merging the topics. Once this is done, start all the topics again, then ask people to raise their hands if they would like to discuss that topic. Write down next to the topic (or on the board) the number of people interested in the topic. If there is a clear winner, ask the person who proposed the topic to start the discussion. Before the voting, tell people that they should try to vote for a single topic but if they are really undecided, it is OK to vote for more than one.
