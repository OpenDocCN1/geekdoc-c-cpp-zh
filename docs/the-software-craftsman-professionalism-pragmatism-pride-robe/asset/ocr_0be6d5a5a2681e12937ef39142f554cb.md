# 2. Agile

Back in February 2001, seventeen influential people in our industry decided to get together in a ski resort in Utah to discuss alternatives to heavyweight and document-based approaches to software development. Each of them had been working and experimenting with different techniques and approaches to software development, and they decided that it would be a good idea to get together and share what they were doing. The idea was to learn from each other and try to come up with better ways to deliver software projects.

They were Kent Beck, Mike Beadle, Alistair Cockburn, Ralph Cunningham, John Fowler, Jim Highmuth, Andrew Hunt, Jon Kern, Brian Martin, Robert C. Martin, Steve Mellor, Ken Schubert, Jeffrey Sutherland, and David Thomas.

They pioneered methodologies and techniques like Extreme Programming (XP), Scrum, the Dynamic Systems Model (SDSM), Adaptive Software Development, Feature-Driven Development, and pragmatic programming.

After discussions, the Agile Manifesto was created and the Agile Alliance formed.

Agile is not a single thing. Agile is a combination of methodologies and techniques that, according to the context, help teams and adapt to the ever-changing nature of software projects and also reduce the risks associated with them. The Agile disciplines are divided into two main groups: process-oriented and technical.

# Process-Oriented Agile Disciplines

These disciplines affect how teams and organizations work, collaborate, and organize. They normally prescribe and/or suggest, among many others, the types of methods the teams should use; the roles people play; ways how to capture requirements, how to measure work velocity, how to work in an iterative way, how to plan and deliver work, and how to demonstrate progress and get feedback.

Process-oriented disciplines help teams focus on what is really important and valuable to the business. Using these methodologies is a great way to make sure the team is building the right thing.

# Technical-Oriented Disciplines

These disciplines are more specific to the challenges of developing, growing, maintaining, and delivering the software. They normally prescribe or suggest technical practices and tests. Test-Driven Development, programming, continuous integration, and simple design are a few examples of these practices and techniques. Technical-oriented disciplines help teams focus on the quality of the software they are producing and helping teams to make sure they are building the right thing.

# What Is It to Be Agile?

> [Agile means] adapting successfully to circumstances.
—Tom Gil

We don't go Agile. Either we are Agile or we are not. Agile methodologies are all about quick and short feedback loops. The quicker and faster our feedback loop, the more agile we can become. Every time we receive feedback, we have an opportunity to react to it, and the act of reacting (or not) to new information makes us (or less) agile. Narrowing the feedback loop helps us make mistakes visible sooner, allowing us to implement and adapt quickly. Agile does not solve all problems; the faster we see a demo or any other feature to test, the earlier we get feedback on it. I'm using user here as a general term. If we are for a product owner, sponsor, or an end user. Feedback from multiple sources is extremely important. When a feature is commercially viable sooner helps mitigate the risks of our industry.

# A Game Changer

Agile was a huge step forward in our industry. Its acceptance by the wider community, immediately after its creation, was massive. The fast pace of change in modern software projects is frustrating and costs a lot of money. Agile was an antidote for that. The mindset changed. Before where we had many documents, big design up-front (BDUF), and bureaucracy, now we have running software from one week. Before where we were reactive to changes, now we embrace them.
