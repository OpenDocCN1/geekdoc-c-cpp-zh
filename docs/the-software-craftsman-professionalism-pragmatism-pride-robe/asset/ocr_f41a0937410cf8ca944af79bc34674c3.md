In The Passionate Programmer, Chuck Fowler says saying yes to disappointment is just lying. He goes further to say that saying yes is addictive and destructive. It has a bad habit masquerading as one.

We usually not like to say no. It sounds weird. When we say no, the last thing we want is to disappoint our customers. We feel that we are not good team players and the last thing we want is to disappoint them. We want to be wanted and succeed, which makes us feel very positive. We need to remember that when we say yes, people will take it into account and make plans based on it. We will make promises to our bosses, other teams, customers, or partners based on it. And not being honest may cause huge damage to the company. Professionalism means being honest with ourselves, with our teammates and with managers.

Somethimes we may not be honest with our boss—or whoever is paying for the job—about the current status of the project. We feel that she is not making other people of our concerns, always saying we will verify that we can scale it down, and asking her if that is really the case. If the situation is getting bad we should arrange a meeting to discuss what can be done. This might sound strange and aggressive, but we will be doing a favor to the team, the company, and our own bosses. Negotiation is a skill we all need to learn. In the way our managers are doing their jobs, so we should also do-making decisions and not making unnecessary confrontations. As long as we do it in honesty and transparently what we say, there is a big chance that no one will ever hurt the team and the company with us.

# Providing Options

Always saying no is not a professional attitude. Every no, should be followed by a list of alternatives. Even if no, we need to analyze the problem and provide options. We may not always be able to come up with options, but at least an incomplete idea could help other people to find a different alternative. At the end of the day, we should always focus on solving the problem, regardless of which solution we chose or proposed it

Saying no without providing options does not help because the receiver of our answer will not be able to do much. I remember a tale that I heard when I was younger, the boss where I had just in town, asked for two assistants to help me carry the juice order. The first one went to the juice shop around the corner, asked for an orange juice, and within five minutes was back saying the boss that the shop did not have oranges. The second one took 20 minutes to come back, and before she said anything, the boss said, "I already know the shop does not have oranges, why did you go?" The second one replied, "Because they have orange juice and pineapple juice. The pineapple juice is free but the juice is also. I asked around and discovered that there is a supermarket far away where I could buy some fresh oranges and make you a pineapple or apple juice in five minutes, or if you prefer, I can make a fresh juice in 30 minutes."

The first assistant, having returned quite empty, left the boss with nothing. We needed to figure him how to solve the problem with an in-depth knowledge of the area. The second assistant gives him positive and when we are done, we should always be honest and transparent. A can-to-do approach is always appreciated, and even when valued.

Sometimes there are problems that we do not know how to solve. When this is the case, we should say it honestly and as quickly as possible. With new information we will be able to investigate the problem and do our best at solving it, we are not making false promises whatever we learn. Providing updates of as often as we can is our best; it enables the team to do what to do with new information and also help in areas where they might have a better understanding than we do. This will make the team really trust us when we say what we know. Honest, no matter how bad the situation is, it is a sign of professionalism
