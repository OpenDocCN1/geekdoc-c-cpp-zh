## Switch Projects for an Iteration

Within a company, it is common to have different teams working on different applications or in different parts of the system. After a period of time, projects take into a routine; some of the initial challenges have been solved and the best design decisions have been made. However, projects rarely evolve. As developers become comfortable with their teams and decisions, they may also consider better ways of doing things. Learning takes over.

Assuming that developers are constantly pairing with each other, we can solve this problem by providing an opportunity for developers to switch projects for at least a iteration. Pairing with different teams offers opportunities to develop technologies, ways of working and knowledge. Having different teams join an existing project, even for a small period of time, makes the team rethink their problems and solutions and explain why they do the way they do. This may bring a few positive effects:

- Validation of the decisions: The new person, understanding the context in the decisions made, can confirm it was a good decision.
- Knowledge sharing: The new developer learns how certain problems can be solved.
- Improvements: The new developer can propose better ways to solve those problems.
- Collective learning: After discussing the current situation and sharing knowledge with each other, existing members of the team and the new developer can come up with a better solution.

Periodic exchanges generally improve motivation, spread ideas across projects, and break knowledge silos. The Scrum master of one team can swap places with a tester from another team.

## Switch Projects for a Few Hours

As a variation to the approach described, we could have developers working with different teams for just a few hours or days. Developers can organize the themselves. They just need to walk to the team's location, sit with one of the developers, and pair for a couple of hours.

I once worked for a large organization where all the software developed was written in Java. Over time, different teams started using Gradle as a testing framework, instead of Maven and JUnit. The team I was working with decided to use Scala and a commercial in-memory data grid instead of Java and Oracle for our new projects.

Being able to walk over to another team and if we could pair with someone for a few hours every day was a great way to expose many of us to different technologies. At the time of this writing, one of our teams is already migrating to Gradle and Spock, and another team is considering Gradle.

## Conduct Group Code Reviews

Group code reviews are another interesting way to create a culture of learning. It's amazing what we learn debating with colleagues whether a piece of code could be improved or if it could be used as an example that should be followed. If done right, group reviews bring the whole team together; everyone feels responsible for the decisions of what quality for them.

Although I value code reviews, it is important to get it right. But what does "get it right”?

- Comments should never be personal.
- It doesn't matter who wrote the code.
- Never look at the commit history during a group code review. Focus on changing the future, not dwelling on the past.
- Comments should be objective and constructive. The focus is on how to make the best code and not why it says so much.
- Sometimes using a facilitator helps. Her role is to make sure everyone speaks and that there is no fingerpointing.
- Don't expect to make a lot of progress. If developers want to spend a lot of time discussing an issue, let them do it.

## Have Hands-On-Coding Sessions

Hands-on coding sessions are sessions where developers get together to write code. The facilitator will choose an exercise, normally a kata, and add some twist (constraints) to it to spice things up. The main objective of a hands-on coding session is not to finish the exercise; the focus is to writing the best code we can possibly write and challenge ourselves. Here are some suggestions for running a hands-on session:

- The facilitator must be familiar with the exercise; she would have done the exercise herself at least twice before the session.
- Developers work in pairs, with a person that they never paired before.
- As a rule, TDD is mandatory, unless stated otherwise by the facilitator.
- All developers should work on the same exercise; the retrospective more relevant and focused.
- The facilitator should walk around and help developers they've done; normally he or she should asking questions, not give them the answer or directions.
- The facilitator should run a small retrospective at the end, where all developers can share what they've done, how they've learned it.
- Developers thank their pairs when the session is over.

Hands-on sessions are very helpful for injecting passion into your teams. It creates an opportunity for developers to learn from each other, and it is a bit of fun to whack.

I suggest you follow the suggestions above if you plan to organize hands-on sessions. They serve as a base for all of the very successful hands-on sessions organized by the London Software Craftsmanship Community (LSCC) and the ones we run internally in our companies.

- Some other important points for running a hands-on session:
- Don't make it work. The focus is to practice and learn in a safe environment.
- Allocate two hours for the session: one hour from the company's time and one hour from the developers' own personal time (i.c. 4 to 6 pm if the working day finishes at 5 pm). This is why we have a mutual commitment to creating a culture of learning.
- Be prepared to run the first few sessions yourself. Very rarely will other developers put themselves to run the first sessions. Make sure you run them in a very informal and friendly way so others get encouraged.

## Factor Technology-Agnostic

Running technology-agnostic sessions It is always easier to run technology-specific sessions, if developers are not interested in the technology, they won't.

When running technology-agnostic sessions, developers are in control of technologies they want to use, which will make the session far more enjoyable for everyone. Choose a kata, add a few constraints (you cannot use all else) ask them to pair up with you, pair your code every two minutes. You need to use a test-don't-approach approach (write the test first), and let them choose which languages and frameworks they want to use.

Technology-agnostic sessions encourage variety. There will always be a pair trying to use a new language or framework. The retrospectives becoming more interesting and interactive, because people will talk about the challenges they faced while using the technology they've chosen.

## Start Internal Communities of Practice (Cop)

If you have a few regular sessions running, invite the developers who come more often to them to help organize the sessions. Try to meet regularly to discuss these sessions and share the load of the organization and facilitation of the Cop. Keep it open and democratic so everyone can participate.

You might need just few people to start the community, internal or external. That is exactly how the largest Software Craftsmanship community in the world started, so you also start like that. You just need two people meeting regularly to attract more people to join. Just sure people are aware that you are meeting and how much fun you are having. Share what you are learning during informal conversations over coffee or lunch.

A just of caution: don't focus on growing the community. Focus on having fun and running good sessions. The growth will come naturally.
