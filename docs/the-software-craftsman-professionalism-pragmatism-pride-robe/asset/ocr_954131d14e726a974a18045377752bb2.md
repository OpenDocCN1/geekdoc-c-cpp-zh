There is a lot we can get from a pair-grouping interview:

+   * How well the developer decides on tests to write—that shows experience
* How familiar is she with the tools (the integrated development environment [IDE]), language, testing and mocking frameworks, usage of shortcuts, etc.)
* Choice of names for classes, methods, and attributes
* How clean and expressive the code is
* How the developer reacts when the interviewer makes suggestions or comments
* Her thought process
* Her care in not solving the problem for but also how she is solving the problem

However, pair-grouping interviews can be very tricky to conduct and choosing a good problem to work on can be quite challenging.

A common approach is to choose one of the katas publicly available or to create our own kata. Katas are great when we want to use TDD and general clean-code principles.

Another approach is to take an existing code from our own code base, especially one that is badly written and without tests, and ask the candidate to write and make it better. This is valuable for projects with loads of legacy red skills the candidate would need if using it.

It is important to have realistic expectations when conducting pair-grouping interviews. We need to remember that, as interviewers, we already know the domain the candidate doesn't know. We shouldn't expect the candidates to get certain solutions strategies. When we know something, tend to forget how much time we spend learning.

If we provide tools (Computer, IDE, testing and mocking frameworks), we need to bear in mind that the candidate may not be familiar with them. This is not a bad thing. Every mainstream language has many testing frameworks and is supported by many different IDEs and editors. It's common for candidates getting stuck when they don't know the expected environment or IDE provided during the interview. Pay attention to how the candidate reacts to that, and whether they need help. Since this is a pair-grouping exercise, the interviewer should show what the candidate can do in the right direction.

Whatever we do as an interviewer, we should not leave the candidate to struggle too long. We should wait for maximum of three or four minutes, and then ask for suggestions. The candidate is already under pressure, and making her struggle too long will just make things worse. As we can mentally keep track of the points where the candidate struggled, we should give them feedback at the end of the interview.

Pair programming means pair programming and not standing over someone's mistake and applying unnecessary pressure. That's not how we usually work with our colleagues and there is no point to benefit this way with a candidate.

Once I interviewed a very experienced guy. On my machine I had Eclipse, JUnit, and Mockito (a Java mocking framework). He told me he was more familiar with IntelliJ and Mockk. He never used Mockito, so he's at least learning it quickly.

He quickly became frustrated because he didn't know the Eclipse shortcuts. "I'm really stupid using the mouse so often," he repeated, he said. "What's the shortcut for running?" I answered and then he took notes on a piece of paper. He then started asking me about other shortcuts and took notes of all of them. Fifteen minutes later, had on, piece of paper, some of the basic shortcuts and started to feel more comfortable.

He also struggled with Mockito. "If you tell me what to do, I can do it for you," he said. "I want to make this class and that constant every time the public method is invoked," I then wrote the code according what he wanted to do. That helped him to understand how Mockito worked. By the end of the interview, he was using Eclipse and Mockito quite well.

The only thing I regretted about that interview was we were not fast enough to get back to him and he got a job somewhere else.
