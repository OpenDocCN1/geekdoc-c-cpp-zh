# Tailor-Made Interviews

Before interviewing any candidate, we should ask: what are our values? What sort of attitude do we expect from team leader? The answers to these questions form the acceptance criteria we use to hire them. But that doesn't mean we shouldn't explore others. It's always good to verify how versatile the candidate is, as we don't reject a candidate for things that are not important to us.

I was once contacted for an opportunity at a major startup in the UK. The role was for a server-side Java developer and the project had no experience. The project seemed interesting and I was comfortable with the vast majority of the technologies listed. I decided to apply.

The interview process consisted of two phases: an online multiple-choice test and an interview with the project manager leader. That was a very bad start because believe online tests are stupid and totally inefficient. On of that,, didn't have to talk to any of the developers during the selection process, there would be no way for me to judge their knowledge, code values, and the real situation of the project. I don't need HR and him to help me this.

The test was 30 multiple-choice questions, randomly selected. Out of the 30 questions, 14 (46 percent) were about Swing and Apples—or old technologies that were totally irrelevant for the job. I noted that the tests were based on a very old version of Java—that had not been updated for years. I had never used Apple before and I couldn't think why anyone would use crap. As for I, Apple-old is still being used in some of their applications.

A couple of hours after I finished the test, the HR department called me. They told us we still deliberating whether we would call me or not for a face-to-face interview, I said that I scored 88 percent on the test (26/30) but the team would only interview candidates who scored at least 90 percent. "Not a problem at all," I said "I'll help them with decision. Please tell them I don't want to continue with the process."

The preselection process is extremely important. That is where you filter candidates according to your core values. Judging candidates by online multiple-choice tests is definitely not how you do it.

# Taking a Punt

Once I interviewed a candidate in his early twenties. He had two years of experience and was still working for his first employer. His CV had no any mention of TDD or Agile-things that are common in a CI days. His CV was quite, with almost nothing in it. Less two years of experience at a single company. It was a CV that I wouldn't normally pay much attention to. However, there was one line that really made me curious. He had been a member of a Linux user group since he was 16 years old.

I invited him for an interview and, as we spoke, he told that he was working for an organization where Agile and Extreme Programming were not allowed. He, also mentioned he was trying to TDD as a project. "How many projects do you have? I asked. "Five," he said. We spent the rest of the interview with him about his projects, his approach to TDD, the challenges, things he would do differently, and so on.

Although he didn't have experience in the technologies we used and had basic TDD skills, I could see how he passed to new things. He was using his own time to learn things he could not do during working hours. He was a good guy, given the right environment and opportunity, could really shine and become a great developer.

We were looking for more experienced developers but something that I could not let him. One year after he joined our team, he became one of the best developers we had.

When interviewing, we should look for talent, attitude, passion, and potential, not knowledge of specific technologies.
