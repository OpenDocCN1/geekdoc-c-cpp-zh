Sometimes, the rejection comes from the developers themselves. They just cannot see the pairing. Some think they would be because their lack of knowledge in certain areas. They do not want. They cannot see the point of writing tests, since at the end of the day they are great developers and do not make mistakes. Mediocre developers need tests, they just want.

And if you do not invest in tests, what is the point of having continuous integration? Just to make sure the code compiles? Simple design and collective ownership do not work for them. Managers' idea of a senior and experienced developer is by how many patterns she knows, how complicated the design and architecture can be, and being the only one who knows the system works.

In cases like that, the rejection comes from source management, they need a bit of education and stronger evidence showing how it is to pay attention to the quality of the software that is being built. When the rejection comes from developers, they need some education, often quite achieved by XP developers who could code along pair with them and lead by example. If that does not work, replacing some of your developers with fresh blood, the ones who are open to learn new techniques and practices.

# A Na Approach to Software Projects

During all years I have seen many approaches to software projects. Some companies that have the important things for a successful software project were expert writing requirements, a technical leader (who doesn't code) drawing widgets and documents, and a manager to supervise (micro-management). When they have these roles in place, they were ready to hire a few cheap developers—normally delegating the task of hiring to them. They were then given a pile of requirements and technical documents—that were prepared in months in advance, and software comes out of the end. One year later, developers present the software to be used, and the bug-free software is deployed to produce a product. What a waste it. It's a matter that it never works.

In all cases where I have worked in a software project there was no access to the real users, no collaboration with the users, and no quick feedback-loop that ended up being far bigger than the problems they could face together. I can feel the stress, unhappiness, and dissatisfaction. But I have always seen we. It even with a couple of very bad developers with offshore outsourcing. I really don't know how the managers were expecting to work with such people. They have no idea how they were hired, knowing about their skills, and they believe that you will provide them with software that will satisfy their needs. After a few years of bad developers, I wonder how much this project really was for them.

Another common problem I see is that the point of leading software projects are too far away from the software itself. Many are not technical or can even remember when they lose their line of code—ever. If not they rely on bureaucracy, and, employing people. A software project cannot succeed without collaboration with good software professionals, who, being skilled in crafting, can also help the business to achieve what they want to achieve, giving them options, feedback, and constructive criticism.

Every software process, Agile processes, will assume technical excellence. Without technical excellence, every software will be a painful, frustrating and expensive experience.

# It's Not All Bad News

Many companies have just a Agile transformation, it is fair to say that, at least, they are much better than before. Improving their projects and having quick feedback loops about their business and delivery capabilities allow them to identify all the problems that they have still. A quick feedback loop is key for any company aspiring to be agile. Reacting to the feedback they gets quickly is what makes them truly agile.

By no am I suggesting that Agile processes are bad and that just being better at quality software is the answer. The point is that we need a good process but delivery capability will not make projects succeed. Having developers who can produce high quality code but having a process that does not work well will not make projects succeed.

The important thing is to identify where the problems are and act on them. It is too late to make things better—it becomes far more when you delay it.

# Agile Software Craftsmanship

A common misunderstanding is to think Software Craftsmanship and Agile mutually exclusive. That's not the case at all. They complement each other nicely. Agile, in its current form, provides a different mindset for organizations and people, Agile methodologies focus on understanding and prioritizing activities by value, and providing feedback loops. This enables the team to react quickly, becoming agile through feedback loops and allows them to do the right thing. Software Craftsmanship is about professionalism in software development. Software Craftsmanship is an ideology, which many developers have decided to adopt in order to get better at what they do. Besides promoting many techniques and practices in which developers can get better at writing and well-crafted code, it also promotes that the developers should do far more to help their clients than just writing code.
