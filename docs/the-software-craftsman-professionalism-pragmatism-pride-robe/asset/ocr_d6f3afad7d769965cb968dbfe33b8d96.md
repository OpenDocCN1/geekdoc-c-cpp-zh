# Employer/Employee Relationship

In creative work, the employer/employee model is the wrong model. On one hand, we have a contractual model that states how people should be paid and also states the legal and behavioral obligations that professionals need to be respected by employers and employees. On the other hand, we have the type of relationship that professionals have with their clients. The top-down, command and control of management became very popular during the Industrial Revolution and again has returned when the manager of work is doing manual or repetitive work. However, it does not work for creative workers. This style of management can be detrimental to the morale of creative professionals, making the company far less innovative. Companies that are still using this type of management struggle to hire talented professionals and slowly losing the ones they still have. But, course, this is just one of the many stories we hear every day. What is said in the industry is that if developers want to be treated as professionals, they should start acting as professionals. A software professional must, among other things, get involved and contribute to the business, provide options and solutions, and offer the best service when it comes to the implementation, technologies and quality of what they produce.

The relationship between software craftsmen and their clients should be seen as a productive business partnership, regardless of which contractual model we may have. A permanent employee, a contractor, a consultant, or a developer working for an outsourcing company should not affect the relationship or the attitude we have toward our clients.

# Books, Many Books

Having our own library, or electronic, we are very lucky to be in an industry where so much information is produced. However, there are many types of books, and choosing which ones to read is a very difficult task.

+   * Technology-specific books are very valuable but they expire. They are essential for the immediate need, when we want to learn a framework, language, or any other software we need to use. They are great at giving us deep understanding of how work and the knowledge acquired can be used immediately. They are also great when we are planning our next projects. They can give us details of how to use the technologies that may be between our current job and our desired job. However, many of the technologyspecific books get old quickly. When a new version of the technology is released, or a different way of doing things happens, they will not be as valuable as before. Examples would be books about Hibernate, Nodejs, or Code.
* Conceptual books are those that give us the foundation in our careers. These are the books that get us introduced to concepts, patterns and practices. The knowledge we acquire through this type of book cannot always be applicable; it may take a significant amount of time to digest the information and become proficient. Often a quote or language may be used to explain technical concepts but the knowledge we get can be applied broadly. Books covering topics like Test Development, Domain-Driven Design, object-oriented design, functional programming, or modeling with NoSQL databases are examples of such books. The knowledge we get in this category is not that will be far more helpful than learning a specific technology, and it may take many years before we get comfortable with them. However, conceptual books are those that give us the foundation to learn specific technologies much quiecer.
* Behavioral books are the books that make us more efficient when working in teams and better professionals in their field. They help us learn how to deal with people, clients, decisions, team members, and so on. By knowing programming languages, frameworks, and practices is not enough if we want to be a good professional. We also need to learn how to deal with everything that is not related to our job but is also part of a software project or organization. Books in this category will cover the more human and professional side of software development, including topics like Agile methodologies. Software Craftsmanship, Lean software development, philosophy, and management.
