
The main problem you have when working with applications is fear. No, I'm not calling you a coward. I'm just stating that you are a responsible developer who understands the implications of making the wrong change in a code because you are scared to change parts of this code you do not understand how it works, and you have no confidence that you will not break something.

This application is working software, but is it enough? Well-crafted software matters, regardless of how old the application is, and can change easily. The side effects are well known and controlled. It has high and reliable, clear, simple design, and business language expressed in the code. Adding or changing features does not take longer than it used to take at the beginning of the project, when the code base was small.

The code must be maintainable. Developers must know what is going on when changing the code and must not be afraid to change it. Changes should be localized and not affect other parts of the application—no ripple effects. In a few minutes, not seconds, after pressing a button, tests will check the application and inform you if something breaks.

In order to evolve, developers must not shy away from changing. Using Test-Driven Development, simple design, and using the business language in the code are the best ways to keep your well-healthy and well-crafted code.

## Not Responding to Change, But Steadily Adding Value

Have you ever wondered how software projects work? Think about the developers working on it. Now think about all the professionals who are also part of the project: teams, production services, operations, business analysts, products, and project managers. There are also marketing, sales, customer service, and back office people. How much is spent in salaries. Now add all the cost of computers, communication infrastructure, office rent, desks, furniture, and coffee. They all come with it. On top of that,

Software projects are normally a massive investment and with any normal investment, companies will want a return. The only reason for companies to invest in a project is to make money, save or project revenue. Within that, it is our job to help them achieve this.

When we talk about adding value, we are not just talking about new features and fixing bugs. It is also about improving the software to be clean, testable, and easy to maintain.

It is our job to make sure the older and better the software is, the more the company will benefit from. We want to keep adding to the project and make the appropriate changes at the same speed that we have used at the beginning of the project. This would allow companies to react quickly to the market regardless of how old their software is. As it gets older, software should become more valuable and not a source of pain and frustration.

Increasing the lifespan, and the ability to keep it fast enough, is our main goal. Good software development, automated testing skills, and talented developers are essential for that; we should always have the code cleaner than we found.

The focus on high-quality software must be the number one if you want long-lasting applications. Rewriting an agile a few years after it has been developed is for a poor return on investment. In many occasions, the decision to rewrite an application is made because of the prohibitive costs of maintaining it.

> Insanity is doing the same thing over and again and expecting different results.
> — Einstein

The problem is that same bad techniques that were used to build the old application will also be used to build the new—making the new application as old as the old after a few months or years. It is our job to break this cycle, building applications with well-crafted code.

## Not Only Individuals and Interactions, but also a Community of Professionals

Sharing and mentoring are at the heart of Software Craftsmanship. Software crafters are passionate and always strive to be better. However, we have a bigger mission: we are responsible for preparing the next generation of craftsmen.

The best way to move our industry is by sharing what we have learned through mentoring and inspiring less-experienced developers. This also relates to the ideas of apprentices, mentors, and masters, where Software Craftsmanship must mentor help them in their journey. Knowledge, ideas, successes, and failures must be shared and discussed within the community in order to move our industry forward.

I was surprised to hear from some developers and Agile coaches that the Software Craftsmanship community is a group of selected and elite developers. An average developer cannot be considered a software craftsman if he thinks Software Craftsmanship is elite, if it is not enough to learn from others, or is not not willing to share his knowledge and mentor less-experienced developers. From my experience, the Software Craftsmanship community is of the most open and friendly communities I know. By its nature, language agnostic, it is a community that embraces all sorts of developers, regardless of their level of seniority or technology.

Learning from each other is by far the best way for us to become better. Writing blogs, contributing to open source projects, making our code publicly, becoming part of our local communities, and pairing with other developers are some of the ways we can contribute to the great good of our industry.

Since 2010, Software Craftsmanship communities are being created in Europe and the United States and they have many free events every month. We welcome developers from all sorts of backgrounds, industries, and levels of experience. These communities promote an environment where developers can share, meet, and write code together.

Besides these communities, this in our manifesto also includes our work. Great developers want to work with great developers. Great developers want to work for great companies. What we want is to work with people who can make us better, that we want to learn and learn from each. We do not want mediocre. We want passionate and inspired professionals that we can call colleagues.

## Not Only Customer, but Also Product Producers

The first thing to mention here is that we do not believe in the employee/employer type of relationship. What the contract says you are a permanent employee, contractor, vendor, paid per day, hour, or month is just for formality. We believe in partnership above all. If you are a permanent employee, you should treat your customer as customer, the same way as if you were a contractor or consultant. Employers, on the other hand, should also expect their employees to provide great service at all times, like a consultant and craftsman is supposed to. Turning up to work, keeping your head down, and doing what you are told to do is not what it means to be professional.

Software craftsmen are not factory. We want to actively contribute to the success of the project, questioning requirements, understanding the business, processes and product partnering with our customers or employers. This is a different approach to the traditional employee/employer model and the advantages for employers. A highly motivated team will make the project succeed. Passion and talented people want to succeed and will always find ways to overcome problems and boundaries.

Software craftsmen need successful projects to build their reputations. We want to be proud of what we do. Successfully delivering high-quality software and having satisfied customers are essential for a software craftsman's craft.

Although extremely important, writing good code is one of the things we need to do to have a project to succeed. We help our customers to improve their processes, provide them with more viable options, help them to remove bureaucracy, understand their business, question their requirements, provide them with good information and knowledge, help them to plan and prioritize work, although they are not ours. Every detail is important to the project. Providing value to our customers at all levels is what we mean by having a partnership.

The core business of many of our customers is not building software, so it is our obligation to help them run a project in the best possible way. That's what we do. For developers that say it is not their job.

## But Some Customers Are Not Ready for Productive

Unfortunately, there are some customers that are not ready for this type of partnership. They see software development as an industrial process and the least important part of the project. For some customers, software developers are as factory workers who are there to follow orders from smarter people. Some of these companies focus on getting the cheapest developers they can find and always have technical managers to micro-manage them. They will not welcome or listen to developers when it comes to business decisions. Some will have no say in development skills to the technical leader (or dictator).

Working for a company with this mentality is always very difficult for any software craftsmen. We always try to turn around this situation and try to prove to our customers that we could help them far more if they wanted to.

However, in the same way that customers have to have good developers, software craftsmen also want to work for good companies. There is no point in spending all our energy and health to help those who do not want to. There is a limit to the efforts we make to help our customers. How to select clients (or employers) is an essential skill for a craftsman. Choosing a client who is not interested in or does not value the skills of a software craftsman is pointless.
