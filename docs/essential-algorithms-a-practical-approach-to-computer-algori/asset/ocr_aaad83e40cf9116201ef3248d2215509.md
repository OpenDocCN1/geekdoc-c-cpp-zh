# Asking Interview Puzzle Questions

The preceding section gives some examples of bad interview puzzles. They rely on knowledge of trivia, or at best, the ability to work backwards from a solution to a problem. Working backwards takes creativity, and you can certainly be creative without that ability.

More than anyone else, these problems tell you how well the candidate combed the Internet, looking for potential interview puzzles. There's some benefit in knowing what the candidate prepared thoroughly for the interview, but it doesn't really tell you much about his or creativity or problem-solving ability.

To get useful information from a puzzle, you need a question that the candidate hasn't seen before. On the other hand, the puzzle can't be so impossible that the candidate panics. It shouldn't rely on a trick or point of trivia that only measures whether the candidate happened to see a particular issue of some obscure magazine.

Unfortunately, that rules out all of puzzles. That remain puzzles that ask the user to perform a calculation, make an estimate, or otherwise something that may be straightforward that gives the candidate room to explore possible approaches.

For example, one popular interview question has a form similar to this: "How many babies fit inside a school bus? is highly unlikely to have memorized, so this question is really asking the user to come up with an estimate. Perform a calculation. (Suppose a school bus is 36 feet long, a baseball is 3 inches in diameter, and so on.) It doesn't really matter whether the assumptions are correct, as long as the process makes sense.

This question determines whether the candidate can perform back-of-the-envelope calculations, which is relevant to software engineering.

Another kind of calculation puzzle comes in a form similar to this: "If I am three times as old as my brother, and in two years I'll be twice as old as he, how old am I?" (See Exercise 6 for the answer). This is mainly an exercise in translating a word problem into a set of equations. That is certainly useful, but people don't like word problems, and real-world problems don't take this form anyway.

Clock puzzles have this form: "How many times does the hour and minute hands on a clock cross each other between noon and midnight?" (See Exercise 7 for the answer). This puzzle and others usually be solved by using a table and plugging in some values. That approach doesn't really let the candidate show off his creativity, but it does show that he can be organized.
