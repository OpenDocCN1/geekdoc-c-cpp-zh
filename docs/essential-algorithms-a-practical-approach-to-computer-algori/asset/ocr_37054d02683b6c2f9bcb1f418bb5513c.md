# ONE GLIB ANSWER

You’re allowed one glib answer, but then you must be prepared to get to work. For example, one common kind of interview puzzle is the estimation question, such as “How many baseballs inside a school bus?” or “How many barbers are there in Tampa, Florida?

These questions often lend themselves to glib answers. For, if the interviewer asks, “How much should you charge to clean all the chimneys in Detroit?” you could say “As much as the market will bear” or “30 per chimney.” You can cause for a chuckle, but then you would start on an estimate.

If the interviewer only wants the glib answer, he will stop you at that point. More likely, he wants to see how you handle a calculation of unknown values.

If you have absolutely no clue about value, leave it in the calculation as a variable. After you come up with an answer, plug in some values to see what happens, and make a guess about whether that seems reasonable. For the chimney, you might come up with this equation:

```
amount = rate × time × population × percentage
```

where:

+   - The hourly rate (say, $20 per hour)
- Is the time required to clean a chimney [say, 1 hour]
- Population is the population of Detroit (say, 1 million)
- Percentage is the percentage of people with a chimney (say, 25%)

The last value may require some further estimates. You might try to estimate the number of people living in each household and the number of people who live in houses as opposed without chimneys.

When you’re done, you plug in your guesses and see what comes out. For the values shown here, that would be as follows:

```
amount = 20 × 1 × 1,000,000 × 0.25 = $5,000,000
```

It doesn’t really matter whether the answer is correct (it certainly isn’t). What’s important is the method you use to calculate it.
