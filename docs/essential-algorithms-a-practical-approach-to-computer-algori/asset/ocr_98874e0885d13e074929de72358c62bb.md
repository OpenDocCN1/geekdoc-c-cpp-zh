# Chapter 16 = Cryptography 413

The strength of RSA relies on the fact that it is hard to factor very large numbers. An attacker who can factor the public modulus n can recover the primes p and q. From p and q, plus the public exponent e, the attacker can then figure out the private key and break the cipher.

This is why the primes p and q must be large—to prevent an attacker from easily factoring n.

> NOTE Although factoring is believed to be a hard problem, and lots of people have spent a huge amount of effort studying it, there is no guarantee that some won't eventually come up with a way to factor large numbers quickly.

# Euler's Totient Function

Step 1 and 2 of the key generation algorithm are reasonably straightforward using the techniques described in Chapter 2.

Step 3 of the keygenerating algorithm requires you to calculate Euler's totient function φ(n). The totient function, is also called the phi function, a function that gives the number of positive integers less than a particular number that are relatively prime to that number. For example, φ(12) is 4 because there are four numbers less than 12 that are relatively prime to it: 1, 5, 7, and 11.

Because a prime number is relatively prime to every positive integer than itself, φ(p) = p - 1 if p is prime.

It turns out that, if p and q are relatively prime, φ(p 3 q) = φ(p) 3 φ(q). If p and q are both primes, they are relatively prime, so in step 3 φ(n) = φ(p 3 q) = φ(p) 3 φ(q) = (p−1) 3 (q−1). This is easy to compute.

For example, suppose p = 3 and q = 5. Then φ(15) = φ(3) 3 φ(5) = (3−1) 3 (5−1) = 2 3 4 = 8. This is true because the positive integers smaller than 15 are relatively prime to 15 are the values 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 11, 13, and 14.

# Multiplicative Inverses

Step 4 in the key generation algorithm is reasonably straightforward. Simply pick random numbers between 1 and φ(e) and use the GCD algorithm to find if they are relatively prime to e.
