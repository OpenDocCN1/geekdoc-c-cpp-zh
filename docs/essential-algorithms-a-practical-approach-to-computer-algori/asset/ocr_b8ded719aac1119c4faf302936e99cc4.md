If that sounds intimidating, don't worry. These concepts are useful for understanding the algorithm, but you don't need to build new networks to calculate maximum flows. Residual capacities, residual capacity networks, and augmenting paths can all be found within the original network without too much work.

A link's residual capacity is the amount of extra flow you could add to the link. For example, the C $ightarrow$ F link on the left in Figure 14-7 has a residual capacity of 2 because the link has a capacity of 3 and is currently carrying a flow of only 1.

In addition to the network's normal links, each link defines a virtual backlink that may not be part of the network. For example, in Figure 14-7 the A $ightarrow$ B link implicitly defines a backlink B $ightarrow$ A backlink. These backlinks are important because they can have residual capacities, and may be able to push backflow across them.

A backlink's residual capacity is the amount of flow traveling forward across the corresponding normal link. For example, on the left in Figure 14-7 the B $ightarrow$ E link has a flow of 2, so the E $ightarrow$ B backlink has a residual capacity of 2. (To improve the solution on the left, the algorithm must push back flow across the E $ightarrow$ B backlink to free up more capacity on the E $ightarrow$ F link. That's how the algorithm uses residual capacities.)

A residual capacity network is a network consisting of links and backlinks marked with their residual capacities. Figure 14-8 shows the residual capacity network for the network on the left in Figure 14-7. Backlinks are drawn with dashed lines.
