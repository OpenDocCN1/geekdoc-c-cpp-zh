Public-key encryption uses two keys: a public key and a private key. The public key is published, so everyone (including the attacker) knows it. The private key is known only to the receiver.

This is why the primes $p$ and $q$ must be large—to prevent an attacker from easily factoring $n$.

> NOTE Although factoring is believed to be a hard problem, and lots of people have spent a huge amount of effort studying it, there is no guarantee that some won't eventually come up with a way to factor large numbers quickly.

## Euler's Totient Function

Step 1 and 2 of the key generation algorithm are reasonably straightforward using the techniques described in Chapter 2.

Step 3 of the key-generation algorithm requires you to calculate Euler's totient function $\phi(n)$. The totient function, is also called the phi function, a function that gives the number of positive integers less than a particular number that are relatively prime to that number. For example, $\phi(12)$ is 4 because there are four numbers less than 12 that are relatively prime to it: 1, 5, 7, and 11.

Because a prime number is relatively prime to every positive integer than itself, $\phi(p) = p - 1$ if $p$ is prime.

It turns out that, if $p$ and $q$ are relatively prime, $\phi(p \cdot q) = \phi(p) \cdot \phi(q)$. If $p$ and $q$ are both primes, they are relatively prime, so in step 3 $\phi(n) = \phi(p \cdot q) = \phi(p) \cdot \phi(q) = (p-1) \cdot (q-1)$. This is easy to compute.

For example, suppose $p = 3$ and $q = 5$. Then $\phi(15) = \phi(3) \cdot \phi(5) = (3-1) \cdot (5-1) = 2 \cdot 4 = 8$. This is true because the positive integers smaller than 15 are relatively prime to 15 are the values 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 11, 13, and 14.

## Multiplicative Inverses

Step 4 in the key generation algorithm is reasonably straightforward. Simply pick random numbers between 1 and $\phi(e)$ and use the GCD algorithm to find if they are relatively prime to $e$.

Step 5 of the key-generating algorithm requires you to find the multiplicative inverse of $d$ modulo $\phi(n)$. In other words, find $e$ that $e \cdot d = 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}$. ($2 \cdot 3 \pmod{\phi(n)}$, $2 \cdot 3 \pmod{\phi(n)}$, and so on on until you discover a value that makes result.)

You can also use an extended GCD to find the value $e$ more efficiently. See http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_Euclidean_algorithm for more information on that algorithm.

## Public-Key Encryption and RSA

Public-key encryption uses two keys: a public key and a private key. The public key is published, so everyone (including the attacker) knows it. The private key is known only to the receiver.

A sender uses the public key to encrypt the message and sends the result to the receiver. Only the receiver, who has the private key, can decrypt the message. In contrast, other forms of encryption are called symmetric-key encryption where you use the same key to encrypt and decrypt messages.

One of the best-known public-key algorithms is RSA, which is named after those whose first described it: Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman.
