One more: if f(x) is scalar-valued, the matrix of second derivatives

```
\frac{d^2 f}{d\mathbf{x}^2} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_d} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_d \partial x_1} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_d \partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_d^2} \end{bmatrix},
```

is called the Hessian.

Here, we introduce a new notation, a vector containing only operators,

```
\nabla = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_d} \end{bmatrix}^T.
```

It is important to note that this is an operator, not a vector. We will do linear algebra sorts of things with it, but by itself, it has no value, not even really any meaning – it must be applied to something to have any meaning. For most of this book, we will deal with two-dimensional images, and with the two-dimensional form of this operator,

```
\nabla = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix}^T.
```

Apply this operator to a scalar, f, and we get a vector that does have meaning, the gradient of f:

```
\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix}^T.
```

Similarly, if f is a vector, we may define the divergence using the inner (dot) product (in all the following definitions, only the two-dimensional form of the ∇ operator defined in Eq. 3.21 is used. However, remember that the same concepts apply to operators of arbitrary dimension):

```
div \mathbf{f} = \nabla \mathbf{f} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} f_1 \\ f_2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial y}.
```

We will also have opportunity to use the outer product of the ∇ operator with a matrix, which is the Jacobian:

```
\nabla \times \mathbf{f} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix} [f_1 \quad f_2] = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial y} \\ \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix}.
```

# 3.2.7 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

If matrix A and vector x are conformable, then one may write the characteristic equation

```
A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}, \lambda \in \Re.
```

Since Ax is a linear operation, A may be considered as a transformation that maps x onto itself with only a change in length. There may be more than one eigenvalue, λ, which satisfies Eq. 3.26. For x ∈ ℝ^d, A will have exactly d eigenvalues (which are not, however, necessarily distinct). These may be found by solving det(A − λI) = 0. (But for d > 2, we do not recommend this method. Use a numerical package instead.)
