will thus calculate (by projection onto the basis vectors) the first \( m \) elements of \( \mathbf{y} \), and replace the others with constants, forming the estimate.

\(\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \sum_{i=1}^m y_i \mathbf{b}_i + \sum_{i=m+1}^d \alpha_i \mathbf{b}_i.\) (10.13)

The error that has been introduced by using some arbitrary constants, the \( \alpha \)’s of Eq. 10.13, rather than the elements of y, is given by

\(\Delta \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} - \sum_{i=1}^m y_i \mathbf{b}_i - \sum_{i=m+1}^d \alpha_i \mathbf{b}_i.\) (10.14)

but since

\(\mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=1}^m y_i \mathbf{b}_i + \sum_{i=m+1}^d y_i \mathbf{b}_i\) (10.15)

we arrive at

\(\Delta \mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=m+1}^d (y_i - \alpha_i) \mathbf{b}_i.\) (10.16)

If we think of x and therefore \( \Delta \mathbf{x} \) as random vectors, we can use the expected magnitude of \( \Delta \mathbf{x} \) to quantify how well our representation works.

\(\epsilon(m) = E \left\{ \sum_{i=m+1}^d \sum_{j=m+1}^d (y_i - \alpha_i) \mathbf{b}_i^T (y_j - \alpha_j) \mathbf{b}_j \right\}\) (10.17)

\(= E \left\{ \sum_{i=m+1}^d \sum_{j=m+1}^d (y_i - \alpha_i)(y_j - \alpha_j) \mathbf{b}_i^T \mathbf{b}_j \right\}\) (10.18)

noting that \( y_i \) is a scalar, and recalling Eq. 10.10, this becomes

\(\epsilon(m) = \sum_{i=m+1}^d E \{(y_i - \alpha_i)^2\}.\) (10.19)

To find the optimal choice for \( \alpha_i \), minimize with respect to \( \alpha_i \)

\(\frac{\partial \epsilon}{\partial \alpha_i} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \alpha_i} E \{(y_i - \alpha_i)^2\} = E \{-2(y_i - \alpha_i)\},\) (10.20)

which can be set to zero, resulting in

\(\alpha_i = E(y_i) = \mathbf{b}_i^T E\{\mathbf{x}\}.\) (10.21)

So, we should replace those elements of y that we do not measure by their expected values. Something both mathematically and intuitively appealing.
