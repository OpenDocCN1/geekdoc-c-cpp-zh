+   In addition, allocate space to hold a one-dimensional accumulator of lengths of the minor axis.
+   3. Scan through P and choose a point x1 that could be the end of the major axis of some ellipse. It could not be the end, for example, if the direction of the gradient would force the major axis to be off the image.
+   4. Now, choose a candidate for the other end of the major axis, x2. Various heuristics may be used to reduce the search for x2. For example 1) the other end must satisfy Mm <= |x1 - x2| <= MM, or 2) knowledge of the gradient direction at x1 provides information of where to search for x2.
+   5. The half length of the major axis, a, is |x1 - x2|/2, the origin, x0, is at (x1 + x2)/2, and the orientation of the ellipse is theta = tan^-1((y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1)).
+   6. Choose a point x such that |x - x0| < a. Let d = |x - x0|, and let f = |x2 - x|.
+   (a) Consider the triangle, x, x0, and x2, illustrated in Figure 9.8, which has an internal angle of tau. Since a^2 + d^2 - f^2 = 2ad cos tau, we can calculate cos tau:
+   cos tau = (a^2 + d^2 - f^2) / (2ad)
+   Let 2b denote the length of the minor axis, so that b is the "half length" of that axis. Then
+   b^2 = a^2 d^2 sin^2 tau / 2ad.
+   (b) Increment the accumulator for the length b.
+   (c) If any more pixels exist that satisfy the conditions of step 6, then repeat step 6 with those pixels.
+   7. Find the maximum of the accumulator. If the maximum is sufficiently high, then x1 and x2 are the endpoints of the major axis of an ellipse. Save this information. If the maximum of the accumulator is too small, continue scanning with step 3.
+   8. Clear the accumulator
+   9. Remove all the points just found on this ellipse from P.
+   10. Continue until the end of P is reached.

This algorithm is fast, efficient, and relatively easy to program and to understand. It has only one problem: if there is a real ellipse, but one end or the other is occluded, the algorithm will not work.
