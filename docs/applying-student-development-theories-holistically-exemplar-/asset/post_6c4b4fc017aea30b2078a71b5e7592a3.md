At *Launch*, each student was given a journal and pen. On the first day, they were instructed on the technique of free writing (Elbow, 1973; Lamott, 1994) and were challenged to keep their pens moving at all times. Mandatory journaling took place every morning as a large group. Everyone, including *Launch* staff, was instructed to find a spot, sit in silence, and write for 20 minutes. Sample prompts were provided each day if needed, but they were not required to adhere to the prompts. Example prompts were: *List three adjectives that describe how you’re feeling now, identify someone you want to get to know better and explain, and name three things you want to make a point to do tomorrow.*

With the attention to all three competency considerations described in Chickering and Reisser’s (1993) first vector of college students’ psychosocial development, the aim was to address new students’ most pressing developmental needs. Through these design elements, *Launch* participants were able to begin the fall term with several friendships already in place, opportunities to discover physical and manual strengths, and adjustments to their expectations for academic success.

## Student Involvement Theory

Though the retreat was hosted off campus, the staff wanted to ensure students were connected to campus and one another. Several guest speakers were invited to expose participants to various involvement opportunities at RMC so that students would feel comfortable seeking out opportunities and eventually get involved in activities that were of interest to them. Each morning meeting featured a guest speaker: Associate Dean of Students, Director of Disability Support Services, Director of the Career Center along with two young alumni, and the campus Chaplain.

Early in the second semester of the participants’ first year, a special *Launch* reunion meeting was offered to review various student leadership positions to which participants might want to consider applying. At RMC, applications for resident assistants, orientation leaders, tutors and mentors, and tour guide positions are due by late February. At the meeting, position descriptions were reviewed and discussions held about what students could expect from the selection process, such as the competitiveness of the positions.

They attended a brief *Launch* orientation meeting, then boarded a bus to a nearby adventure retreat facility. No phone or internet service was available at the facility. On the retreat grounds, students and college staff had access to two bunk cabins, a full-service kitchen, two common rooms, a pool, a lake, and retreat facility staff who could guide students through the adventure courses (i.e., high ropes, low ropes, zip-line, and water trampoline). The group returned to campus on the day the residence halls opened for the rest of the incoming class.

Each day began with a student-prepared breakfast. The first activity of the day was a guided journaling activity. A two-hour morning session that included guest presentations, activities, or panels followed. After a student-prepared lunch, small groups engaged in different adventure activities (i.e., lake day, low-ropes challenges, or high ropes). The late afternoon was scheduled for free time, with options for a grocery shopping trip, hiking, pool time, or hanging out. After a student-prepared dinner, a volunteer *Launch* participant would build a bonfire. All of the staff, student mentors, and participants came together at the bonfire for reflection ring time. At the reflection ring, every attendee was invited to respond to a sharing prompt (e.g., “What are your hopes and fears for *Launch*?”). After the reflection ring, participants were dismissed for evening free time, while the *Launch* staff and student mentors held a nightly reflection and meeting to prepare for the next day.

After *Launch*, several activities were held throughout the year. For each activity, participants were invited to bring friends. After one month, a reunion was held. After two months, the participants and their guests took a field trip to the Holocaust Museum in Washington, DC. After four months, the students took a field trip to an amusement park. And after six months, in February of their first year, another reunion was held. At this reunion, discussions were held about available leadership positions on campus, as well as what the application and selection processes looked like.

## Assessment and Evaluation

At the beginning of *Launch*, participants shared their reasons for attending. The students who attended seemed to share two qualities: those who had concerns about their transitions to college life, and those who had concerns about their competency. Some students had realistic concerns about their transitions because of challenging personal and theoretical constructs that were used as a base for the design of *Launch*: competence and social integration.

The assessment data showed that students reflected most explicitly and positively on the theoretical constructs related to interpersonal competence and social integration on campus. They did not mention specific activities when describing impacts related to these theoretical constructs, but on *Launch* overall. To put it simply: They were concerned about not having friends or supports on campus, and *Launch* gave them the opportunity to make friends and find supports on campus.

Based on the overarching goal of *Launch* and the literature on retention, the goals related to intellectual competence, perseverance as it relates to academic challenges, and creating a community of peers that protect diversity also are important. Through student reflections, some data was collected that indicated that the students appreciated the campus resources staff members who attended, staff members who serve in roles that address academic needs and campus climate. It would be helpful to create a way to collect assessment data that explicitly addresses all four of the program developmental goals and the corresponding outcomes, as well as the overarching goal of increasing the retention rates of the participants.

*Launch* brought together students who have lower retention rates in college. At the retreat, students expressed a great deal of concern about the transition to college and their competence. The staff members surmised that intentionally bringing together a group of students who have these concerns could have the potential to backfire. Students might reinforce and conflate one another’s concerns. The staff members determined that near-constant attention needed to be paid to messaging in promotional materials and program design considerations to make explicit the overarching goal, developmental goals, and outcomes of the retreat—and the *hopeful* nature of *Launch*.

## An Identity Development and Support Initiative for Black Men

Jonathan A. McElderry, Jordan Williams, Marcus Mayes, Donald D. Gilliam, and Curtis Taylor, Jr.

The Mizzou Black Men's Initiative is a program for first-year, Black male-identified students at the University of Missouri. The student development theories that informed the creation of the program are Black Identity Development (Cross & Phagen-Smith, 2001) and Sense of Belonging (Strayhorn, 2012). With theories as guides, the program aids in the successful transition of these individuals into the collegiate environment, with a focus on support, development, and involvement. This chapter provides a description of the program, guiding theories, and assessment and evaluation techniques. Additionally, discussion is included on lessons learned from applying theory as well as recommendations and resources necessary to impact the identity development and sense of belonging for Black males in the collegiate environment.

The University of Missouri ("Mizzou" or MU) located in Columbia, Missouri, was established in 1839. It is a land-grant research university and was the first public university west of the Mississippi River. Mizzou has 19 colleges and schools with more than 300 degree programs and is one of five universities in the United States that offers law, medicine, veterinary medicine, and a nuclear research reactor (University of Missouri, 2014). According to the Fall 2016 Student Body Profile (University of Missouri, 2016), the total enrollment was 33,266. Mizzou is a Predominantly White Institution (PWI). A total of 20,273 of the undergraduates identify as White, and 9,814 as White men.
