
While we based our practice in two well-known theories, we surmised we would not cultivate the undergraduate paraprofessional staff members’ buy-in by framing Passion to Action with student development theory or describing how departmental learning outcomes would be met. We believed we needed a more relatable and personal hook.

While brainstorming multimedia that might inspire our undergraduate paraprofessional staff members to embrace our new initiative, we came across the YouTube channel of Kid President, a young boy who produced a series of videos on leadership, compassion, and relationships, among other things. The video entitled A Pep Talk from Kid President to You (2013) served as the kindling for introducing “Props for Passion.” In the video, Kid President issued a call to action for humankind to “stop being boring,” “do something,” and “be on the road that leads to awesome.” He delivers a poignant call to never quit, because quitting limits the potential for the big successes of life and restricts people to their comfort zones. We used these sentiments as the inspiration for “Props for Passion” and framed a motivating question: “What will you create that will make the world awesome?” This broad question allowed our staff members to engage with the initiative in a personal manner and across many levels of comfort and familiarity with diversity topics while also progressing through Kolb’s (1984) stages of experiential learning, and while using Bloom’s revised Taxonomy of Educational Objectives (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) to craft the learning outcomes for their “Passion Project” programs.

Our second lesson was to understand the power of language. The Kid President used language that we hoped would cue our team to think about diversity, inclusion, and social justice, though he does not explicitly say any of those words. We found that using a popular social media platform aligned with the normal habits of our undergraduate paraprofessional staff members and further hooked them with programmatic outcomes such as honing communication skills, crafting presentations for impact, and incorporating “awesomeness” into their programming. It allowed us to freely use language that was simple and understandable, again, while creating learning outcomes for their programs based on Bloom’s taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). As we developed Passion to Action, we kept this video as the constant and consistent inspiration and showed it during both fall and winter pre-service training.

Our final lesson was understanding how the timing of Passion to Action would be reflected in short-term and long-term programmatic changes.

This new program would initially place the majority of our programming efforts in the spring semester, and then evolve to have returning undergraduate paraprofessional staff members ready to lead social justice programming in the fall. At various times in the year it felt as though none of the intended outcomes was being achieved, and it was reassuring that we had a strong theoretical framework and undergraduate paraprofessional staff members who were committed to being social justice educators.

## RECOMMENDATIONS AND RESOURCES

The *Passion to Action* program was created to respond to a perceived gap in comprehension and implementation of diversity and social justice efforts among the undergraduate paraprofessional staff members. To ensure that the program—or similar programs—are sustainable, we recommend the following:

### Align with Departmental Objectives

New York University’s RLHS provided five departmental program learning goals: Life Skills, Multiple Perspectives, Community, Diversity, and Wellness. Each hall’s leadership team defined how these goals would be achieved within their communities. Prior to *Passion to Action*, in Lafayette Hall, undergraduate paraprofessional staff members were submitting program proposals heavily representing Life Skills, Multiple Perspectives, Community, and Wellness, but few for the Diversity goal. One of the goals of *Passion to Action* was to enhance the alignment between the expectations of the department and the offerings in the building, and to facilitate programming that supported the Diversity goal.

## Theory-in-Use Core Concepts: Perspectives From Scholarly Practitioners

Katie Branch with Jeanne S. Hart-Steffes

*The examples provided in the previous chapters of student development theories used in programs and practices that promote student success demonstrate an array of holistic synergies. This chapter summarizes data from the proposals submitted for this book, and draws out thematic perspectives from lessons learned and recommendations made by contributing authors. Core concepts about applying student development theories in contemporary higher education contexts are highlighted, with the importance of scholarly practitioner engagement noted. Cautions or reminders are given about transferring theory use to multiple contexts and diverse student populations. Finally, applying theory in higher education environments is not magic; rather, it relies upon the knowledge, skills, and intentionality of practitioners dedicated to the holistic well-being of students.*

When analyzing information provided in the proposals received for this book as well as the contributing chapters selected ultimately for publication, there are patterns and themes that emerge. We received 26 complete proposals during the two-month period from when we issued a Call for Proposals and began final reviews for selection purposes. We sorted these proposals into the clusters of theories outlined: psychosocial and social identity development, cognitive development, and influences on development. Proposal authors identified four psychosocial and social identity theories that were used in contemporary practices, with the most commonly cited theory being Chickering and Reisser's (1993) Theory of Identity Development (i.e., the Seven Vectors). Proposals also cited Cross and Fhagen-Smith’s (2001) Model of Black Identity Development, Phinney’s (1989) Model of Ethnic Identity Development, and Davis’s (2010) scholarship on first-generation student experiences. As anticipated in the cluster of theories identified primarily as psychosocial in nature, some proposals also incorporated ideas from frameworks that emphasize *influences* on development (versus theories that describe adult development sequences).

From among cognitive development theories, nine frameworks were used in practice: Perry’s (1970) Scheme of Intellectual and Ethical Development; Belenky, Clinchy, Goldberger, and Tarule’s (1986) categories from *Women’s Ways of Knowing*; Kohlberg’s (1981) Theory of Moral Development; Gilligan’s (1982) Theory of Women’s Moral Development; Kitchener and King’s (1981) Reflective Judgment Model; Kegan’s (1994) Theory of Evolution of Consciousness; and Baxter Magolda’s (1992) Epistemological Reflection Model, Theory of Self-Authorship (2001), and Learning Partnerships Model (2004). The cognitive cluster of developmental theories was the one in which proposal authors brought in frameworks not listed on our Call for Proposals (i.e., Kegan’s Theory of Evolution of Consciousness and Baxter Magolda’s Learning Partnerships Model), making it evident that scholarly practitioners understood the links among these theories’ authors as well as foundational cognitive constructs. Baxter Magolda’s scholarship was cited frequently; scholarly practitioners embraced the direct connections among research, theory, and a model for using the theory in practice (i.e., her Learning Partnerships Model).

In the theory cluster labeled *Influences on Development*, proposals were received that made pragmatic links to all seven of the frameworks listed on our Call for Proposals, and Astin’s (1999) Theory of Student Involvement. These were as follows: Bronfenbrenner (developmental ecology), Schlossberg (transitions), Kolb (experiential learning), Jung or Myers and Briggs (personality type), Clifton and Anderson (personality strengths), Sedlacek (non-cognitive variables), and Strayhorn or others (sense of belonging). Because of the prevalence of Schlossberg’s Transition Theory (1981, 1984) and Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory (1984) across the proposals, we selected contemporary applications that emphasized applying those two theories holistically.
