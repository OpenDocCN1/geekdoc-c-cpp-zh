University, these resources help staff who serve international students understand what they expect from higher education, and how they describe their experiences, which can inform the design and components of mentoring programs.

There are other institutions that have programs similar to Tippie Buddies, many designed to help ease the transition of new international students. Higher education and student affairs professionals who are considering domestic-international student buddy programs should consider examining Georgia State University’s International Exchange Student Buddy Program (https://cas.gsu.edu/international-exchange-student-buddy/) and the International Student Buddy Program at Columbia College Chicago (https://students.colum.edu/student-diversity-and-inclusion/international-student-scholar-services/buddy-program.php).

In this chapter, we proposed just one of many possible methods to create environments and opportunities for undergraduate students to explore their ethnic identities. We posit that our own success with the program is contextually (and potentially even institutionally) bound, but the relative ease with which a buddy program can be created makes it a feasible option across institutional settings.

Research suggests it is important to understand the multiple identities individuals hold and the holistic impact of different components of identity (e.g., Iwamoto & Liu, 2010). Ethnic identity exploration and development as a process may occur regardless of intentional programming, but our hope is that by offering the opportunity and structured, supportive environment which encourages students to interact and engage in dialogue, it allows the process to be both positive and meaningful.
