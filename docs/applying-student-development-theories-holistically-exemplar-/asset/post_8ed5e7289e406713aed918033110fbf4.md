Basic job functions needed to be included in the IAP (e.g., RAs complete residence hall rounds, write incident reports as needed, build community; SOULS recruit, select, and train an orientation team). Many of these job parameters may be set by departmental or divisional expectations through the forms of policy or professional standards. RAs at Miami University have specific programmatic requirements and parameters but the way in which each parameter is met is the decision of each RA. Resident Assistants select how they would be held accountable for the timeliness and completion of the IAP. It is completely acceptable for each RA to have a plan unique to them, as noted in the “individualized” label of the accountability plan. The goal of IAPs is to encourage and celebrate the ways in which student staff members already know themselves and their strengths, challenges, and areas for growth. Through this approach, student staff members are more heavily invested in their role because they help create and drive how they will do their work. This allows them to identify experiences that help them make decisions using the knowledge and expertise that they already have as students, leaders, and campus staff members.

On a fundamental level, IAPs vary in structure and content based on the supervisor’s as well as the student supervisee’s needs and expectations. In the example provided in Table 7.1, one RA supervisee decided that they would like to make meaning of their experience, work role, and values by writing a blog. Another student made meaning of their role by tracking the progress toward their goals on a spreadsheet and then processing or celebrating their work verbally. Each of these evaluation tools was co-created by the supervisor and the supervisee to best meet the needs of the residential community, the staff team, and the RA themselves.

Although IAPs are co-created, it is the work of supervisors to decide on and communicate the parameters within which student staffs are able to be creative. For example, a Resident Director may plan to include some specific expectations and initiatives in their planning. At Miami University, some of the parameters include:

- Meeting departmental expectations and contractual obligations
- Performing duty/rounds in the hall
- Attending staff meetings
- Upholding the Student Code of Conduct
- Upholding and implementing programs aligned with programming requirements
- Implementing strategies related to community building, cultural competency, intrapersonal development, and academic success

As is evident, student staff members are not left to create what the expectations of their role should be; rather, they do have the opportunity to shape what their particular experience looks like and feels like on a day-to-day basis within the context of larger parameters.

To create an IAP, student staff members take the parameters set forth by their supervisors and examine the best way to go about meeting those expectations. ## Assessment and Evaluation

Self-authorship and meaning-making, as complex concepts, require complex approaches to assessment (Baxter Magolda & King, 2007). Three approaches to measure self-authorship include using existing measures for cognitive change, using a scoring method to measure student outcomes, or using semi-structured interviews and student narratives (Pizzolato, 2007). Thus, to assess IAPs, we have decision; moving to the edge of thinking by considering the most, least, best, and worst extremes; and asking the same question in different ways to gain deeper reflection and understanding.

The process of assessing self-authorship can be a developmental activity in and of itself (Baxter Magolda & King, 2007). When assessing IAPs, we also are assessing student learning, developmental capacity, and meaning-making. As supervisors, we focus on the intentional reflection of student staff to increase their developmental capacity and understanding themselves as learners. We assess our student staff members, both RAs and SOULs, throughout their experience in their positions, during an academic course that relates to their positions, and in exit interviews as they leave positions.

## LESSONS FROM APPLYING THEORY ARTFULLY

Overall, in our experience, implementing IAPs with student staff is rewarding for both supervisors and students. Student staff members using IAPs are afforded more ownership for their developmental journey and work, creating an explicit learning environment for these students. For supervisors, the additional ownership and responsibility on the part of student staff created less direct constant oversight. RAs and SOULs were effective in their roles when faced with adaptive challenges, which occur often in these roles.

IAPs take time to develop and implement and must be living, breathing plans. Because initiatives and plans are so personalized, supervisors must be open to making IAPs a focal point of the staff dynamic, keeping IAPs at the forefront of the work. IAPs become meaningless if they are not revisited and prioritized. Supervisors must be open to change and edits over the course of a year. This is a critical connection back to how the LPM aligns with IAPs. In the LPM, meaning is mutually constructed, which means that it must be flexible and adaptable as new needs emerge or a student is prepared for greater challenge. If a supervisor is tied to the idea that they are a distributor of knowledge that their supervisee must simply receive in a transactional relationship, then opportunities for development are limited. As students grow and develop, and as environmental events impact students’ lives and understanding of the world they live in, it may make sense to adjust IAPs throughout the student staff member’s term. It is also important to make changes if something that the RA or SOUL thought would work to hold them accountable simply does not. The supervisor should not hesitate to encourage the student staff members to revisit this plan if it is not effective.

Managing this iterative IAP process increases the ownership that student staff has over their work, and the IAP must be heavily dependent on, and thus the responsibility of, the student staff member. It is, however, important to note that depending on each student staff member's developmental capacity and experiences, the student may need more or less support from the supervisor during this process. One should not expect that all student staff will have the developmental capacity to complete this on their own, and the supervisor should provide the appropriate level of support to each staff member to help build a robust and meaningful IAP.

This appropriate level of support can take on different forms. Baxter Magolda and King (2007) suggest that the supervisor must offer time, space, and encouragement for an effective interview or conversation. If one does not allow time and silence for the student staff member to think and reflect, then they may curtail important processing time. Although there are strategies to approach these conversations as developmental practice, there is no simple procedure to follow to understand one's developmental capacity without investing significant time to understand how they make meaning. Through our own learning and growth as supervisors and interviewers, we can become more effective, but this requires self-reflection and developmental work for ourselves.

## RECOMMENDATIONS AND RESOURCES

### IAPs Are Instrumental for a Campus-Wide Approach

There are significant opportunities to continue to utilize IAPs to connect self-authorship with student staff supervision through use of the LPM. IAPs could be expanded to include entire departments or student life areas on college campuses. For maximum benefit, this requires the entire organization to commit to using this approach.
