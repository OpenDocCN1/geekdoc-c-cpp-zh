
## RECOMMENDATIONS AND RESOURCES

It is important to instill a nuanced understanding of the 8KQ and to ensure that students understand the larger concepts of each question, not just the key words or phrases as they are stated in the programs. To achieve these goals, we make the following recommendations.

### Allot Significant Time for Development

Thorough and non-confirmatory usage of the questions also requires significant time. When students analyze a scenario, allow plenty of time for self-interrogation; stretching their minds is a process. We encourage students to acknowledge an immediate reaction, set it aside, and ask questions in each of the ethical areas to develop this practice.

### Offer Repeated 8KQ Application Opportunities

Multiple interactions with the 8KQ allow students to develop the habit of asking questions as part of their decision-making and to recognize when ethical dilemmas arise. Using the 8KQ in groups is also an important developmental tool to expose students to diverse viewpoints and stimulate dissonance.

### Explore Resources About the Program

Resources available at the program’s website include the Eight Key Question Handbook (The Madison Collaborative, 2013) that explains each of the 8KQ, the JMU Ethical Reasoning Rubric© (2014) used to rate student assessment essays and understand what is considered proficient in ethical reasoning at JMU, and a link to request 8KQ educational materials.

As partners in planning and delivering It’s Complicated to incoming students, the office of Orientation hosts the website for students to access their pre-move-in assignment related to ethical reasoning. On this site, you may read the written version and view the video of the current case study used for the program. The detailed guide or script designed yearly for It’s Complicated facilitators is available upon request.

## Promoting Self-Authorship Using Individualized Accountability Plans

Erik Sorensen and Elizabeth K. Walsh

Student staff members are critical to the success of student life divisions in higher education. Resident assistants, orientation leaders, and other student leaders are at the forefront of work with additional students. As such, their development and effectiveness in their roles are of the utmost importance. A specific tool and technique to foster development is the use of Individualized Accountability Plans (IAPs) that are grounded in the theoretical perspectives of Kegan’s (1994) Orders of Consciousness and Baxter Magolda’s (2004) self-authorship. IAPs help student staff and their supervisors co-create action plans to inform their work, and decide how each staff member will be held accountable. Co-creating a plan of this nature is a developmental practice of its own—akin to Baxter Magolda’s (2004) Learning Partnerships Model. This chapter describes the philosophical and theoretical background of IAPs, a process of implementing IAPs, possible approaches to assess effectiveness of IAPs, and recommendations to develop further the IAP process.

Individualized Accountability Plans (IAPs) are action plans co-created by supervisors and supervisees that help staff members take ownership of their positions, including how they approach and are held accountable for their work. This chapter describes how the authors have implemented IAPs with student staff at Miami University in Ohio. Erik serves as an Assistant Director of Residence Life at Miami University; he works closely with Resident Assistants (RAs) as well as supervises full-time and graduate hall staff. Elizabeth, who is known as "Liz," serves as the Assistant Director for Orientation and Transition Programs working with Student Orientation Undergraduate Leaders (SOULs); Liz has prior experience at Miami University working in Residence Life with RAs. Both Erik and Liz studied in the Student Affairs and Higher Education program at Miami University: Liz as a master’s student and Erik as a doctoral student. Both authors have implemented IAPs with student staff with the guidance of both our coursework and professional experience.

Miami University is situated primarily in the small, Midwestern town of Oxford, Ohio. Founded in 1809, the charter of the institution preceded the establishment of Oxford. Thus, Miami University’s Oxford campus is highly residential. It is a four-year public university that has a large undergraduate population of approximately 17,000 students. Approximately 40% of Miami students are in-state students. Demographically, 51.5% of students are female, and 48.5% of students are male. Domestic students are 87% of the population, while international students represent 13% of the student population. Domestic students of color are 14.3% of the student body. Because of strong Miami University tradition, legacy students (e.g., sons, daughters, siblings of alumni) are 11.7% of students (Miami University Admissions, 2017).

Its Carnegie basic classification is a doctoral university with higher research activity. Miami University holds strong traditions and values in academia and co-curricular life, making it a great place to challenge students to find their voices, values, and passions. This can include partnering with students in co-creating experiences inside and outside of the classroom.

### DESCRIPTION OF INDIVIDUALIZED ACCOUNTABILITY PLANS

IAPs were originated by the Office of Residence Life as a tool for Resident Directors to actively engage student learning and personal growth in the Resident Assistant (RA) position. IAPs have since been applied in multiple contexts with different student staff positions in order to closely align human development with student affairs practice. The goal of using IAPs is to help student staff members make meaning of their jobs and leadership roles on an individualized level that propel them toward the construct of self-authorship. IAPs serve as a strategy to provide student staff members with greater autonomy and encourage them to describe the complexity of the problems in the world through two types of challenges when faced with change: adaptive challenges and technical problems. Technical problems can be solved by expert knowledge, where adaptive challenges require new learning to solve. Kegan and Lahey (2009) suggest that approaching adaptive challenges with technical solutions often does not work to the benefit of those involved. These solutions are ultimately not optimized as more creative, learning-centered solutions. Also important to note is that adaptive solutions often lead to the growth of developmental capacity of the learner. Student staff roles manage a series of adaptive challenges, which includes the unpredictable element of working with other human beings. Student staff members who address adaptive challenges through their role often do much of their work without direct supervisory oversight at all times, and so these staff members need to be able to make complex and mature decisions on their own. A self-authored form of mind helps staff members make legitimately internalized decisions (Baxter Magolda, 2004).

A framework that supervisors can use to grow developmental capacity toward self-authorship in student staff members is through the use of the Learning Partnerships Model (LPM; Baxter Magolda, 2004). Three key principles of the LPM are to validate students as knowers, to situate learning within the learner’s experience, and to mutually construct meaning (Baxter Magolda, 2004). LPM allows for an environment that fosters challenge and support, but always in concert with the involvement of others in the partnership.

A specific strategy that both authors have used to engage in a learning partnership with student staff members is the development of Individualized Accountability Plans. IAPs provide an opportunity for student staff to take additional ownership and responsibility for their work. Details and recommendations on the implementation of IAPs with student staff members are the focus of the remainder of this chapter.
