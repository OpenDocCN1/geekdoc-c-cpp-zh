
In regard to institutional characteristics, all proposals involved four-year institutions of higher education—although one also linked directly to a two-year community college located in the Midwest region of the United States. All geographic regions of the contiguous United States were represented by the institutions of higher education identified in proposals. Institutional control included U.S. state-chartered (i.e., public) institutions as well as those considered private; no proposals were received from for-profit institutions. Among private institutions, most were independent—although religiously affiliated colleges also were represented. Institutions considered small, medium, and large—based on student enrollment figures—were represented. All small-size institutions were private and nearly all of the large-size institutions were public universities.

When looking across all of the complete proposals received, it was evident that the contemporary theory-in-practice applications focused on students as staff members as well as other student leaders. The emphasis by higher education and student affairs practitioners on the academic missions of their institutions (i.e., student learning support and retention) was clear. Assisting with transitions in, during, and out of institutions; building interpersonal relationships and attending to community dynamics; and making meaning of knowledge and experiences were hallmarks of the examples of student development theories applied holistically.

## THEMES ACROSS CHAPTERS

Following a thorough review of all proposals received, nine were selected for inclusion in this book. The selection process incorporated feedback and suggestions from current Master of Science degree students in the College Student Personnel graduate program at the University of Rhode Island; the perspectives of these emerging student affairs professionals helped to ensure both applicability and understandability of theory-in-practice examples. The exemplars of theory-in-practice in the preceding chapters demonstrated multiple approaches using relevant theories, covered a range of functional areas, and examined environments at a variety of institutional types. When looking across the *Lessons Learned and Recommendations* and *Resources* sections of chapters for patterns as well as unique perspectives, the following themes emerged.

### Intentionality

The deliberate and purposeful attention to using student development theories and models was evident, especially in design of outcomes. While not necessarily stated directly as programmatic goals, influencing student development and learning was reinforced continuously. Practitioners also demonstrated intentionality and discernment in monitoring for—and countering—deficit approaches grounded in negative stereotypes.

### Structure

Organization and clarity about intended outcomes as well as the components of programs provided structured guidance with practical significance. Informing and reminding those participating in (i.e., students) or linked to (e.g., staff, faculty, alumni, civic leaders) initiatives about intended outcomes often was a pattern. These outcomes, while inclusive of human development aims, were not solely developmental.

### Reflection

Reflection was a core component, both for students and those involved with implementation specifics. The powerful combination of experience and reflection influences growth, as noted by educational philosopher and reformist John Dewey (1933). The deep reflection demonstrated by those implementing programs aligned closely with intentionality and structure.

### Implementation Inconsistencies

Some practitioners noted that implementation specifics within programs or initiatives were not carried out consistently. This was both acceptable and, at times, helped to advance student learning and development. Sometimes various staff used differing techniques, which could be used as teachable moments for students. On other occasions, inconsistencies demonstrated adaptations to the uniqueness of students.

Adapting for varying developmental levels of students led to changes in how programs were carried out. Practitioners were mindful about human agency (i.e., respecting individual decisions) and noted the lack of direct linearity in student development. Thus, having programs be voluntary or having flexible elements within programs were important.

### Challenges of Assessment and Resource Sustainability

Because potential developmental outcomes are not only cumulative but also noticeable only after time has elapsed, assessment of specific developmental outcomes was challenging. Thus, most assessment and corresponding evaluative decisions were most relevant for improving implementation aspects—especially processes. In addition, fruitful assessment came from products or processes incorporated naturally as part of a program or initiative.

Resource allocation was an ongoing challenge. Struggles to find money to support the personnel needed for implementation of initiatives as well as potential financial opportunity costs to students who participated in certain programs were noted (e.g., a pre-orientation program, which could result in matriculating students ending summer employment earlier than peers do). For the sustainability of programs, institutional buy-in is key; thus, practitioners commented on clear links to the mission (or vision) of their functional area, division, college, or institution. To help with financial support, practitioners suggested working with institutional advancement officers; for assistance with personnel needs, seeking out local community members was an option. Connecting with intra-institutional resources was important to informing invested constituencies, gaining buy in, and enhancing achievement of intended outcomes.

The holistic elements of theory-in-practice applications were complex and required scholarly perspectives. Knowing and using student development theories, while meaningful, often was combined with other relevant scholarship (e.g., content-related theories, research findings, publications from professional associations). ### Some Cautions or Reminders

While the contributing authors in this book might make applying student development theories seem “easy,” none used this adjective—or the adverb “easily”—to describe their work. Rather, practitioners shared perspectives that highlighted complex and contextual decisions. Applying student development theories holistically was a blend of science with art. Readers need to keep in mind that the examples provided in this book are not for wholesale or ready transfer to their own institutions of higher education; there are no recipes for easy replication. There are ideas, however, for consideration, reflection, and discussion that can jumpstart transferability.

While the number of adult development theories used by higher education and student affairs professionals has grown tremendously, readers need to keep in mind that much of this scholarship has been conducted in the United States. With globalization of student affairs *practices* (see Mikk & Steglitz, 2017), it may be tempting to overlook the socio-historic context in which these *theories* came into existence. Knowing such information does not mean using these theories with students from other parts of the world lacks efficacy but caution needs to be exercised about theory relevancy in intercultural contexts.

Additional caveats include being mindful of the changing nature of language, which shifts interpretations of theoretical terms (e.g., intersectionality; see Bartlett, 2017), and the need to use theories from multiple academic disciplines (e.g., counseling, management) to boost outcomes. Finally, while this book provides examples of theories applied at the programmatic level, it is important to remember that much work of higher education and student affairs professionals takes place on a daily basis with individual students. The application of student development and learning theories with each student encountered can be just as meaningful.
