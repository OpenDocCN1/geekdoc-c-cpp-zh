As students grow in their intellectual and moral abilities, they may engage with the 8KQ more broadly, and would hypothetically move progressively from a self-centered worldview to a more inclusive worldview, reflecting the shift from SLO One and Two to SLO Three. For example, a shallow, self-focused application of the fairness question, “*How can I act equitably and balance all interests,*” may consider the equal distribution of a good, preferably in the decision-maker’s favor. Whereas, a goodness as self-sacrifice (Gilligan) or conventional (Kohlberg) application of the fairness question would bring about questions of the distribution of justice or fair practices for everyone involved. As indicated by Gilligan (1982), this stage may be represented by self-sacrifice, swinging to the other end of the self-focused pendulum, resulting in the temporary abandonment of caring for oneself.

SLO Three most closely reflects advancement in the mid-development stages of multiplicity, member of society, and goodness as self-sacrifice/from goodness to truth. In these stages, thinkers are questioning legitimacy of authority and peers may become relevant in decision-making. With the ability to see themselves as decision-makers, students can deeply understand and apply the key questions to scenarios. They can challenge their own thinking when answers to those questions conflict, providing cognitive dissonance.

Similarly, the transition to Kohlberg’s (1981) post-conventional stage should reflect a further nuanced understanding of fairness, for example, perhaps demonstrated by a realistic balancing of multiple inequities while maintaining an empathic approach or aiming to create justice among multiple groups of people that may or may not include the self. In advanced 8KQ ethical reasoning, the thinker (decision-maker) would raise additional questions of rights, outcomes, character, liberty, authority, and responsibilities applied to all parties involved in the situation. The Ethical Reasoning in Action program adds additional “voices” to the important moral conversations and decisions in a sea of ill-structured problems that require complex, nuanced, and informed decisions.
