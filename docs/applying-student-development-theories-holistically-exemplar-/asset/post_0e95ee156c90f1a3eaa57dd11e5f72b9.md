## DESCRIPTION OF THE MIZZOU BLACK MEN’S INITIATIVE

The MBMI is a leadership development program implemented by the Gaines/Oldham Black Culture Center. The staff at the center understand that leaving home and coming to a PWI can be challenging for students of color. The purpose of the program is to effectively assist first-year Black males in their transition into the Mizzou collegiate environment. Three pillars guide the operation of the program: support, development, and involvement.

Nationally, Black men have the lowest graduation rates of racial groups and genders (Harper, 2006). MBMI was created in 2009 in response to low retention and graduation rates of Black male students at Mizzou. The first iteration of the program was open to all Black undergraduate men. In 2013, the scope of the program was modified to focus solely on incoming Black first-year males. Over time, the program leaders added increased structure, incorporated academic credit, and added one more goal: to create the next generation of student leaders on Mizzou’s campus.

The current iteration of the program includes a two-semester, credit-bearing course that entails:
- presentations by and discussions with campus leaders and successful Black men
- leadership training and participation in service programs
- membership in student organizations on campus
Class discussions, activities, and assignments are intended to promote reflection and meaning-making for participants.

## Guiding Theories

According to *The Climate for Underrepresented Groups and Diversity on Campus* (Hurtado & Ruiz, 2012), one in five Black students feel some sort of exclusion on their college campuses. Furthermore, Black students have directly or indirectly targeted racial dynamics as a factor that affects their psychological well-being while attending a PWI (Hurtado & Ruiz, 2012). A space where a strong sense of belonging can be achieved: The program ensures interaction with a diverse group of Black men with multiple identities who are engaged on campus and in the community.

## Implementation Specifics

### Leadership Team

The MBMI program is led by a team comprised of a professional staff member, two teaching assistants, and six peer mentors. The staff member works in the Gaines/Oldham Black Culture Center (GOBCC) under the Division of Inclusion, Diversity, and Equity. The teaching assistants are typically undergraduate seniors who have served in the peer mentor role and are selected by professional staff at the GOBCC. The six peer mentors are chosen from previous years’ MBMI programs; they are selected and trained in the spring semester in preparation for the program in the fall. Peer mentors are to serve as role models, meet with their assigned mentees biweekly, and plan and implement community service events, study halls, and social activities.

### Recruitment and Selection

MBMI participants are recruited both informally (e.g., word of mouth, social media, emails, etc.), and formally, (e.g., summer orientation and letters to all incoming Black first-year males). Prospective MBMI participants are required to complete an application that gauges their interest in the program; strengths and weaknesses; how they define leadership, service, and mentorship; what they believe they can contribute to the program; and what their individual goals are and anticipated challenges they may face during their collegiate experience. After reviewing applications, the leadership team creates cohorts of students from various backgrounds, intended majors, and geographic locations. The program accepts 25 to 30 students for each cohort each year, which translates to approximately 10% of the incoming class of Black first-year males. Cohort size is constrained by staffing and funding resources.

### Structure

MBMI consists of a two-semester course titled, *SSC 1150: Student Success Center*. The course objectives are to foster a sense of belonging, promote engagement in the curricular and co-curricular life of the university, encourage self-responsibility, and facilitate understanding by the analysis of each student’s projected versus actual GPA is completed at the end of the first semester. Retention and graduation rates are also tracked for participants. Several other formative and summative assessment methods are used throughout the program.

## Support

Student support is partially gauged through a needs assessment administered early in the program and throughout the semester. Participants are asked to articulate what they expect to learn from the MBMI program overall and their expectations of the leadership team. Participants also establish community norms with their classmates to encourage a positive learning environment. Both the needs and expectations are revisited in one-on-one meetings with members of the leadership team. The goal is to ensure the MBMI program remains focused on the identified needs of its participants. While providing general resources and information is relevant to the students’ matriculation, the program revises the curriculum to meet the students’ individual needs.

Students are required to meet with their peer mentors regularly throughout each semester. During the meetings, peer mentors dialogue with their assigned mentees to gauge their personal, social, and academic transitions. Based on what is discussed in the meetings, peer mentors provide recommendations on how the MBMI participants can adjust and improve their experiences. Peer mentors work to create group mentor sessions based on identified needs and obstacles. In doing so, participants can connect with their peers on a personal level based on the vulnerability and transparency of their relationship. Participants also are required to meet with the instructor at least once a semester to discuss academic obstacles and progress.

After attending campus events and completing the reflection papers, participants engage in peer-led discussions about what they learned and wrote about for the assignments. These group discussions allow students to affirm one another’s experiences as well as challenge their perspectives in a way that contributes to a supportive learning environment. Reflection papers help give the leadership team insights into the needs of the participants. In turn, members of the leadership team can utilize information learned from the papers in their one-on-one meetings.

## Development

The development of MBMI members is facilitated through curricular and co-curricular activities designed to address students holistically, and specifically to address the development of healthy Black identities as students navigate adult nigrescence (Cross, 1971). Given the students are most likely to be in the Encounter and Immersion-Emersion stages, the out-of-class and in-class activities, such as reflection papers, provide experiences and opportunities for students to challenge their feelings about Black identity and immerse themselves in Black culture. Additionally, the MAB experience allows participants to engage in a variety of service opportunities, fosters individual growth, and strengthens the cohesiveness of the group. The combination of activities on the trip prompts discussion, reflection, and development as students challenge themselves collectively and individually as Black men. These challenges are often highlighted in the nightly reflections and discussions throughout the trip.

The class reflection papers encourage participants to critically examine and reflect on several academic and social topics, while simultaneously addressing their Black identity development and sense of belonging. While the students are not prompted to specifically identify where they are (i.e., a stage) in the Black Identity Development model (Cross & Phagen-Smith, 2001), the reflections help shape discussion for one-on-one meetings, in-class discussions, and future reflection papers. For example, a student who has actively engaged in curricular and co-curricular activities may demonstrate a commitment to change (Immersion-Emersion stage). The leadership team can then encourage them in transitioning to a state of embracing what it means to be Black and exuding self-love (Internalization/Internalization-Commitment stage).

Harper and Quaye (2007) suggested that higher levels of out-of-class engagement directly contribute to stronger racial identity attitudes for Black male students. Given the MBMI program is an initiative of the Gaines/Oldham Black Culture Center, the participants and their families are immediately connected to the staff and a robust programming agenda. The GOBCC is an immediate source of support to aid in the participants’ development. These connections and opportunities assist participants in advancing from the Encounter to the Immersion-Emersion stage.

## Involvement

To aid in their successful transition to the MU campus, MBMI participants are encouraged to get involved in campus activities. Involvement refers to the investment of physical and psychological energy in various activities (Astin, 1984), and positively impacts developing a sense of belonging and a healthy Black identity (Strayhorn, 2008).
