Issues that prevented them from engaging in these topics included a lack of common language (e.g., “inclusivity” used to describe being considerate and welcoming to others rather than acknowledging and validating others’ social identities), self-confidence (e.g., only external facilitators were secured for diversity-related programming), and ability to dialogue (e.g., intentionally disengaging from casual conversations about diversity because of discomfort).

We created and implemented “Props for Passion” to address these challenges with the hope that it would lead to more face-to-face discussions and ultimately enhance the social justice culture in the residential community. After participating in the intervention, our undergraduate paraprofessional staff members reported having more conversations oriented toward social justice, enhanced opportunities to collaborate in planning programs related to social justice, and a greater confidence in engaging with diversity topics with their residents and colleagues. Based on these assessments and the returning undergraduate paraprofessional staff members’ excitement for the program, we restructured the initiative into *Passion to Action*, a two-part program with “Props for Passion” (sharing a personal story) in the fall semester and “Passion Projects” (social justice activity to share with and bring to the community) in the spring semester.

## Guiding Theories

We structured *Passion to Action* according to Kolb’s (1984) stages of experiential learning. The program goals of *Passion to Action* were two-fold: (a) undergraduate paraprofessional staff members would be able to connect to and share their own passions and (b) undergraduate paraprofessional staff members would be able to implement more interpersonal, emotional, and intellectually challenging social justice programming in the community. The activities in this stage required critical thinking, conceptualization, and implementation, and were informed with higher-order outcomes on Bloom’s revised cognitive and knowledge dimensions, including *carry out, use, integrate, design,* and *create* (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). For many undergraduate paraprofessional staff members, the higher-order activity of “integration” was challenging, especially with the two seemingly disparate concepts of “my passion” and “what I do with my residents.” For some, this stage required more vulnerability than presenting in front of the team because it required sharing with the wider audience of residents within the building. Full staff (i.e., professional staff, graduate students, faculty fellows) involvement was key for providing support to the undergraduate paraprofessional staff members. A faculty fellow shared, “It was also very helpful to discover commonalities with other members of the staff, including undergraduate paraprofessional staff members, through their own passions, and it actually led me to approach some of them with ideas to program together.” Some undergraduate paraprofessional staff members quickly connected their passion to programming ideas, like Riya who reflected that she knew she wanted the program to “serve as a vehicle for educating others on the critical gaps present in the healthcare system.” For others it involved working through the intimacy of sharing their passion, issues conceptualizing the passion into a structured activity with residents, or gaining skills in program preparation.

### Stage 4: Active Experimentation

The final goal was for undergraduate paraprofessional staff members to design programs for building residents that engaged them in social justice. We asked each undergraduate paraprofessional staff member to engage in an active experimentation activity and consider the details required to engage the residents who attend their program event in social justice work. For this activity, we utilized outcome language informed by Bloom’s revised taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001), such as *generate, assemble, design,* and *create,* to structure learning in this area. This activity centered on understanding social justice work, program concept and flow, and implementation skills.

This goal was a stretch for many undergraduate paraprofessional staff members, especially envisioning themselves as the facilitator or leader for this type of program. Connecting passion topics to a social justice program was usually possible, but was made more difficult depending on how related the initial “Props for Passion” (i.e., personal sharing of an important topic) topic was to social justice concepts. For example, some undergraduate paraprofessional staff members presented generically on “family” and had to critically examine to find a social justice connection. For others, the process was seamless because the passion topic related directly. For example, Rolah shared that he facilitated a program about disabilities called *Walk a Day in My Shoes*. He set up stations to emulate different disabilities. He said that it was a completely different program from anything he had ever done, seen, or attended. He indicated that it felt like a bigger accomplishment because he got people to think more about themselves and their lives and put things into perspective.

Over time, we observed this outcome was often achieved in their second year as undergraduate paraprofessional staff members engaged in subsequent cycles of *Passion to Action*. First, *Passion to Action* exposed the undergraduate paraprofessional staff members to new ideas, concepts, and topics around diversity, inclusion, and social justice. Second, consistently discussing the “Passion to Action Macro-Cycle” gave staff a roadmap to use outside of their roles. Eventually, returning undergraduate paraprofessional staff members were planning social justice programs concurrent with “Props for Passion” in the fall and completing their “Passion Projects” on new topics in the spring.

Opportunities included staff meetings, one-on-one meetings, resident feedback (e.g., surveys, conduct meetings, hall government), and program proposals and assessments for "Passion Projects." During "Props for Passion," staff members demonstrated high levels of vulnerability as they presented their passion area in front of a large group of peers. That vulnerability was met with validation, as seen in the ways that the undergraduate paraprofessional staff members supported one another, and the ways in which the information shared resonated with many different colleagues. Ninga shared, "The more vulnerable the topic, the more supportive they were! It was very cathartic." Similarly, Riya reflected, "While sharing my story and becoming emotional, I heard murmurs of agreement and support from the audience—something that emboldened me."

During "Passion Projects," we observed that the programs were often higher quality (e.g., more advanced planning, better marketing, multiple stakeholders) than others developed in the spring semester. Hyland shared, "I was way more invested in this particular program because I felt that I was actually sharing a part of myself, and not just meeting program/event quota." Undergraduate paraprofessional staff members often programmed together and supported one another's visions and the residents who attended their events. Bryanna shared, "Helping on other Passion Projects, and even thinking about my own, I made lasting connections with residents who were genuinely interested in the topic and gave us a 'common bond' regarding the program."

In self-assessment surveys, the undergraduate paraprofessional staff members demonstrated an ability to articulate how the initiatives were achieving the learning outcomes they intended for their residents. For example, Rolah articulated, "The goal was to teach residents about different disabilities that many people have to live with . . . people came in not taking it seriously but were the most vocal . . . talking about their feelings and why the activity resonated with them."

## LESSONS FROM APPLYING THEORY ARTFULLY

Our first lesson was determining that the diversity and social justice training, at the time, was not enough for our team to be effective social justice programmers. The undergraduate paraprofessional staff members recognized their own knowledge, confidence, and skill gaps in engaging and implementing these opportunities at Lafayette Hall.
