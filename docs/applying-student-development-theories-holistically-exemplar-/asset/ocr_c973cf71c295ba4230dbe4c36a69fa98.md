Perry’s (1970) *Scheme of Intellectual and Ethical Development* provided a structure to understand what we should expect from students with varying intellectual abilities and how students might interpret and make meaning of different ethical challenges (in Evans, Forney, Guido, Patton, & Renn, 2010). Kohlberg’s (1981) *Stages of Moral Development* added to our understanding of how moral development occurs and what thoughts/actions/choices would be indicative of movement among developmental stages, especially when the reasoner sees through the eyes of justice and autonomy (in Evans et al., 2010; in Gilligan, 1982). Gilligan’s (1982) relationship-based “ethic of care” work expanded our vision to include the voices of care (empathy) and responsibilities when reasoning. Important to the premise of asking 8KQ when reasoning, Gilligan recognized that the one voice may be predominant in one’s thinking, “both ‘justice’ and ‘care’ voices are inherent in the life cycle, constituting alternative grounds on which to evaluate the moral” (Pascarella & Terenzini, 2005, p. 44).

In Perry’s (1970) cognitive development framework, many incoming traditional-aged college students are *dualistic* thinkers conceiving of ethical and other decisions in simple right or wrong terms, or considering parents/authority figures as the individual with answers (Pascarella & Terenzini, 2005). As students learn new methods of inquiry in college, they move into the *multiplicity* stage, in which peers become valid sources of information, they see multiple ideas and opinions as right or correct, and do not distinguish between those to determine which is most true. With continued cognitive growth, students can evaluate information to distinguish who is a valid authority and use that to construct arguments, termed *relativism*. Ultimately, Perry (1970) proposed that students would move into *commitment in relativism*, making decisions in the context of the world around them.

Like Perry (1970), Kohlberg (1981) and Gilligan’s (1982) developmental models informed Ethical Reasoning in Action. Both theorists outlined how students make ethical decisions at different stages. In the beginning stages of both models, *pre-conventional* (Kohlberg, 1981) and *orientation to individual survival* (Gilligan, 1982) students make decisions that center the self: minimizing harm to themselves, avoiding punishment, and fulfilling personal desires or needs (Evans et al., 2010; Pascarella & Terenzini, 2005). As students begin to see the role others play in ethical situations and consider how decisions impact others, they move to the next stages of the respective models, *member of society* and *goodness as self-sacrifice*.
