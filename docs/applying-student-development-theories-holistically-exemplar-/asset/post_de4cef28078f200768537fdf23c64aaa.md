According to Astin, the degree of student involvement can be conceptualized as a continuum, from uninvolved students who devote minimal time and energy to academic and co-curricular activities, to highly involved students who devote substantial amounts of time and energy. ## Cross and Fhagen-Smith's (2001) Model of Black Identity Development (Nigrescence)

This six-sector model details the process of determining what it means to be Black and developing one's Black identity. Sector 5, Adult Nigrescence, describes the trajectory of individuals' perceptions of race as unimportant to the construction of an entirely new worldview in the context of theirs and others' Black identity. The four stages are pre-encounter, encounter, immersion/emersion, and internalization/commitment.

## Strayhorn's (2012) Sense of Belonging

According to Strayhorn (2012), sense of belonging refers to "students' perceived social support on campus, a feeling or sensation of connectedness, the experience of mattering or feeling cared about, accepted, respected, valued by, and important to the group or others on campus" (p. 3). Strayhorn's conceptual model consists of seven core elements: sense of belonging as a basic human need, a fundamental motive, sufficient to drive human behavior, sense of belonging takes on heightened importance in certain contexts, its relation to and consequence of mattering, social identities intersect and affect sense of belonging, its association with positive outcomes, and the necessity of a perpetual sense of belonging and likelihood of change as circumstances, conditions, and contexts change.

## Phinney's (1989, 1993) Model of Ethnic Identity Development

This three-stage model captures the process of ethnic identity achievement. In the first stage, unexamined ethnic identity, students are described as being in one of two states: diffusion or foreclosure. Both states reflect unexplored personal feelings or attitudes, or interactions with individuals who share similar ethnic identity. The next stage, moratorium references an encounter that prompts ethnic identity exploration, while processing various emotions and reactions to information gathered. In the final stage, identity achievement, individuals develop a positive bicultural identity—building awareness and appreciation for other ethnicities.

## Perry's (1970) Scheme of Intellectual and Ethical Development

This theory describes how students move from simplistic, binary forms of. Gilligan proposed a theory that centered women's moral development and reasoning. Similar to Kohlberg's theory, the theory consists of three levels (orientation to individual survival, goodness as self-sacrifice, and the morality of nonviolence). Progression through levels is achieved via two transition stages (from selfishness to responsibility and from goodness to truth).

## Kegan's (1994) Theory of Evolution of Consciousness

Kegan's five orders of consciousness outline the "principles of mental organization that affect thinking, feeling, and relating to self and others" (Love & Guthrie, 1999, p. 67). Each order contains intrapersonal and interpersonal dimensions that supplement the cognitive nature of the orders. Orders 0 to 1 are specific to young children (ages 0–2). Orders 2 to 5 are more relevant to the adult population we typically serve on college campuses. They are Order 2: Instrument mind; Order 3: Socialized mind; Order 4: Self-authoring mind; and Order 5: Self-transforming mind.

## Baxter Magolda’s (2001) Theory of Self-Authorship

Self-authorship is defined as “the internal capacity to define one’s beliefs, identity, and social relations” (Evans et al., 2010). Baxter Magolda’s theory describes the epistemological development of young adults in connection with one’s sense of self and relationships with others. Three overarching questions are explored via the theory: How do I know? Who am I? How do I want to construct relationships with others? The journey to self-authorship is depicted by four phases that explain how individuals shift away from externally assigned values and belief systems to defining one’s own: following formulas, crossroads, becoming the author of one’s life, and internal foundation.

## Schlossberg’s (1984) Transition Theory

Life can sometimes throw curve balls that result in changed relationships, routines, and roles, (i.e., transition) and can be anticipated, unanticipated, or a nonevent. Schlossberg’s theory provides insight into how individuals interpret and cope with transitions. Effective coping is contingent upon one’s appraisal of resources in four areas (situation, self, support, and strategies) to facilitate the transition process of moving in, moving through, and moving out.

## Kolb’s (1984) Experiential Learning Theory

According to Kolb (1984), learning is defined as “the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience” (p. 38). The theory presents a four-stage cyclical learning process that “combines experience, perception, cognition, and behavior” (p. 21). The four stages are concrete experience, observation and reflection, forming abstract concepts, and testing in new situations.

For readability and the ability to compare across examples, the next nine chapters, which are written by contributing authors, are presented using a consistent template. Each chapter is organized around a framework that highlights contemporary perspectives of higher education and student affairs practitioners on incorporating theory, models, and additional scholarship into their work. The template (i.e., order of information and perspectives provided in Chapters 2–10) is as follows:

- Abstract
- Brief introduction that sets the institutional and local context for the theory-in-practice application
- Description of [name/identifying phrase for program or initiative]
  - Guiding theories
  - Implementation specifics
  - Assessment and evaluation
- Lessons from applying theory artfully
- Recommendations and resources
- References

The final chapter of the book, Chapter 11, does not follow this template. Rather, it summarizes data from the proposals submitted for this book and draws out thematic perspectives from lessons learned and recommendations made across contributing authors. Conclusions about applying student development theories in contemporary contexts that highlight the need for engagement by scholarly higher education and student affairs practitioners are offered. In addition, some cautions are noted about transferring theory use to multiple contexts and student populations.

## IT IS NOT MAGIC

Applying theories holistically to encourage college student development and learning, as Susan R. Komives points out in the Foreword, is not magic. Instead, it involves a confluence of often subtle, yet ever-changing scholarly knowledge and intentional practices—as if looking at vibrant images through a kaleidoscope. In the subsequent nine chapters, scholarly practitioners share their perspectives on the complexity of theory-in-practice work. The kaleidoscopic reflections and angles described demonstrate their expertise as engaged, scholarly higher education and student affairs practitioners.
