Transition theory research was continued and extended by introducing coping factors known as the *4 S’s*: situation, self, support, and strategies (Schlossberg et al., 1995). Passport enhanced its pre-transfer advising to address Schlossberg, Waters, and Goodman’s situation coping variable within the *4 S’s* framework. The situational factors that produce a positive outcome of transition adaptation include role change, affect, source, timing, onset, and duration (Schlossberg, 1981). Passport utilizes transition theory in supporting ITISA students via pre-transfer advising as well as developing transfer resources and services to further assist these students.

Chickering and Reisser’s (1993) initial two vectors of *developing competence* and *managing emotions* also have been core influences with which Passport designed and evolved its programming and resources. The *developing competence* vector can be divided into three prongs: intellectual, physical, and interpersonal competence. Intellectual competence involves expanding one’s skills to understand and interpret information as well as acquiring a thorough understanding of the content presented (Chickering & Reisser, 1993). Interpersonal competence involves taking in information and effectively communicating with others (Chickering & Reisser, 1993). The *managing emotions* vector involves students recognizing the triggers that may influence their emotions and effectively coping with such situations in suitable ways (Chickering & Reisser, 1993).

To supplement the works of Schlossberg (1981, 1984; Schlossberg et al., 1995) and Chickering and Reisser (1993), Passport applies practical connections from recent research. Flaga’s (2006) study on the process of community college transfer guides Passport’s services and programming through the following dimensions of transfer transition: *Learning Resources*, *Connecting*, and *Familiarity*. *Learning Resources* focuses on the ways students collect information through the three types of learning (i.e., formal, informal, and initiative) to help them understand campus resources and culture (Flaga, 2006). Information and resources shared with students during a pre-transfer advising appointment is an example of formal learning: information learned from campus staff and through campus programming (Flaga, 2006). *Connecting* focuses on the ways students create relationships with those who play a role within the student’s academic, social, and physical contexts (Flaga, 2006). *Familiarity* focuses on how at ease students feel after a campus tour, connecting with an IUPUI advisor in their major, attending an IUPUI Transfer Workshop on an ITISA campus, and immersing in IUPUI student culture by attending events that match involvement interests. In connection with Flaga’s (2006) dimension of *connecting* during the transfer process, Passport strives to help students feel interconnected with the IUPUI community by suggesting a campus visit, meeting faculty and staff, and taking part in IUPUI social programming.

Passport advisors also maintain an office space within the ITISA academic advising center physically closest to the IUPUI campus where advising sessions are held four days a week. Wyner et al. (2016) suggest “establishing a visible presence on partners’ campuses is another investment with high return” (p. 9). By having a presence at ITISA, Passport is able to advise students at their home campus for transfer, offer in-house support for advisors to address inquiries on IUPUI curriculum or admission process, and attend weekly ITISA advising staff meetings for ongoing updates.

Additionally, Passport conducts yearly meetings with IUPUI and ITISA academic departments to learn about curricular changes and strategies to best advise students. Flaga (2006) suggests summer academic unit visits at both institutions is key to maintaining relationships among advisors. Passport then compiles the information into a newsletter that is shared with ITISA and IUPUI faculty and staff to maintain communication between institutions. Highlights of the newsletter include ITISA and IUPUI academic departmental updates and course transfer policies by major.

Passport discovered off-site advising is essential in its pre-transfer advising efforts to serve the mass ITISA student population interested in IUPUI. In addition to the North Meridian campus, which is Ivy Tech’s largest campus and located just three miles from IUPUI, Passport also advises at four other ITISA locations. Passport also expanded its offering of in-house advising days for ITISA learning communities. Learning communities such as TRiO, Nina/Bowen Scholars, 21st Century Scholars, Phi Theta Kappa Honor Society, and Male Empowerment Initiative in the ITISA allow Passport advisors to hold appointments within their program suites each semester. These collaborations actively promote a culture of transfer within their programs and encourage co-advising.

To support the “characteristics of the pre- and post-transition environment” as noted by Schlossberg (1981), in-house advising provides IUPUI via Passport (*post-transitional environment*) and ITISA learning communities (*pre-transitional environment*) the opportunity to provide students with positive coordinated institutional support for transfer. Wyner et al. (2016, pp. 29–40) also confirmed three of the strategies in The Transfer Playbook that tailored transfer student advising to discuss degree exploration, such as hosting in-house interactions, are essential to student success.

## Transitional Programming, Advisor Training, and Resources

Transitional programming, advisor training, and Passport resources are developed by Passport’s Assistant Director as a response to the needs of ITISA and IUPUI students, faculty, staff, and administrators. The Assistant Director oversees the compilation and dissemination of two resources: the newsletter (as mentioned previously) and the Passport Transfer Guide. Key topics of the Passport Transfer Guide include transfer options available to students, IUPUI admission requirements, application deadlines, and scholarships.

Through student anecdotes and daily advising interactions with students, some students expressed frustration regarding the complexity of the transfer process. These anecdotes revealed students may experience mixed emotions as they navigate the transfer process, which may demonstrate some students are still developing in the managing emotions vector of Chickering and Reisser’s (1993) seven vectors. Passport created the Passport Transfer Guide as a succinct resource to address the major components of the transfer process and in turn attempt to make the transition for students as smooth and simple as possible, thus limiting the frustration.

Schlossberg et al.’s (1995) identified coping variables are also addressed in Passport’s resources. The Passport Transfer Guide provides a gradual onset of the transitional journey by “setting the stage” for transfer. The overall voice of the guide encourages students to focus on the positive affect of transitioning to IUPUI, as well as re-establishes students as the internal source to direct their transition.

Throughout the fall and spring semesters, Passport visits ITISA’s Student Success in University Transfer courses (i.e., IVYT 111) and capstone courses to share IUPUI transfer information with students. As these courses are offered in various formats across ITISA, Passport provides instructors the option of an in-person presentation or a voice-over infographic Microsoft PowerPoint presentation. These presentations may serve as a student’s first point of contact with Passport. As a result, a connection is established in which students seek Passport advising and attend supplemental events designed to aid in their transition.
