## A Pre-Orientation Retreat to Increase Retention and Develop Competence

Kelly Carter Merrill

Launch describes a pre-orientation retreat at Randolph-Macon College, a small private liberal arts institution in Virginia. Launch was designed with a psychosocial developmental focus and the intention of improving student retention at the college. This overarching goal was actualized by addressing four intended developmental goals: ease new students’ social transition, prepare them for academic challenges, encourage reflection on perseverance, and create a social community of peers who would value and protect their diversity. Four theoretical constructs guided the design and curriculum of Launch: developing competence (Chickering & Reisser, 1993), student involvement theory (Astin, 1999), mindset and perseverance (Dweck, 2012), and microaggressions and climates for diversity (Sue, 2010).

Randolph-Macon College (RMC) is a small (1,400 students), private, selective, liberal arts college in central Virginia with a largely regional draw (73% in state). It is a residential college, with 92% of first-year students, and 80% of all students, living on campus. Originally, RMC was an all men’s institution founded in 1830 with a Methodist mission. Today, the institution boasts 53% enrolled women (having gone coeducational in 1971) and is the oldest Methodist-related college in continuous operation (Office of Institutional Research, 2017; SCHEV, 2017). The college’s Carnegie classification is Baccalaureate College—Arts and Sciences, and the athletic conference is the Old Dominion Athletic Conference (ODAC) within the National Collegiate Athletic Association’s Division III.

One third of Randolph-Macon students are athletes, and nearly a third (29%) are in Greek Letter organizations. Seventy-eight percent of the students identify as White, 8% as Black, 4% as Hispanic/Latino, 4% as multiracial, 2% as Asian American, and 4% as other or unknown. More than half of the students (55%) receive need-based financial aid. The six-year graduation rate is 58% (Office of Institutional Research, 2017; SCHEV, 2017).

In 2010, the RMC historical average freshman-to-sophomore retention rate was approximately 75%, and the two years prior to the 2015 *Launch* retreat, it had risen to 77% (2013 cohort) and 78% (2014 cohort) (SCHEV, 2017). In 2015, RMC faculty, staff, and administrators turned their attention to addressing new students’ developmental considerations, and *Launch* was conceived as an effort to continue to improve retention.

### DESCRIPTION OF LAUNCH

During the summers of 2015 and 2016, incoming students were invited to attend a four-day, pre-move-in retreat called *Launch*. Sixty-eight students participated (28 in 2015, 6% of the incoming class; 42 in 2016, 10% of the class). The initial invitation targeted students of color, community college transfer students, and first-generation students—students who typically have lower retention rates in colleges and universities (National Student Clearinghouse Research, 2017; National Student Clearinghouse Research, 2012; Engle & Tinto, 2008).

The Director of Student Transitions in the Higgins Academic Center administered *Launch*. The Academic Center assists students through their academic and social transition by offering free tutoring, mentoring, and disability support services. The Academic Center has a robust upper-level student staff with 37 tutors and 36 mentors, several of whom hold both positions. Both the tutoring and mentoring programs are certified by the College Reading and Learning Association; the staff members meet rigorous national selection and training requirements. *Launch* student mentors were selected from the tutor and mentor staff, and received additional training specific to *Launch*’s developmental goals.

Physical and Manual Competence: While the mentoring group experience provided opportunities for participants to develop interpersonal competence, it was the *content* of the challenges that focused on students’ physical and manual competency. Mentoring group challenges included: an introductory bulletin board decorating contest that was judged based on creativity and success at introducing the group members (the bunk cabins had eight bulletin boards); daily adventure challenges, including high ropes, low ropes, zip-line, and lake trampoline; planning, shopping, and preparing two meals for the entire *Launch* group and cleaning up after two different meals; and a concluding skit competition that was judged on the group’s creativity. Each of these challenges offered opportunity for a variety of students’ physical and manual strengths to emerge.

Intellectual Competence: Understandably, many *Launch* participants wondered if they had the intellectual competence to succeed in their first year of college. Three strategies were embedded to address this concern. It was important to help students set realistic GPA expectations for the fall semester. During one of the morning meetings, each student was given a piece of college letterhead with an envelope. The staff members facilitated a discussion about the difference in academic life from high school to college, and the potential impact of that change on GPA. Next, the participants were instructed to address the envelope to their home address and write a letter with their GPA goal for fall term and two messages to themselves: *What do you want to tell yourself if you make your goal? What do you want to tell yourself if you do not meet your goal?* The letters were mailed in December after fall grades were submitted.

Second, by utilizing student mentors and student tutors from the Academic Center as *Launch* mentors, *Launch* students were intentionally connected with students who were highly trained to assist with academic transitions. The intention was that *Launch* students would already be socially connected to the staff at the Academic Center, thus easing any anxiety they might have about reaching out to the Academic Center later in the year when they knew they needed help.

Third, *Launch* students were taught the technique of free writing to encourage their writing fluency. Students at RMC receive a lot of feedback on their writing.
