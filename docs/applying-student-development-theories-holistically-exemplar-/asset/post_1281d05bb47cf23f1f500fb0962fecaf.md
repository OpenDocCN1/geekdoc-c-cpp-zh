## APPLYING STUDENT DEVELOPMENT THEORIES HOLISTICALLY

This book dives into student development theory, unpacking key foundational and emergent theories of college student development while providing contemporary examples and application. Helping aspiring higher education and student affairs practitioners grasp and use theories holistically, this important text brings to life theoretical knowledge to enhance the development and learning of college and university students. Showcasing a diversity of programs, practices, and services across a variety of institutional types, *Applying Student Development Theories Holistically* demonstrates how professionals are intertwining the science of theory with the art of practice in multidimensional, holistic ways.

**Katie Branch** is Associate Professor in the Department of Human Development and Family Studies at the University of Rhode Island, USA.

**Jeanne S. Hart-Steffes** is Vice President of Student Affairs and Dean of Students at Western New England University, USA.

**Christine M. Wilson** is Assistant Vice President for Student Affairs and Director of Student Activities at the University of Connecticut, USA.

## Foreword: It Is Not Magic

My son is a rocket scientist—really! He grew up loving airplanes, drawing airplanes, visiting the Smithsonian Air and Space Museum, doing his high school senior project in the wind tunnel at the University of Maryland, joining the U.S. Air Force Reserve Officers Training Corps, and most recently earning his doctorate in aeronautical engineering at the University of Minnesota. ### College Outcomes

Many years ago my dear friend Alexander 'Sandy' Astin addressed what he called "the black box" between student inputs (i.e., who they are and what they bring) and the college outcomes they achieve. Astin's college impact model, known as inputs-environment-outputs, or I-E-O (1993; see Figure 0.2), addresses the impact of environmental experiences, including pedagogical practices that are intentionally designed to interact with salient dimensions of student inputs leading to desired college outcomes.

![](img/c03cf8065037f42512bddd1febea4918_8_1.png)

Figure 0.2 Komives' Boarding-Magic-Flying Link With Astin's Inputs-Environment-Outcomes (I-E-O) Model

As presented in many chapters in this book, Astin (1993) made college educators aware that student involvement, that is, the amount of time and energy they spent in developmentally powerful experiences, was critical to achieving those desired outcomes. Refinements to the I-E-O model, of course, also illustrate the direct effect of inputs on outcomes, but the focus of this book (and the metaphor of how the plane flies) is best understood in a linear relationship.

Readers of this book well know the emphasis universities have adopted for establishing and assessing learning and developmental outcomes in the past 30 years. College educators are held accountable by students, families, employers, and the public for delivering these outcomes. Over the past several decades in intentional student affairs practice, educators have successfully addressed numerous complex issues of student success such as persistence, achievement, and developing cognitive complexity. Compilations such as the third edition of *How College Affects Students* (Mayhew et al., 2016) synthesize the many interventions and engagements that matter in student learning and development and the impact of individual and group differences on that process.

In this book, Katie Branch, Jeanne S. Hart-Steffes, and Christine M. Wilson and their talented contributors illustrate how educators can shape the college environment through intentional application of theory and research to the experiences the environment offers to diverse students. Grounded in knowledge of students and their personal development, educators in this book have used various theories of student success and development and vast research on environmental factors, including high-impact practices (Kuh, 2008), to address desired outcomes.

### High-Impact Educational Practices

George Kuh and his colleagues’ body of research through the National Survey of Student Engagement study identified 10 high-impact practices (HIPs) that contribute to student learning and development (Kuh, Kinzie, Schuh, Whitt, & Associates, 2005). Advanced by the Association of American Colleges and Universities (see www.aacu.org/resources/high-impact-practices), these developmentally powerful experiences include:

- First-year experiences
- Common intellectual experiences
- Learning communities
- Writing-intensive courses
- Collaborative assignments and projects
- Undergraduate research
- Diversity/global learning
- Service learning, community-based learning
- Internships
- Capstone courses and projects

Of importance to this book are the six behavioral themes that Kuh (2008) extrapolated that were undergirding those HIPs that made the impact they evidenced. These themes are:

- Investing time and effort
- Interacting with faculty and peers about substantive matters
- Experiencing diversity
- Responding to more frequent feedback
- Reflecting and integrating learning
- Discovering relevance of learning through real-world applications

One example of the progress educators have made is evident in our own work with student leadership development. Colleges have incorporated student leadership theory (e.g., the social change model of leadership development [SCM; Higher Education Research Institute, 1996]) along with related theoretical input frames (e.g., racial identity, leadership self-efficacy) with their data from the Multi-institutional Study of Leadership (MSL; see www.leadershipstudy.net) that examines the SCM and numerous campus engagement experiences to identify experiences that promote growth in leadership capacity. Numerous college environment practices such as engaging students in discussions with others on sociopolitical issues, mentoring, holding positional leader roles, and community service are high-impact practices for developing leadership capacity (Dugan, Kodama, Correia, & Associates, 2013). Combined with understandings of leadership identity development (LID: see Komives, Owen, Longerbeam, Mainella, & Osteen, 2005), campuses are now scaffolding their leadership programs to build developmental complexity using leadership HIPs identified from studies such as the MSL research.
