Specifically, the first outcome, upholding the five core values, is addressed through each WIGS meeting and each group activity. Evidence of members upholding the five core values is demonstrated by their continued requests to discuss the values at meetings and relating their experiences to the values without prompting from the advisors. It is also demonstrated by how they define and connect to the values personally.

The increases in both the number of members and invitations to present about FGS to various groups at WIU speak to the group’s consistent effort to raise awareness of FGS—our second outcome. Individual members also share experiences with new members about their pride as FGS. They also share thoughts about the behaviors they engage in that are “typical” FGS behaviors, but not helpful in accomplishing their goals while at college. For example, various members can clearly articulate how they navigate between the culture of college and their families, as well as the challenges that come with such navigation.

The third outcome, exploring the campus and surrounding community, is documented by compiling a list of all activities attended by the WIGS membership. Additionally, individual members can express the experiences they would like to have and from there coordinate steps to create or attend those experiences together. Furthermore, members share information about community resources, such as campus tutoring, with the other group members to raise awareness and encourage utilization of those services.

Encouraging and supporting the holistic development of the WIGS membership, the fourth outcome, is demonstrated and documented in GroupMe® conversations and membership participation in weekly check-in activities. An example of a member making strides in this outcome occurred when the current President shared she no longer needs consistent follow-up from an advisor regarding her to-do list. She can now manage her time at school in order to enjoy her time with her family as well as accomplish her academic goals.

Working through personal biases involves a great deal of self-reflection and self-awareness, which is not easy work. However, it is work that is essential in allowing us to create a safe space for students and to connect with them in a meaningful way.

As challenging as the process of self-reflection is for us as advisors, experience has taught us that it is a more difficult task for students because self-reflection could surface and affirm differences in their minds between themselves and their peers. However, Kolb’s (1984) theory of experiential learning tells us that reflection is an integral component of the learning process. The act of reflecting on an experience is as important as having the experience, because it is through reflection that meaning is made. We find it important to create intentional opportunities—such as check-ins—for students to reflect on their experiences.

In our role, we emphasize the need to be prepared to push back against any messages of shame that might surface, which often accompanies discussions involving social class. Reframing discussions to be inclusive of each student’s reality, without judgment, is critical. This might mean, for example, responding with "it does stink to not have money, and does seem as if everything costs too much," versus engaging in the awkward silence that often accompanies conversations about money. This might also mean being sensitive to and discussing a current event, such as that of the Kalief Browder story. Browder was a Black male who was accused of and imprisoned for a crime, despite a lack of evidence; after he was released, he died by suicide; advocates for prison reform point to his story as evidence for needed change. After discussing this tragedy, we opened the conversation to discuss racial bias and mental health challenges of male students and students of color.

The blend between the LPM and experiential learning cycle for an organization dedicated to first-generation students demands that advisors focus on helping to make the organization "real," rather than "good looking." At times, we may need to stop what we are doing to engage in a conversation about the importance of mindfulness activities to manage stress, or to arrange study sessions during midterm weeks. However, we must also balance the members’ present mindset simultaneously. It is challenging to continually remind them to focus on the future, while focusing on present tasks. One downside to this learning-centered approach is efficiency, as transformative learning experiences require ample time. We periodically need to readjust our expectations about what we can accomplish.

Lastly, inviting outside speakers to the organization requires meaningful preparation with the speakers so that they can engage with students from a “knowledge” perspective, rather than a deficit (i.e., “no knowledge”) perspective. Advisors are often the first to ask questions of the speakers to demonstrate to students the types of questions that can be asked, as well as how to go about asking them.

## RECOMMENDATIONS AND RESOURCES

### Create Support Structures for FGS

FGS report feeling less confident and more isolated, and are more likely to work and live off campus than their non-FGS peers (Davis, 2010). Support for FGS spaces on campus validates the FGS experience, reaffirms their innate capacity to learn, and helps them feel their life experiences are valuable. When creating these spaces, remember that the FGS label exists in conjunction with other identities: race, gender, sexual orientation, and dis/ability to name a few. If FGS hold other marginalized identities in addition to their FGS status, they might be susceptible to a compounded effect by systems of oppression and domination. As such, understanding how FGS’s multiple identities (Orbe, 2008) and identity salience (Orbe, 2004) impact the ability to successfully navigate college campuses may prove helpful when working with FGS.

### Create an Environment Allowing FGS to Access Resources More Readily

FGS often do not seek help, and are aware of the differences in their experiences compared with other students (Davis, 2010). Parker (2014) found that talking about these differences, socioeconomic differences in particular, helped increase GPA and use of college resources. Talking about the FGS experience empowers FGS to take an active role in their college education. While there are obstacles unique to the FGS experience, FGS possess a plethora of strengths. As demonstrated earlier, students became sounding boards for their peers with regard to problem-solving, critical thinking, and personal support.

### Shape the Environment to Specifically Meet the Needs of FGS at Your Institution

Feedback and involvement from FGS are essential to shaping the organizational, and hopefully, institutional, environments to meet their needs. It is difficult to predict the needs and demographics of FGS at any particular institution. The WIGS student organization exists at a midsize, four-year, regional state institution—thus, more research regarding the experience of FGS at other institutions is needed. Creating FGS student support systems at various institutions can help develop more well-rounded research about the FGS student experience and, subsequently, how to better support FGS.

### Consider How Best to Support FGS at Your Institution

Practitioners seeking to create student organizations similar to WIGS may want to consult with the office of student involvement about how to start a student organization and to learn the requirements for getting an organization recognized.

There are many ways to support FGS in addition to creating student organizations. Initiatives such as *I’m First* (Center for Student Opportunity, 2017), created by and for FGS, provides on-line multimedia resources for institutions and FGS. This resource allows others to listen to student stories and learn about initiatives throughout the country to assist in creating FGS spaces.
