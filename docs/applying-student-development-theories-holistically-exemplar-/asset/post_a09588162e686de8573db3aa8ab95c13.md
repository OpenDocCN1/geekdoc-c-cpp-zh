## LESSONS FROM APPLYING THEORY ARTFULLY

The incorporations of the LPM and the concept of intersectionality have truly transformed the work we do in the Pride Center. Since implementation in 2015, the Pride Center has seen markedly increased engagement in programming. This increased engagement can be attributed in large part to the deep learning and community building (and, therefore, increased investment and productivity) that has occurred within the student staff team. Admittedly, the development and implementation of this model have not been easy. Often at our institution, student staff members are given task-oriented roles to fill a need within a department; work-study or wage positions are rarely seen as developmental opportunities. Because of this, the office has faced the challenge of creating a unique, impactful experience in an institutional context where little precedent exists.

Though our model intentionally creates and advances developmental outcomes, we also aspire to maintain a balance between these outcomes and the core functions of our space. This balance includes planning large- and small-scale programming for our campus community. As a result, we have been challenged to be intentional in the ways in which we structure our limited time with student staff members. For example, during the weeks leading up to the larger-scale, signature events, many perfunctory tasks need to be discussed and executed during the student staff meetings. However, we ensure time and space for self-exploration and self-care. During periods of the semester where programming is less demanding, we dedicate extended time to professional development, discussions about personal identities, and community building. This investment throughout the student staff experience ultimately makes the implementation of programming more impactful, as the ideas that are generated by the student staff are grounded in intersectional thinking, and the execution of their ideas is made more meaningful by the synergistic community of our team. Most important, the environment we have created allows for intentional reflection after implementation of each program, both individually as well as collectively. Depth of learning occurs as students take a critical perspective on their own work and the work of their peers, making meaning of what. A broad spectrum of identities and experiences sets the stage for an environment where challenge and support can occur. Simply creating a diverse team, however, is not sufficient; it is critical to also build an environment where all students know that they are supported and where exclusive behaviors, whether intentional or unintentional, are confronted and addressed. To do this, we actively recruit the most marginalized populations while being mindful of how our own identities impact our work.

### Clearly State the Learning Outcomes of the Model to the Students

At the Pride Center, treating students as collaborative partners means modeling transparency whenever possible, and this includes with the goals. This has also been incredibly important given the institutional context where student staff positions rarely incorporate intentional learning; clearly communicating intentions to the staff has shown them the investment that the professional staff have in them as individuals and as a collective beyond their role of developing and implementing programming. Of course, in order to state the learning outcomes, we had to spend time crafting them and building them into the larger curricular plan.

### Establish Norms of Collaborative Learning

Collaborative, multi-directional learning is a core tenet of the LPM (Baxter Magolda, 2004). To position ourselves as both teachers and learners within the student staff team, we regularly challenged student staff members to seek answers to their questions and to creatively problem solve rather than rely on hierarchical notions of an all-knowing supervisor.

### Craft Time for Developmental Experiences, Reflective Conversations, and Self-care

A critical lesson learned early on in developing the student staff model was that focusing on tasks alone is detrimental to building a developmental experience. Through time and intentionality, we have found creative ways to maximize our student staff’s time so that the team could focus on reflection, education, and self-care while still ensuring that tasks were accomplished. Whether opening each staff meeting with a self-care activity, reflecting on the learning moments from implementing a signature event, or taking a staff meeting to delve into a racial justice topic, the prioritizing of developmental experiences is interwoven throughout the model.

### Be Open to Feedback and Critique

The Pride Center student staff model is a work in progress, and we have found it essential to be open to input from our student staff members to maximize the effectiveness of their experience. Through the comprehensive review process, one-on-one conversations, and periodic evaluations, we have gained valuable information that has allowed us to tweak and adjust the model in a way that better meets our students’ needs; additionally, including student input ensures that they feel that they are co-creators of their experience.

### Explore Resources Related to Critical Praxis and the Learning Partnership Model

We were inspired by ETR tools used by the University of North Carolina, Asheville (UNC Asheville Student Affairs, 2018) and Grand Valley State University (GVSU, 2018), as well as the top five employability traits (i.e., communication, teamwork, critical thinking skills, intercultural competency, and administrative/organizational management) as researched by the National Association of Colleges and Employers (2016).

To state the learning outcomes, we had to spend time crafting them and building them into the larger curricular plan; the American College Personnel Association’s Residential Curriculum Institute was an instrumental resource for this work.

The approach to the management of the team was inspired by the tenets of intersectional feminist praxis in supervision, as described by Fullerton (2017) in this NASPA video: www.youtube.com/watch?reload=9&v=sMB3To1LJZY. Additionally, bell hooks’ (1994) *Teaching to Transgress* and Paolo Freire’s (1970/1996) *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1970/1996) have both been key influences on the ways we have approached gathering and responding to feedback as part of supervision.

Finally, Brené Brown’s (2010) *The Gifts of Imperfection* has been an inspirational framework for us to develop ourselves as authentic practitioners in all interactions with the students.

Though we still have much to learn, we are confident in the possibilities of the applicability of this model to other cross-cultural center contexts and beyond, and hope to continue to hone our practice to better meet the developmental needs of our students. Ultimately, we look forward to continuing to learn in partnership with our students as we all journey toward more critically minded, self-authored versions of ourselves.
