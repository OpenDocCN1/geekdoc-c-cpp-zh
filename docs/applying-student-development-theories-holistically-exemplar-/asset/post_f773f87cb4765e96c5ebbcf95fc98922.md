Perry’s (1970) *Scheme of Intellectual and Ethical Development* provided a structure to understand what we should expect from students with varying intellectual abilities and how students might interpret and make meaning of different ethical challenges (in Evans, Forney, Guido, Patton, & Renn, 2010). Kohlberg’s (1981) *Stages of Moral Development* added to our understanding of how moral development occurs and what thoughts/actions/choices would be indicative of movement among developmental stages, especially when the reasoner sees through the eyes of justice and autonomy (in Evans et al., 2010; in Gilligan, 1982). Gilligan’s (1982) relationship-based “ethic of care” work expanded our vision to include the voices of care (empathy) and responsibilities when reasoning. Important to the premise of asking 8KQ when reasoning, Gilligan recognized that the one voice may be predominant in one’s thinking, “both ‘justice’ and ‘care’ voices are inherent in the life cycle, constituting alternative grounds on which to evaluate the moral” (Pascarella & Terenzini, 2005, p. 44).

In Perry’s (1970) cognitive development framework, many incoming traditional-aged college students are *dualistic* thinkers conceiving of ethical and other decisions in simple right or wrong terms, or considering parents/authority figures as the individual with answers (Pascarella & Terenzini, 2005). As students learn new methods of inquiry in college, they move into the *multiplicity* stage, in which peers become valid sources of information, they see multiple ideas and opinions as right or correct, and do not distinguish between those to determine which is most true. With continued cognitive growth, students can evaluate information to distinguish who is a valid authority and use that to construct arguments, termed *relativism*. Ultimately, Perry (1970) proposed that students would move into *commitment in relativism*, making decisions in the context of the world around them.

Like Perry (1970), Kohlberg (1981) and Gilligan’s (1982) developmental models informed Ethical Reasoning in Action. Both theorists outlined how students make ethical decisions at different stages. In the beginning stages of both models, *pre-conventional* (Kohlberg, 1981) and *orientation to individual survival* (Gilligan, 1982), students make decisions that center the self: minimizing harm to themselves, avoiding punishment, and fulfilling personal desires or needs (Evans et al., 2010; Pascarella & Terenzini, 2005). As students begin to see the role others play in ethical situations and consider how decisions impact others, they move to the next stages of the respective models, *member of society* and *goodness as self-sacrifice*. Others become extremely important

As students grow in their intellectual and moral abilities, they may engage with the 8KQ more broadly, and would hypothetically move progressively from a self-centered worldview to a more inclusive worldview, reflecting the shift from SLO One and Two to SLO Three. For example, a shallow, self-focused application of the fairness question, “*How can I act equitably and balance all interests,*” may consider the equal distribution of a good, preferably in the decision-maker’s favor. Whereas, a goodness as self-sacrifice (Gilligan) or conventional (Kohlberg) application of the fairness question would bring about questions of the distribution of justice or fair practices for everyone involved. As indicated by Gilligan (1982), this stage may be represented by self-sacrifice, swinging to the other end of the self-focused pendulum, resulting in the temporary abandonment of caring for oneself.

SLO Three most closely reflects advancement in the mid-development stages of multiplicity, member of society, and goodness as self-sacrifice/from goodness to truth. In these stages, thinkers are questioning legitimacy of authority and peers may become relevant in decision-making. With the ability to see themselves as decision-makers, students can deeply understand and apply the key questions to scenarios. They can challenge their own thinking when answers to those questions conflict, providing cognitive dissonance.

Similarly, the transition to Kohlberg’s (1981) post-conventional stage should reflect a further nuanced understanding of fairness, for example, perhaps demonstrated by a realistic balancing of multiple inequities while maintaining an empathic approach or aiming to create justice among multiple groups of people that may or may not include the self. In advanced 8KQ ethical reasoning, the thinker (decision-maker) would raise additional questions of rights, outcomes, character, liberty, authority, and responsibilities applied to all parties involved in the situation. The Ethical Reasoning in Action program adds additional “voices” to the important moral conversations and decisions in a sea of ill-structured problems that require complex, nuanced, and informed decisions.
