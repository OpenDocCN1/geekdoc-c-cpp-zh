The Ethical Reasoning in Action program is unique in its campus-wide application at JMU. Educational interventions occur in the co-curriculum (e.g., student employee training, student leadership programs, career selection and preparation discussions, student-led workshops, student-conducted educational workshops, resident advisor training) and in the curriculum (e.g., general education courses, major and pre-professional courses, graduate and doctoral courses). Numerous faculty, staff, and students design and deliver interventions that target one or more learning outcomes.

Bridging the gap between moral development theories and practice can be challenging. This is partly why the team that developed Ethical Reasoning in Action conceived of ethical reasoning as a *skill* that could be developed and practiced. Traditional lecturing on ethics codes or philosophical theory encourages complex thinking on an issue but does not necessarily develop skills to make ethical decisions. Through experiential learning activities, students connect with the content and process of ethical reasoning. Case scenarios serve as the primary experiential vehicle. Many case discussions utilize a circle process with a talking piece to encourage an open conversation for students to express their thoughts and challenge each other in a respectful way while using the 8KQ as a structure. Through these peer interactions, students may be challenged to look beyond the authorities that have dominated their worldview to see others as valid sources of information.

Beyond the superficial connection of Gilligan (1982) to the empathy question or Kohlberg (1981) to the fairness question, the theories come alive when considering that students’ cognitive and moral development is spurred by ethical reasoning educational interventions. By asking the 8KQ, students’ self-focused thought processes are intentionally interrupted and challenged to produce “ah-ha” moments. They can be challenged to move forward in their moral development by bringing thoughts about others into their decision-making, and by being supported through fictional and real-life problems and ethical dilemmas. Following are five program-specific educational interventions.

### Peer-to-Peer Workshops
A group of Ethical Reasoning in Action volunteer peer educators delivers interactive ethical reasoning workshops for other students, in moral development. A long-term partnership with the Orientation office makes *It's Complicated* logistically possible and a repeated success.

### Student Employee Hiring and Training in University Program Board
The hiring and training processes of the campus University Program Board include a focus on ethical reasoning. During an executive council group interview process, students are asked to use the 8KQ in a circle process to decide what to do in a scenario based on an incident the board faced regarding selection of a controversial movie to show on campus. Interviewers (staff and students currently in the role) observe which of the 8KQ interviewees reference in their answers, how well they interrogate their instincts, and how they work together to make decisions.

Once students are hired, they engage in longer training using the 8KQ to analyze and make decisions about other ethical scenarios they may face in their roles, including organization membership issues and inappropriate performers. Throughout the year, the students use the 8KQ to debate a course of action when actual ethical scenarios arise.

### Student Conduct Educational Workshops
As part of educational programming for minor student conduct offenses, the Office of Student Accountability and Restorative Practices staff members utilize the 8KQ to help students examine their values and behaviors. In the *Values in Action* program, they discuss scenarios where two or more values were in conflict and use the 8KQ to make sense of that situation. Students are also asked to reflect on a personal ethical decision and how it might be different had they used the 8KQ. Both activities support the overarching goal of helping students consider their own values before acting. In the *Calling the Shots* program, after students participate in discussions to establish the benefits and drawbacks of alcohol use, they ask the 8KQ to help them identify and consider the broader complexities in the decision whether to drink alcohol.

The office also conducts both small- and large-scale restorative circles surrounding various issues of harm to the community. In reflection papers and group circle processes, students are asked to use the questions to consider the harms inflicted on others and what ways those may be effectively repaired.

Ideally, in all programmatic applications of 8KQ, students will retain the concepts of the 8KQ, making them more thoughtful community members, and be able to apply them to future situations.

## Assessment and Evaluation
As a campus-wide program, student learning assessment occurs at the institutional level using a longitudinal design: Time 1 assessment occurs the last day of first-year orientation and Time 2 assessments are administered in February of the sophomore year. Three assessment instruments were developed in collaboration with JMU’s Center for Assessment and Research Studies; these measure the five Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs). Assessment for specific educational activities is designed by individual faculty and student affairs professionals to measure the effectiveness of the specific educational interventions.

Program assessment is centered on students’ ability to delineate among and apply the 8KQ concepts broadly, rather than an elusive evaluation of student “ethical behavior.” The Defining Issues Test-2 (Rest, Narvaez, Thomas, & Bebeau, 1999), the dominant tool for measuring moral development, is not used because it focuses too narrowly on justice-oriented moral judgment to be a fit for Ethical Reasoning in Action assessment needs. Essay assessment scoring rewards the recognition of an ethical situation, the broad (and accurate) application of Key Questions, an explanation of each KQ’s relevance or irrelevance to the decision, and the demonstration of weighing and balancing ethical considerations. The JMU Ethical Reasoning Rubric© (2014) provides parameters for assessment specific to 8KQ proficiency.

Overall, ethical reasoning skills have improved since the program began, as indicated by a cross-sectional analysis comparing Fall 2012 baseline data collected prior to any ethical reasoning interventions to data collected in subsequent years where students have at a minimum experienced the It’s Complicated introductory program (Bashkov, Smith, Fulcher, & Hawk, 2014; JMU, 2016; Pyburn, Smith, & Ames, 2016; Smith, Fulcher, & Pyburn, 2015). However, longitudinal scores on the recall instrument, the Ethical Reasoning Recall Test (measuring SLO One) decline slightly or show no significant change between Time 1 and Time 2 as indicated in yearly assessment reports (Bashkov et al., 2014; Pyburn et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2015). We consider this to be, at content, involvement opportunities, and personal life. Anyone (at any university or organization) with a basic knowledge of the 8KQ can infuse them into their work with students or employees. This does not require expertise in ethics or psychology, or in cognitive or moral reasoning development theory to be able to help students grow. However, because of the flexibility and broad distribution of this program, implementation is not always consistent, which can affect learning and assessment.

Ethical Reasoning in Action evolved from the university's reaccreditation process, which requires the conception, funding, and implementation of a new idea focused on enhancing student learning (JMU, 2012). Because of this, we are at a significant advantage for an institution-wide implementation. However, one of the best things about this program is its scalability. Smaller-scope and audience-specific programs are created by staff motivated to develop students' ethical reasoning abilities. The large scale of a program may be difficult to implement across a campus without institutional support, but it is entirely possible for an office or professional staff member to develop a program around this content. Aside from time and staffing, program costs are minor: copies of materials and writing utensils.

One unintended outcome of the 8KQ framework relates back to application of the 8KQ in a confirmatory (Haidt, 2001) rather than exploratory way, which can block growth. The questions should be used prior to making decisions rather than to describe what one has already done. This leads to a second caution: The 8KQ should not be used to judge or analyze someone else's actions, but rather as a prompt for personal agency and responsibility for decision-making. While ethical reasoning can be used to understand another's actions, it is a tool for forward thinking and acting.

One significant lesson learned from implementation of this program is the limitation of students' cognitive development over time. As practitioners, we look at theories as a map for students' growth and know it is not always that straightforward, but implementation of a large-scale program like this emphasizes and makes real that limitation. We must ask ourselves: What can we actually expect students to be able to think and do? Upon graduation, some students will reach advanced levels of ethical reasoning, but many will not. Our hope is that by developing the habit of asking questions, the "plus one" nature of the program will continue to help them challenge their own quick thinking and provide repeated growth opportunities.
