Intersectionality is rooted in the unique subordination of Black women and “any analysis that does not take intersectionality into account cannot sufficiently address the particular manner in which Black women are subordinated” (Crenshaw, 1989, p. 140). As a concept, intersectionality finds its roots in critical race theory and Black feminist thought, which predates the coining of the term. In *Pursuing Intersectionality, Unsettling Dominant Imaginaries*, May (2015) describes intersectionality as a both-and way of thinking that moves beyond single-axis thinking to more complex, matrix ways of thinking. We utilize intersectionality as a guiding framework to consider the implications of power, privilege, and oppression on the lived realities of our students who are pushed to the margins because of their various social identities. When discussing issues of privilege, social justice, and equity with students, it is imperative to use a critical lens; thus, our curriculum incorporates readings and activities that cultivate this nuanced approach.

We see an intersectionality framework as deeply connected to the concept of self-authorship, another foundational theoretical concept in the work with our student staff. Self-authorship is an example of constructive-developmental pedagogy that, according to Baxter Magolda (1999), “is more than letting students talk and generate their own ideas. It is a matter of creating the developmental conditions that allow them to generate their own ideas effectively, in essence to develop their minds, their voices, themselves” (pp. 7–8). The matrix way of thinking emphasized in May’s (2015) description of intersectionality closely aligns with Baxter Magolda’s (2008) process of self-authorship, particularly with student reflection and examination of their closely held values and beliefs. Deconstructing oppressive messages is also a key part of self-authorship (Baxter Magolda, 2008); when working with student staff members through one-on-one conversations, we actively challenge students to examine ways in which internalized oppression has impacted their paradigms and beliefs.

The implementation of the model’s curriculum starts with a staff retreat at the beginning of the school year. At the retreat, the professional staff express the expectation that the community regards learning as non-hierarchical, and challenge staff members to explore their own social identities and corresponding positionality. Following the retreat, all staff begin their weekly shifts. Staff members work one two-hour shift per week and are assigned specific tasks and collaborative projects. The student staff guide the center’s programmatic efforts, which are grounded in our core belief that we cannot work toward liberation for LGBTQIA+ communities unless liberation for all marginalized communities is centered. In addition to weekly shifts, the staff meet together on a weekly basis for one hour. The staff meetings follow a model that rotates between the following four elements:
- a guest speaker,
- a student-led professional development conversation,
- a self-care highlight,
- and a combined staff meeting with the Office of Multicultural Affairs during which staff teams engage in dialogue around a topic of inclusion and equity.

To further foster self-authored learners through reflection and self-examination, the Pride Center, along with the Office of Multicultural Affairs, developed the ETR, inspired by tools used by the University of North Carolina, Asheville (UNC Asheville Student Affairs, 2018) and Grand Valley State University (GVSU, 2018). The rubric outlines five specific employability traits that were selected based on the National Association of Colleges and Employers’ (2016) list of top attributes desired by employers to develop skills that our student staff could discuss during internship and job interviews:
- communication,
- teamwork,
- critical thinking skills,
- intercultural competency,
- and administrative/organizational management.

The five traits are deeply connected. We define communication as one’s ability to demonstrate active listening, clearly present ideas through written and oral formats, and consider specific strategies for communication to meet the needs and expectations of the intended audience. We define teamwork as one’s ability to interact effectively with peers and foster relationships with individuals and other units/offices across campus. To foster such relationships, communication skills must be effectively utilized. Critical thinking skills are developed throughout the student staff experience, and include making informed decisions, seeing problems from different perspectives, creating alternate interpretations of ideas, and identifying multiple approaches to solving problems. The fourth trait, intercultural competency, is deeply embedded in the student staff experience, and is grounded in an intersectional framework. We define a foundational component of intercultural competency as actively seeking opportunities to increase one’s personal understanding of cultures and lived experiences different from one’s own. We challenge student staff members to move beyond recognizing intercultural competency as an individual process to acknowledging institutional and systemic components of oppression and marginalization. We define the last trait, administrative and organizational management, as adhering to high standards of personal and professional conduct, responding positively to change, and demonstrating a willingness to learn new methods for organization and administrative tasks.

ETR is intended to support the learning and development of undergraduate student staff in the Pride Center and Office of Multicultural Affairs and is utilized throughout the year as both a reflective and evaluative tool. Throughout the year, student staff members are challenged to strengthen these five traits through their work in the Pride Center, in the classroom, in other leadership capacities, and personally. Student staff reflect on the five traits regularly in one-on-one meetings with other staff members and supervisors. ETR is also incorporated into biannual comprehensive reviews. The reviews are grounded in the concept of multi-rater feedback and challenge traditional unidirectional performance review approaches (Bracken, Dalton, Jako, McCauley, & Pollman, 1997), as well as the principles of the LPM, which views learning as co-creative and non-hierarchical (Baxter Magolda, 2004).

While student staff are held to high standards, we recognize the need to learn alongside the student staff and meet our own expectations within the various traits outlined in the rubric. At the end of each semester, both professional and student staff undergo the comprehensive review process infused with language from the rubric, whereby we evaluate ourselves, our peers, our supervisors, and our supervisees (if applicable). The comprehensive reviews and weekly one-on-ones aid to ensure staff accountability for growth to meet our holistic, developmentally appropriate outcomes.

## Assessment and Evaluation

Assessment is an integral aspect of the student staff model in the Pride Center. Through the utilization of both formal and informal assessment techniques, strengths and opportunities for growth were acknowledged. Our assessment approach is multi-faceted; we begin with each student staff member completing a self-assessment that asks them to reflect on their strengths and growth edges in the context of the ETR. Following the completion of the rubric, bi-weekly one-on-ones are constructed as opportunities to discuss projects and aspects of the rubric to ensure that students are working to utilize their strengths and learning more about areas of personal growth. Last, we utilize a comprehensive review process at the end of each semester where all staff (professional staff included) complete an evaluation for each of their fellow team members. The staff may send the form to other individuals if they desire feedback from people in other areas of their lives.

Anecdotally, we have witnessed our students progress in their understanding of intersectional praxis as they willingly embrace the process of critical reflection about their own social identities and how they interact with systems of power, privilege, and oppression. We have found that bi-weekly check-ins, and the use of comprehensive reviews and ETR, helped students critically reflect on growth areas in increasingly deeper, more intentional ways. Many of the student staff members hold leadership roles in student organizations that are advised by Pride Center staff. We have seen these student staff members encourage their executive boards to think about how leadership decisions may impact general members who may hold different identities than their own. While planning Pride Center programs, the staff challenge each other to think critically about how their biases may impact the way they plan such events. These are just two examples that demonstrate student progress in their development toward self-authorship and intersectional leaders.
