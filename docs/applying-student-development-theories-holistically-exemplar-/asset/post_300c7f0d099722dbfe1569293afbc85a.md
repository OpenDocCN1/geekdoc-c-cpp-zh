
## STUDENT DEVELOPMENT THEORIES IN PRACTICE

Buoyed by such supports as the policies in the standards and guidelines of the Council for the Advancement of Standards in Higher Education (2015), research evidenced by the body of work in *How College Affects Students* (Mayhew et al., 2016), and the theories of student development such as those presented in this book, today’s student affairs educators have addressed many challenges in students’ education. And it is not magic; we understand why and how those interventions work particularly through the theoretical grounding they evidence.

We remain challenged to address deep and pervasive societal issues such as acts of hate, racism, alcohol and drug abuse, and sexual violence. Our scholarship helps us understand root causes of these issues, but successful resolution still evades taking interventions to scale. Hopefully readers will rise to the challenge to address these pervasive and difficult issues. Examples in this book offer lessons from their programs that can be leveraged or at scale to address the complex agenda.

Branch, Hart-Steffes, and Wilson and their authors in this book provide examples that explicitly link useful theories to the intentional design of programs and practices that achieve desired student outcomes. Every reader should pause now and reflect on their personal knowledge of relevant theory, knowledge of related research, skill at applying that scholarship to practice, and ability to learn from practice to redesign programs. Every professional needs a growth mindset (Dweck, 2008) that we can learn to be proficient in this process. It takes all of us sharing good praxis to advance the work of any of us.

Each practitioner/educator must adopt a professional identity that includes some combination of being a scholar and practitioner. Some years ago, I wrote about a continuum of professional orientation (Figure 0.3; Komives, 1998).

Many staff members are solid practitioners. They may be administratively skilled, operating from informal theory and strong common sense, but are largely atheoretical and uninformed by evidence. Practitioners who bring scholarship to their work seek out theory and research that informs their practice. The practitioner-scholars identify more with scholarship and are skilled at making applications of both theory and research to practice. Scholar-practitioners ground their work in related theory and evidence to support or modify their programs. On the far extreme are scholars who thrive by developing theory, models of practice, and doing original research, making recommendations to practice but not engaging in the design or applications of practice. All readers should locate their own professional identity on that continuum. I sincerely hope that identity includes being a scholar-practitioner.

The primary audience for this book is graduate students in student affairs and higher education programs who are in courses such as adult development, the impact of college on students, student affairs leadership, and program or policy development in higher education. Another audience is current higher education professionals who need either updates on their theoretical knowledge or inspirations for theory-in-practice links. Thus, this book can be helpful in both an academic curriculum and professional development initiatives.

The contents of this book are a ready companion to other publications that provide detailed descriptions of human development theories used in higher education. For example, many of the names of theorists and theoretical constructs in this book are discussed in books that serve as primary course texts (e.g., Jones & Abes, 2013; Patton, Renn, Guido, & Quaye, 2016; Strange & Banning, 2015; Strayhorn, 2016). Furthermore, because of the potential theoretical overlap with previously acquired knowledge, this book’s contents can fold into professional staff development initiatives designed to encourage the intentional, holistic use of theoretical knowledge and assessment techniques.

What is unique about the contents of this book are the demonstrations of how practitioners are using theories from recent scholarly publications and conference presentations in their work with twenty-first-century college students, how assessment is being carried out, and what is being learned about the complexities of applying human development theories in multidimensional, holistic ways. Authors provide insights about what they learned when approaching the theory-in-practice cycle with professional intentionality, make recommendations, and provide resource suggestions.

The chapters are organized into three parts, which parallel theoretical groupings in the research focused on the development of college students:

- Psychosocial and social identity (i.e., Who am I?)
- Cognitive-structural processes (i.e., How do I make meaning?)
- Environmental influences (i.e., What external elements influence my development?)
