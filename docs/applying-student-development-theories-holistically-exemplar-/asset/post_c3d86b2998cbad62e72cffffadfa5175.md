
## Guiding Theories

Conception and continued development of Tippie Buddies are guided by Phinney's (1993) model for ethnic identity development. Similar to Phinney's definition, there should be a clear distinction between ethnic identity development and racial identity development. This can be a difficult task as the two are often conflated. This juxtaposition is further exacerbated by the persistent lack of differentiation between race and ethnicity in the United States and the claims of reaching a "postracial" era (Johnston-Guerrero, 2016). In operationalizing this model, we believe it is important to note that ethnic identity is defined with a critical, intersectional lens that acknowledges systems of power that affect students' identities and how they navigate college life on campuses. In this sense, the program is designed to engage students from all backgrounds, including White students, in activities that require reflecting and sharing with others what it means to be a member of their respective ethnic groups.

Phinney's (1993) model operates in three stages:

- Unexamined ethnic identity, whereby individuals have not explored their ethnicity in relation to self-identity
- Ethnic identity search/moratorium, whereby encounters with difference (e.g., overt discrimination/racism either toward self or others, minoritized group(s), situations that reveal privilege) cause greater awareness of the role ethnicity plays in one's everyday life and the integration of such experiences with one's own identity
- Ethnic identity achievement, where resolutions of such experiences with difference coalesce with other aspects of self-identity to acceptance of one's membership in his/her/their ethnic culture

Individuals engage in processes that guide their understanding through each phase or status. Periods of exploration emerge when one learns about both positive and negative aspects of one's own group and others. Moratorium can occur during the exploration process when one is uncertain about sense of belonging in the group and whether to fully identify as being associated with group members. Commitment is the process by which acceptance occurs and is typically associated with positive group attitudes.

Phinney's (1993) model is often used within the context of ethnic minorities but was adopted as Buddies' guiding theory because it has greater understanding of self and others while understanding there is no concrete terminus point to the process.

## Implementation Specifics

Now in its eighth semester, Tippie Buddies is a voluntary pairing program with multiple and varied touchpoints throughout the semester. The design strategy is guided by the intended developmental outcomes mentioned previously, with the overarching goal being the creation of a structure within which students have a strong chance of participating in meaning-making experiences.

Students apply to the program through a simple on-line application, which collects information such as major, year in school, gender, hobbies, and personal adjectives (e.g., “How would your friends describe you?”). Following the application deadline at the end of the second week of the semester, the Assistant Director and Ambassadors create pairings based on students’ applications. Participants are notified of the name and email address of their buddy once pairings are complete. The following week, the pairs meet for the first time at a kick-off event, which is intended to be both social and informational in nature.

For the remainder of the semester, buddies are connected to the program through structured events and incentives for independent meetings. Buddies are expected to meet at least five times per semester. Touchpoints include the following:

## Assessment and Evaluation

Tippie Buddies is designed to engage students from all backgrounds with the intent for self-exploration and sharing with others what it means to be a member of their respective ethnic groups. In the next section, we described how these outcomes are assessed.

### Assessment Through Post-Participation Surveys and Advisor Observations

Feedback and insight from participants have informed the direction of the program since its inception. Assessment data for the Tippie Buddies program has primarily come from an anonymous post-participation “exit” survey made available to participants upon conclusion of the program. Survey items are open-ended and prompt students to articulate aspects of the program they felt went well and aspects that could be improved. Additionally, the survey invites participants to reflect on the benefits, if any, the program has provided. The overall response through the semesters has been positive. For example, of respondents to past surveys, given every semester since Spring 2014, 81% either agreed or strongly agreed with the statement, “The program helped reduce misunderstandings that my buddy and I had about each other’s cultures.” Data collected within that same time frame indicated more than 97% of respondents either agreed or strongly agreed with the statement, “I would recommend the Tippie Buddies program to a friend.”

Assessment data is collected informally through individual advising meetings, where students often discuss and disclose both positive and negative experiences with the program openly. The dual role of the Assistant Director and colleagues as both coordinators of Tippie Buddies and undergraduate academic advisors proves useful when soliciting participant feedback. This allows the professional staff to gain deeper insight in one-on-one meetings to elaborate on trends that emerge from the exit survey, for instance.

### Opportunity to Experience Difference

Many participants have explicitly noted that they chose to come to the University of Iowa because they knew it would afford ample opportunities to connect with people different from them and, as one student said, “expand my cultural perspective.” We have learned that day-to-day interactions as part of a buddies program can spark deeper reflection in students. For example, several students remembered how issues of personal space, and the cultural context of what that means when interacting with others, prompted more critical examination of how to be sensitive to others’ preferences.

In the same sense, students often claim that one of the biggest values of the program is providing, in another student’s words, “a space for open dialogue about differences in cultures.” Additional examples of survey responses include the following:

- “I never felt awkward during the buddy events because we all knew that we were doing something a little different than what we were used to and because of that I felt like I really learned what it’s like to understand someone from another country.”
- “It creates a positive and safe space for buddies to share on a one-on-one basis without feeling any potential academic or social pressures.”
- “I liked that we were encouraged to discuss each other’s cultures and personalities without using small talk. We felt comfortable enough to discuss challenges, stereotypes, fears, and hopes while at the University of Iowa.”
- “I have learned that international students are no different than I am—I love that my buddy and I share the exact same morals and values, even though we are from opposite sides of the world.”

### International Students Experience Difference Within a Welcoming Support Structure

For international students in particular, the program appears beneficial because, despite the university’s efforts, it can be difficult to build meaningful friendships with students of different cultural backgrounds, particularly at a PWI. For many international students, the idea of difference is inherent from the time they enroll at the university, not the least of which stems from the strict regulations that govern their stay.
