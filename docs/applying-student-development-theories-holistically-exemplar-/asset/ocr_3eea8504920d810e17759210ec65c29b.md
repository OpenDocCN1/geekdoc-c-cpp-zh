Of importance to this book are the six behavioral themes that Kuh (2008) extrapolated that were undergirding those HIPs that made the impact they evidenced. These themes are:

+   - Investing time and effort
+   - Interacting with faculty and peers about substantive matters
+   - Experiencing diversity
+   - Responding to more frequent feedback
+   - Reflecting and integrating learning
+   - Discovering relevance of learning through real-world applications

One example of the progress educators have made is evident in our own work with student leadership development. Colleges have incorporated student leadership theory (e.g., the social change model of leadership development [SCM; Higher Education Research Institute, 1996]) along with related theoretical input frames (e.g., racial identity, leadership self-efficacy) with their data from the Multi-institutional Study of Leadership (MSL; see www.leadershipstudy.net) that examines the SCM and numerous campus engagement experiences to identify experiences that promote growth in leadership capacity. Numerous college environment practices such as engaging students in discussions with others on sociopolitical issues, mentoring, holding positional leader roles, and community service are high-impact practices for developing leadership capacity (Dugan, Kodama, Correia, & Associates, 2013). Combined with understandings of leadership identity development (LID: see Komives, Owen, Longerbeam, Mainella, & Osteen, 2005), campuses are now scaffolding their leadership programs to build developmental complexity using leadership HIPs identified from studies such as the MSL research.
