Across the literature, a range of students are recognized as FGS because no one definition as to what constitutes FGS status exists (Davis, 2010).

This chapter explores Western 1st Generation Society (W1GS), a registered student organization at WIU for FGS. The advising team for W1GS and authors of this chapter include a faculty member and graduate students from WIU's College Student Personnel (advising team members are also the authors of this chapter.)

A key component we considered during the creation of W1GS was finances. We understood that nationally, FGS have a lower median household income and more unmet financial need compared with non-FGS students (Postsecondary National Institute, 2016). Although research indicates that not all FGS are low-income individuals (Davis, 2010), we considered it important to keep the cost for W1GS to a minimum. Accordingly, W1GS was created and implemented with little or no costs to the students.

## DESCRIPTION OF WESTERN 1ST GENERATION SOCIETY

The idea for W1GS originated from the lead author of this chapter. Because I had worked in student activities, creating a student organization was a familiar process for addressing an unmet need. "More than anything else, FGS need a place to go, a place to be themselves, when they begin their college career. Finding a place to go, to study, to hang out with friends, accelerates the acclimation process by directly addressing the transition from the home space to the college space" (Davis, 2010, p. 72). While some needs of FGS students may be served through resources such as cultural centers, FGS also have multiple identities that influence their success. It seemed as though an experience tailored for FGS could better aid in supporting their success.

The initial implementation and continued development of W1GS are centered on five core values: dedication, grit, curiosity, community, and integrity. The advising team selected these values based on research regarding mindsets, stereotype threat, behaviors, and skills that FGS use to succeed in college (Davis, 2010; Steele, 2011; Ward, Siegal, & Davenport, 2012). Each of these values are identified as abilities FGS
can learn, or already have, but need help transferring into the college environment (Davis, 2010; Steele, 2011; Ward et al., 2012).

## Guiding Theories

The design for W1GS is guided by three theories: Davis’s (2010) content theory on the first-generation student experience, and two process theories: Baxter Magolda’s (2001) theory of self-authorship and Kolb’s (1984) theory of experiential learning. Davis’s (2010) theory helps us understand what the unique needs of FGS are. When used in tandem, self-authorship (Baxter Magolda, 2001) and experiential learning (Kolb, 1984) challenge us to think about how we go about creating and implementing an initiative to meet those needs.

According to Davis (2010), FGS are largely underserved by colleges and universities. This is especially true at public, four-year institutions where students are “more likely allowed to stay passive about education and potentially become psychologically disaffected, or fall through the cracks” (p. 20). Davis also highlights specific issues FGS face in higher education settings that impact their success. Each issue informs the developmental outcomes created for W1GS and the process through which those outcomes are achieved.

Davis (2010) also indicates that “FGS are unfamiliar with the culture of college and, to one degree or another, unfamiliar with what it means to be a college student” (p. 29). To introduce them to college culture, FGS need a “guide,” not “an expert” (p. 32). W1GS advisors serve as guides to students in the student organization.

## Imposter Syndrome

Research demonstrates “many FGS suffer from imposter phenomenon” both inside and outside of the classroom (Davis, 2010, p. 48). They suffer “when things are going badly and when things are going well” (p. 48). FGS often talk themselves out of believing they deserve positive experiences, and negative experiences affirm their belief that they “are taking a place at the institution that should have gone to a more worthy individual” (p. 49). FGS feel more acute anxiety about not fitting in than their non-FGS counterparts (Davis, 2010).

## Implementation Specifics

Initially, the advising team developed ideas for recruitment and promotion, and identified the organization’s values. W1GS would be open to all self-identified FGS. We agreed that, if the organization attracted no students, it would not be pursued further; we wanted W1GS to be an organization created by the students, for the students. Advertising involved a table display in the middle of campus, where we recruited the initial group of student members. On the first day, more than 60 students signed up to join the organization.

Throughout the initial semester, we worked with student members to create a name, logo, and motto. "Western 1st Generation Society" became our name and "First in Line" became the motto. We also considered what the core values (i.e., dedication, grit, curiosity, community, and integrity) meant for the organization as a whole. During this process, members were able to build a general sense of community that was further enhanced by having different group members and advisors share personal FGS stories at each meeting. Our approach to creating this organization served as a way for everyone to connect through common experiences, which is why we decided to use this process each semester.

The second semester, W1GS members and advisors worked to create governing documents for the organization. The students held elections for President and Treasurer and registered W1GS as an official student organization. One major task in this process was drafting a constitution, which includes the developmental outcomes. The constitution also defines a nontraditional meeting structure; weekly meetings are run as a collaborative effort among the members, rather than a positional effort. W1GS alternates weekly between business meetings and social meetings. Social meetings allow members to continue building connections with one another, their campus, and their community. Business meetings provide members time to discuss ideas and make organizational and logistical decisions.

Today, W1GS claims more than 165 members. Approximately 10 to 15 FGS consistently attend weekly meetings. All members of the organization receive weekly emails containing resources for FGS and the upcoming W1GS meeting agenda. Members are also invited to join the W1GS Facebook group and to communicate via the GroupMe® texting application. ## Outcome Five: Creating and Serving as a Community of Resources for W1GS Members

Members have a community of support and resources beyond the W1GS meetings themselves. For example, around midterms and finals, members reach out to request support for upcoming exams or assignments through the GroupMe® texting application. Advisors participate in the texting application so they are aware of what support has been requested. When students note that they need assistance in specific areas, members work together to identify and compile available resources, which are then shared through the application. Similarly, when issues occur in the world (e.g., hurricanes), or in one’s personal life (e.g., family member passing), the texting application is used to help one another find personal support.
