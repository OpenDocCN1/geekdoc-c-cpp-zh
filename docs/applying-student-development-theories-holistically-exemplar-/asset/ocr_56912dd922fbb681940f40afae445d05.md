# Guiding Theories

The design for W1GS is guided by three theories: Davis’s (2010) content theory on the first-generation student experience, and two process theories: Baxter Magolda’s (2001) theory of self-authorship and Kolb’s (1984) theory of experiential learning. Davis’s (2010) theory helps us understand what the unique needs of FGS are. When used in tandem, self-authorship (Baxter Magolda, 2001) and experiential learning (Kolb, 1984) challenge us to think about how we go about creating and implementing an initiative to meet those needs.

According to Davis (2010), FGS are largely underserved by colleges and universities. This is especially true at public, four-year institutions where students are “more likely allowed to stay passive about education and potentially become psychologically disaffected, or fall through the cracks” (p. 20). Davis also highlights specific issues FGS face in higher education settings that impact their success. Each issue informs the developmental outcomes created for W1GS and the process through which those outcomes are achieved.

Davis (2010) also indicates that “FGS are unfamiliar with the culture of college and, to one degree or another, unfamiliar with what it means to be a college student” (p. 29). To introduce them to college culture, FGS need a “guide,” not “an expert” (p. 32). W1GS advisors serve as guides to students in the student organization.

# Imposter Syndrome

Research demonstrates “many FGS suffer from imposter phenomenon” both inside and outside of the classroom (Davis, 2010, p. 48). They suffer “when things are going badly and when things are going well” (p. 48). FGS often talk themselves out of believing they deserve positive experiences, and negative experiences affirm their belief that they “are taking a place at the institution that should have gone to a more worthy individual” (p. 49). FGS feel more acute anxiety about not fitting in than their non-FGS counterparts (Davis, 2010).
