This does not require expertise in ethics or psychology, or in cognitive or moral reasoning development theory to be able to help students grow. However, because of the flexibility and broad distribution of this program, implementation is not always consistent, which can affect learning and assessment.

Ethical Reasoning in Action evolved from the university's reaccreditation process, which requires the conception, funding, and implementation of a new idea focused on enhancing student learning (JMU, 2012). Because of this, we are at a significant advantage for an institution-wide implementation. However, one of the best things about this program is its scalability. Smaller-scope and audience-specific programs are created by staff motivated to develop students' ethical reasoning abilities. The large scale of a program may be difficult to implement across a campus without institutional support, but it is entirely possible for an office or professional staff member to develop a program around this content. Aside from time and staffing, program costs are minor: copies of materials and writing utensils.

One unintended outcome of the 8KQ framework relates back to application of the 8KQ in a confirmatory (Haidt, 2001) rather than exploratory way, which can block growth. The questions should be used prior to making decisions rather than to describe what one has already done. This leads to a second caution: The 8KQ should not be used to judge or analyze someone else's actions, but rather as a prompt for personal agency and responsibility for decision-making. While ethical reasoning can be used to understand another's actions, it is a tool for forward thinking and acting.

One significant lesson learned from implementation of this program is the limitation of students' cognitive development over time. As practitioners, we look at theories as a map for students' growth and know it is not always that straightforward, but implementation of a large-scale program like this emphasizes and makes real that limitation. We must ask ourselves: What can we actually expect students to be able to think and do? Upon graduation, some students will reach advanced levels of ethical reasoning, but many will not. Our hope is that by developing the habit of asking questions, the "plus one" nature of the program will continue to help them challenge their own quick thinking and provide repeated growth opportunities.
