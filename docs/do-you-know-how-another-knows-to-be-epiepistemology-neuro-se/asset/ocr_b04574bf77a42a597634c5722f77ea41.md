Then, one day she came on a very upbeat note. Her husband came home one evening and said: I need a holiday. We are going camping. This was exactly the kind of holidays that she always had with him and with the children. She was overjoyed. At last, the family could be together and have some really good quality time. She heard nothing more about the holiday but kept the faith and patiently waited for the notice when they would be off.

He came home one evening and told her: This Friday, your father and I are going up north camping and fishing! She was stunned into silence! She never said a word nor showed her bitter disappointment. She knew he needed a break.

However, when he returned from his holiday with her father, she was “fit to be tied” and she dragged him to see me. She blurted out the above story to me. He was silent. When she had told her story, I slowly turned to him and asked:

> I know that having this holiday with your father-in-law was the RIGHT thing for you.
> (I paused.)

> However, I wonder, do you think it was a FITTING thing to exclude your wife and your children from the holiday?

Now, upon hearing what I put to him, he bowed his head quite chastened. He quietly replied:

> No!

All of us are driven by our idiosyncratic and uniquely personal conclusions with respect to what for us is RIGHT to do. Yet, by this case example, at a deeper level, this man had no choice but to agree. From his anchored innate, heritable program of lawfulness and semantic well-formedness, he was forced to admit that what he had decided to do for his holiday was not well-formed; it was ill-fitting to exclude his wife and his children.
