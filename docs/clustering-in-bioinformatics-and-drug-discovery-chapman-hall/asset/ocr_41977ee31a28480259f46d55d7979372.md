With $C$ form an $N \times N$ matrix, $A_C$. Here, the $(i,j)$ matrix element represents the presence (1) or absence (0) of the $i$ and $j$ data members in the same cluster. Similarly, a binary matrix is generated using the expert partition, $\mathcal{E}$, $A_E$. The binary matrices can then be compared much like the binary strings in Chapter 2. Only the $(N)(N-1)/2$ triangular portions of the matrices are needed as the binary matrices are each symmetric. Any of the various binary measures can be used but typically the most common is the Tanimoto (for historical reasons more commonly referred to as the Jaccard in this application) to capture the similarity between the clustering and the expert partition. It is then a question as to what value is sufficient to make the claim that the clustering method works well. If the clustering method is hierarchical in nature, then comparisons can be made between different levels of the hierarchy and the expert partition.

Other measures are also used in this connection, most notably Hubert’s $\Gamma$ statistic,

$\Gamma = \sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N} A_C(i,j)A_E(i,j), \qquad (10.8)$

and its normalized form,

$\hat{\Gamma} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}(A_C(i,j)-\mu_{A_C})(A_E(i,j)-\mu_{A_E})}{\sigma_{A_C}\sigma_{A_E}\left(N(N-1)/2\right)}, \qquad (10.9)$

where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviations of the $\binom{N}{2}$ $(i,j)$ elements of the respective matrices. $A_C$ and $A_E$. Hubert’s method is slower computationally, but still useful for modest sized data sets. Because of the quadratic nature of the matrices with respect to the size of the data, these methods nevertheless become unmanageable when the data size becomes large. Experts however are also limited in the size of a reference classification they are able to generate. However this form of external validity can be used to examine clusterings of the same data in a relative fashion, so their computational complexity will be an issue in this context.
