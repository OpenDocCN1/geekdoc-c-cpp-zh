The transpose of a matrix, $A^T$, swaps the $(i,j)$th element for the $(j,i)$th element. The determinant of a matrix is given the notation, $|A|$, and it represents an operation that returns a scalar value. If all the vectors of a matrix are linearly independent (their pair-wise inner product is zero), the determinant is non-zero. The inverse of a matrix, akin to the reciprocal of a real number, is denoted by $A^{-1}$, providing the matrix is invertible; that is, the determinant of the matrix is not zero—analogous to the fact that there is no reciprocal of zero in simple arithmetic.

The concept of eigenvalues and eigenvectors are very important and often fundamental to a considerable amount of statistical learning theory. It is used in single value decomposition, principal component analysis, spectral clustering, and multi-dimensional scaling among other applications. It would be nice therefore to give a more extended presentation on this topic, thereby giving an intuitive feel for the subject, rather than just a set of definitions, equations, and identities.

Starting with the mathematics, for an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the linear equation

$$A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}, \tag{12.9}$$

can be solved in $n$ ways. Namely, there are $n$ possible vectors that $\mathbf{x}$ can take on with a corresponding $n\,\lambda$s (that may not be necessarily unique). Namely,

$$A\mathbf{x}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{x}_i, \tag{12.10}$$

where $i=1,2,\ldots,n$.

To solve for $\mathbf{x}_i$ and $\lambda_i$, Equation 12.9 can be transformed to

$$(A-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=0, \tag{12.11}$$

where $0$ is a vector of $n$ zeros. Now if we take the determinant of the expression $A-\lambda I$, where $A$ is an $n \times n$ square matrix, we get the characteristic equation,

$$|A-\lambda I|=\lambda^n+a_1\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n-1}\lambda+a_n=0. \tag{12.12}$$

This equation can be solved for $n$, not necessarily unique roots, $\lambda$. With each $\lambda_i$ we can solve the set of linear equations that would occur by substitution in 12.11 and derive the corresponding eigenvector, $\mathbf{x}_i$.
