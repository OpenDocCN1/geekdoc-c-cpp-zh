Write software which works first, and only then worry about the bottlenecks and fine-tune them for performance – let the compiler handle the rest. The only time you need to optimise is when a constraint – usually either a time or memory constraint – is not met. This chapter is intended as a guide to manual optimisation and, like most of this book, focusses on doing it reliably and safely.

# Know the operators

You will often hear you can improve execution speed by carefully selecting the operators you use. For instance, multiplication or division by a power of two is often claimed to be more efficiently as either a left or right shift, respectively. This technique, however, is only valid for unsigned integer types and, at least in the case of multiplication, is not guaranteed to result in any increase in speed.

It is worth having at least an idea of the relative performance of the different operators. Generally, for integer arithmetic: addition, subtraction and bitwise operations will typically perform the fastest, shifts usually have similar performance, multiplication will usually, but not necessarily, be slower (this largely depends on whether the CPU includes a hardware multiplier) and the division and modulo operators will almost invariably be the slowest. For floating point arithmetic, multiplication may be marginally faster than both addition and subtraction but is likely to have similar performance, and division is much slower.

For floating point calculations (but not integer ones) it can sometimes be quicker to multiply by the reciprocal of a number than to divide directly.
