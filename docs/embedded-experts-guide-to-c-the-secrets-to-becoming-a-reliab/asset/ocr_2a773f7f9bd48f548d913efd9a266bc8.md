5 Unusual or even unique encodings are not to be confused with encryption, even though it may take time and effort to make the data stored using them intelligible. Crucially, a good encryption algorithm cannot be reversed using knowledge of the algorithm alone; cipher designers start from the assumption that “the enemy knows the system” – a rule known as Kerckhoff’s (not to be confused with Kirchhoff!) Principle or, in that particular form, as Shannon’s Maxim. Cryptographers are notoriously paranoid and will often regard even the most hypothetical of weaknesses as a vulnerability.

6 The cipher most often referred to is RSA. It relies on the fact that there is currently no known efficient method of factoring the product of two large prime numbers. Large is something of an understatement – at the time of writing, the largest value publicly known to have been factorised is for an 829-bit (250 decimal digit) number. It took the equivalent of 2700 Intel Xeon processors working on the problem for a year. It is currently recommended that RSA keys should be at least 2048 bits in length – 512-bit keys have been shown to be insecure using readily available hardware and software, and it is considered likely that a 1024-bit key could be broken by a determined and well-resourced attacker, such as a government security agency. Symmetric ciphers can use much shorter keys for a comparable level of security and do not have such stringent key generation requirements.
