In practice, unless you use dynamic memory allocation, it is determined at link time. Don't worry if this doesn't mean much to you – it's covered later, but it's also not particularly important for most purposes.

- 7 The wording was changed in C11, which confuses things further and has led to claims the behaviour is no longer unspecified for C11 and newer compilers. C99 lists “the value of a union member other than the last one stored into” as unspecified behaviour; C11 changes this to “the values of bytes that correspond to union members other than the one last stored into” which would appear to imply only the bytes which do not overlap are unspecified. The members are guaranteed to, at least partially, overlap, since a pointer to one union member is a pointer to all other members of that union. However, no version of the C Standard makes any guarantees about how different types are represented in memory, so type punning using unions is still not part of any version, even if it is widely supported in practice.

- 8 The maximum frequency deviation from the carrier which can be accurately captured is known as the Nyquist frequency, which is exactly half the sampling frequency. (this is sometimes mistakenly considered the maximum frequency which can be accurately sampled; however, this is only true when there is no carrier signal or the carrier is a DC offset, a fact which can be exploited to drastically lower the sampling frequency used). Frequencies with greater deviations from the carrier will result in aliasing; they will be shifted into the range of sampled frequencies and cause corruption of the signal. Conversely, when generating a signal from output samples, the output should be updated at a rate at least twice the desired signal bandwidth. This analysis is most commonly associated with sampling analogue signals, but it is equally true of digital signals as well, although it is the bit rate, which is sometimes confusingly referred to as bandwidth, of a digital signal which is important rather than the bandwidth of the underlying electrical signal. Analogue inputs almost always need to be processed using an analogue low-pass or band-pass filter prior to sampling – these are known as anti-aliasing filters and cannot be replaced by digital filters.

- 9 Port is a generic term used to refer to a group of inputs or outputs which may be accessed collectively. You may also come across the term bus. A bus is more specific and often refers not only to the physical connections, but their purpose and the protocols they use as well.

- 10 These all require additional hardware of varying complexity in order to interface with a microprocessor or microcontroller. They are all simple, however, in terms of their software requirements.

- 11 A related, but distinct technique known as Pulse Density Modulation (PDM) is a possible alternative (some sources argue PWM is a special case of PDM; I’m not convinced and in any real application such a purely theoretical equivalence is irrelevant anyway). PDM is closely related (and equivalent) to delta-sigma modulation, a technique widely used in analogue-to-digital converters. The details are entirely outside the scope of this book, but PDM can have some interesting benefits, such as reduced electromagnetic emissions, despite requiring a much higher sampling rate to achieve equivalent resolution.

- 12 “Bus” may also be an ambiguous term; more complex architectures will often include a data bus, an address bus and a control bus, collectively referred to as the “system bus,” or simply as the bus. For the purposes of this book, it is assumed the reader is not overly concerned with the exact details of the hardware and the term is used to collectively refer to all required interconnections for implementing a specific protocol. In this sense it includes at least the “physical layer” of the (often largely academic and not overly helpful) seven-layer Open Systems Interconnect (OSI) model.

- 13 Such requirements are common, although timing is often not so critical for a small mismatch to cause problems (the rationale for using opposing clock edges for read and write operations is to ensure hardware set-up and hold times are satisfied, even allowing for mismatched signal propagation delays). At the data rates where bit-banging is useful you are unlikely to encounter problems, although it is another factor limiting the achievable bandwidth without dedicated hardware. Other requirements may prove much more difficult to ignore. I2C, for instance, relies upon open-drain output drivers being used to negotiate communication speed and bus arbitration (determining which of several devices should be transmitting at a given time); without such outputs implementing a true I2C bus through bit-banging is impossible and may even result in hardware damage; even with them you will still encounter significant challenges.

## Chapter 6: Data Security and Integrity

A topic that is sadly neglected in many software development resources is that of security and integrity. This area is, however, more important than ever – the enormous growth in connectivity over the past twenty years or so now means that many seemingly trivial products have the potential to expose their users to problems ranging from nuisance and inconvenience to severe financial losses or physical harm. You may question the need for a plethora of everyday, Internet connected widgets (quite rightly, if you ask me!), but the fact remains that they are being built and sold. Of course, there are many other reasons why this topic is important – it is just no longer to be considered a specialist topic; it is something that every embedded engineer needs to know about.

First, a couple of definitions:

Integrity is about ensuring that data is “correct” – it has not been corrupted between being transmitted or stored and being received or retrieved. Data that is erroneous should be detected and either corrected where possible or discarded.

Security is about ensuring that data cannot be accessed, or commands issued by, those with no right to do so, either intentionally or by mistake. This includes the subject of encryption – the process of making data completely inaccessible without access to additional information.
