# Endnotes for Chapter 1 - Trust the Programmer

1 The idea that C is a portable assembly language has been around for some considerable time; although not explicitly stated, it is hinted at in the introduction to the first edition of The C Programming

2 The “trust the programmer” philosophy was inherited from one of C’s predecessor languages, BCPL. BCPL: The language and its compiler elaborates on this: “The philosophy of BCPL is not one of the tyrant who thinks he knows best and lays down the law on what is and what is not allowed; rather, BCPL acts more as a servant offering his services to the best of his ability without complaint, even when confronted with apparent nonsense. The programmer is always assumed to know what he is doing and is not hemmed in by petty restrictions.” Perhaps at a time when assembly languages still dominated, this was not such a bad philosophy; today it is almost indefensible.

3 Roughly speaking, infinite loops with no side-effects, since C11, result in undefined behaviour. The actual rule explaining this is somewhat more verbose and nuanced. This can cause problems, since sometimes those loops can be very useful!

4 Incidentally, floating point divide-by-zero operations are generally allowed, usually resulting in positive or negative infinity. This is not because the operation is any more meaningful when using floating point arithmetic; rather, it is a quirk included in IEEE 754 as an aid to tracing errors through calculations.
