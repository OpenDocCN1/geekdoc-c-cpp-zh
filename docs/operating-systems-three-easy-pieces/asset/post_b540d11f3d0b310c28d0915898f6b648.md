## The Era of Multiprogramming

Operating systems really took off in the era of computing beyond the mainframe, that of the microcomputer. Classic machines like the PDP family from Digital Equipment made computers hugely more affordable; instead of having one mainframe per organization, now a smaller collection of people within an organization could likely have their own computer. Surprisingly, one of the major impacts of this drop in cost was an increase in developer activity; more people got their hands on computers and thus made computer systems more interesting and beautiful things.

In particular, multiprogramming became commonplace due to the desire to make better use of machine resources. Instead of running one job at a time, the OS load a number of jobs into memory and switch rapidly between them, improving CPU utilization. This switching was particularly important because I/O devices were slow; having a program wait on the CPU while its I/O was being served was a waste of CPU time. Instead, why not switch to another job and run it for?

The desire to support multiprogramming and overlap in the presence of I/O forced innovation in the conceptual development of operating systems along a number of issues. Issues as memory protection became important; we wouldn't want one program to access the memory of another program. Understanding how to deal with the concurrency introduced by multiprogramming was also critical; making sure the OS was behaving despite the presence of interrupts is a great challenge. We will study these and related topics later in the book.

One of the most practical advances of the time was the introduction of the UNIX operating system, primarily thanks to Ken Thompson (and Dennis Ritchie) at Bell Labs (the phone company). UNIX took many good ideas from different operating systems (particularly from Multics [O72], and from systems like TENEX [B+72] and the Berkeley Time-Sharing System [S+68]), but made them simpler and easier to use. ### ASIDE: THE IMPORTANCE OF UNIX

It is difficult to overstate the importance of UNIX in the history of operating systems. Influenced by earlier systems (in particular, the famous Multics system from MIT), UNIX brought together many great ideas and a system that was both simple and powerful. 

Underlying the original "Bell Labs" UNIX was the unifying principle of building small powerful programs that could be connected together to form larger workflows. The shell, where you type commands, provided primitives as pipes to enable such meta-level programming, and thus became easy to string together programs to accomplish a big task. For example, to find lines of a file that have the word "foo" in them, and to count how many lines exist, you would type: `grep file.txt | wc` using the grep and wc (word count) programs to achieve each.

The UNIX environment was friendly for programmers and developers alike, also providing a compiler for the new C language. Making it easy for programmers to write their own programs, as well as share them, made UNIX enormously popular. And it probably helped a lot that the authors could copy for free to anyone who asked, especially free open-source software.

Of critical importance was the accessibility and readability of the code. Having a beautiful kernel written in C invited others to play with the kernel, adding new and cool features. For example, an enterprising group at Berkeley, by Bill Joy, made a wonderful distribution (the Berkeley Systems Distribution, or BSD) which had some advanced virtual memory, file system, and networking subsystems. Unix later found Microsystems.

Unfortunately, the spread of UNIX was slow a bit as companies tried to assert ownership and profit from it, an unfortunate (but common) result of lawyers getting involved. Many companies had their own variants: SunOS, AIX from IBM, HPUX (a.k.a. “H-Pucks”) from HP, and IRIX from SGL.
