# MECHANISM: ADDRESS TRANSLATION

# References

[M65] "On Dynamic Program Relocation" by W.C. McGee. IBM Systems Journal, Volume 4, 1968, pages 184–199. This paper is nice summary of early work on dynamic relocation, as well as some basics on static relocation.

[P90] "Relocating loader for MS-EXE executable files" by Kenneth D. A. Pillay. Microprocessors & Systems, Volume 14 (September 1990). An example of a relocating loader for MS-DOS. Not the first, but a relatively modern example of how such system works.

[S74] "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems" by J. Seltzer and M. Schroeder. CACM, July 1974. From this paper: "The concepts of base-and-bound and hardware-interpreted descriptors appeared, apparently as early as 1958, on three different memory protection goals. At M.I.T., McCarthy suggested the base-and-bound ideas as part of the memory protection system necessary to make time-sharing feasible. IBM independently developed the base-and-bound register as a mechanism to permit relocatable multiprogramming of the Stretch (7030) computer system. At Burroughs, during the development of the B5000 computer system, R. Barton argued that hardware-level support provided direct support for the naming rules...

[S04] "System Call Support" by Mark Smother. 2004. people.clemson.edu/mark/syscall.html. A neat history of system call support. Smother has also collected some early history on items like interrupts and other aspects of computing history. His well pages for more details.

[WL93] "Efficient Software-based Fault Isolation" by Robert Wahahe, Steven Lucas, Thomas E. Anderson, Susan L. Graham. SOPAS '93. A terrific paper about how you can use compiler support to bound memory references from a program, without hardware support. The paper sparked renewed interest in software techniques for building reliable systems.

[W17] Answer to footnote: "Is there anything other than cannot be hacked?" by Wacium Wanjoki. 2017. The answer, thanks to Mr. Wanjoki: "It is only since about 1970 that 'weak havoc' has been more popular than 'weak vengeance.' In the 1880s, the wreak was almost always followed by 'his/their vengeance.' Apparently, when you wreak, you are up to no goal, but at least wreakers have some options.
