## 7.1 Workload Assumptions

Before getting into the range of possible policies, let us make a number of simplifying assumptions about the processes running in the system, sometimes collectively called the **workload**. Determining the workload is a critical part of building policies, and the more you know about workload, the more fine-tuned your policy can be.

The workload assumptions we make here are mostly unrealistic, but that is alright (for now), because we will relax them as we go, and eventually develop what we will refer to as ... (dramatic pause) ... a fully-operational scheduling discipline'.

We will make the following assumptions about the processes, some-times called jobs, that are running in the system:

- 1. Each job runs for the same amount of time.
- 2. All jobs arrive at the same time.
- 3. Once started, each job runs to completion.
- 4. All jobs only use the CPU (i.e., they perform no I/O)
- 5. The run-time of each job is known.

We said many of these assumptions were unrealistic, but just as some animals are more equal than others in Orwell's Animal Farm [045], some assumptions are more unrealistic than those in scheduling. In particu-lar, it might bother you that the run-time of each job is known: this would make the scheduler omniscient, although it would be great (prob-ably), is not likely to happen anyone.

## Scheduling Metrics

Beyond making workload assumptions, we also need one more thing to enable us to compare different scheduling policies: a scheduling met-ric metric is something that we use to measuring, and there are a number of different metrics that make sense in scheduling.

For now, let us simplify by simply having a sin-gle metric: turnaround time. The turnaround of a job is defined as the time at which the job completes minus the time at which the job arrived in the system. More formally, the turnaround time $T_{turnaround}$ is:

```
$T_{turnaround} = T_{completion} - T_{arrival} \quad (7.1)
```

Because we have assumed that all jobs arrive at the same time, for now $T_{turnaround} = 0$ and hence $T_{turnaround} = T_{completion}$. This fact will change as we relax the aforementioned assumptions.

You should note that turnaround time is a performance metric, which will be our primary focus of this chapter. Another metric of interest is fair-ness, as performance and fairness are often at odds in scheduling; a scheduler, for example, by Jain's Fairness Index [91]. Per-ample may optimize performance but at the cost of preventing a few jobs from running, decreasing fairness. This conundrum shows that life isn't always perfect.

## First In, First Out (FIFO)

The most basic algorithm we can implement is known as First In, First Out (FIFO) scheduling or sometimes First Come, First Served (FCFS).

FIFO has a number of positive properties: it is clearly simple and thus easy to implement. And, given our assumptions, it works perfectly well.
Let's do a quick example. Imagine three jobs arrive in the system, A, B, and C, at the same time ($T_{arrival} = 0$). Because FIFO has to put some job first, let's assume that they arrived simultaneously, A arrived just a hair before B which arrived just a hair before C. Assume that each job runs for 10 seconds. What will the average turnaround time be for these jobs?

From Figure 7.1, you can see that A finishes at 10, B at 20, and C at 30. Thus, the average turnaround time for the three jobs is simply $\frac{10+20+30}{3} = 20$. Computing turnaround time is as easy as that.

## 7.4 Shortest Job First (SJF)

It turns out that a very simple approach solves this problem; in fact it is an ideal from operations research [C54,PV56] and applied to scheduling of jobs in computer systems. This new scheduling discipline is known as Shortest Job First (SJF), and the name should be easy to remember because it describes the policy completely: it runs the shortest job first, then the next shortest, and so on.

![](img/133d518e3f69f4caaead32c569ae6c49_111_0.png)

Figure 7.3: SJF Simple Example

Let’s take our example above with SJF as our scheduling policy. Figure 7.3 shows the results of running A, B, and C. Hopefully the diagram makes it clear why SJF performs much better with regards to average turnaround time. By simply running B and C before SJF, it reduces average turnaround from 110 seconds to 50 (10+20+120=50), more than a factor of two improvement.
