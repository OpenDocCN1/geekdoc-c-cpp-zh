# INTRODUCTION TO OPERATING SYSTEMS

# The Era of Multiprogramming

Operating systems really took off in the era of computing beyond the mainframe, that of the microcomputer. Classic machines like the PDP family from Digital Equipment made computers hugely more affordable; instead of having one mainframe per organization, now a smaller collection of people within an organization could likely have their own computer. Surprisingly, one of the major impacts of this drop in cost was an increase in developer activity; more people got their hands on computers and thus made computer systems more interesting and beautiful things.

In particular, multiprogramming became commonplace due to the desire to make better use of machine resources. Instead of running one job at a time, the OS load a number of jobs into memory and switch rapidly between them, improving CPU utilization. This switching was particularly important because I/O devices were slow; having a program wait on the CPU while its I/O was being served was a waste of CPU time. Instead, why not switch to another job and run it for?

The desire to support multiprogramming and overlap in the presence of I/O forced innovation in the conceptual development of operating systems along a number of issues. Issues as memory protection became important; we wouldn't want one program to access the memory of another program. Understanding how to deal with the concurrency introduced by multiprogramming was also critical; making sure the OS was behaving despite the presence of interrupts is a great challenge. We will study these and related topics later in the book.

One of the most practical advances of the time was the introduction of the UNIX operating system, primarily thanks to Ken Thompson (and Dennis Ritchie) at Bell Labs (the phone company). UNIX took many good ideas from different operating systems (particularly from Multics [O72], and from systems like TENEX [B+72] and the Berkeley Time-Sharing System [S+68]), but made them simpler and easier to use.
