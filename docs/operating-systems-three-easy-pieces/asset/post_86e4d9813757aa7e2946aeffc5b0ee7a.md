# Preface

## To Everyone

Welcome to this book! We hope you'll enjoy reading it as much as we enjoyed writing it. The book is called *Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces* (available at http://ostep.org), and the title is obviously an homage to one of the greatest sets of lecture notes ever created, by Richard Feynman on the topic of Physics [96]. While this book will undoubtedly fall short of the high standard set by that famous physicist, perhaps it will be good enough for you in your quest to understand what operating systems (and more generally, systems) are all about.

The three easy pieces refer to the three major thematic elements the book is organized around: virtualization, concurrency, and persistence. In discussing these concepts, we'll end up discussing many of the important things an operating system does; hopefully, you'll also have some along the way. Learning new things is fun, right? At least, (usually) it should be.

Each major concept is divided into a set of chapters, most of which present a particular problem and then how to solve it. The chapters are short, and try (as best as possible) to reference the source material where the ideas really come from. One of our goals in writing this book is to make the paths of history as clear as possible, as we think that helps a student understand what is, what was, and what will be more clearly. In this case, seeing how the sausage was made is as important as understanding what the sausage is for.

There are a couple devices we use throughout the book which are worth mentioning here. The first is the **crux of the problem**. Whenever we try to solve a problem, we first try to state what the most important use is; such a crux of the problem is explicitly called out in the text, and hopefully solved via the techniques, algorithms, and ideas presented in the rest of the text.

In many places, we will explain how a system works by showing its behavior over time. These timelines are at the essence of understanding; if you know what happens, for example, when a process page faults, you are on your way to truly understanding how virtual memory operates.

> Hint: Or eating! If you're a vegetarian, running away from comprehend what takes place when a journaling file system writes a block to disk, you have taken the first steps towards mastery of storage systems.

There are numerous **aides** and **tips** throughout the text, adding a little color to the mainline presentation. Aides tend to discuss relevant (but perhaps not essential) topics to the main text; tips tend to be general lessons that can be applied to systems you build. An index at the end of the book lists all of these tips and aides (as well as the plural of crux) for your convenience.

We use one of the oldest didactic methods, the dialogue, throughout the book, as a way of presenting some of the material in a different light. These are used to introduce the major thematic concepts (in a peachy way, as we will see), as well as to review material now and then. They are also a chance to write in a more humorous style. Whether you find them useful, or humorous, that matters another entirely.

At the beginning of each major section, we will first present an abstraction that is an operating system, and then work in subsequent chapters on the mechanisms, policies, and other support needed to provide the abstraction. Abstractions are fundamental to all aspects of Computer Science, or it is perhaps no surprise that they are also essential in operating systems.

Through the chapters, we try to use real code (not pseudocode) where possible, for virtually all examples, you should be able to type them yourself and run them. Running real code on real systems is the best way to learn about operating systems, so we encourage you to do when you can. We are also making code available for your viewing pleasure.

In various parts of the text, we have sprinkled in a few homeworks to ensure that you are understanding what is going on. Many of these homeworks are little simulations of pieces of the operating system; you should download them and run them to quiz yourself. The homework simulators have the following feature: by giving them a different seed, you can generate a virtually infinite set of problems; thus, you can test and re-test yourself until you have achieved a good level of understanding.

The most important addendum to this book is a set of projects in how you learn about real systems by designing, implementing, and testing your code. All projects (as well as the examples mentioned above) are in the C programming language [K&88]; C is a simple and powerful language that underlies most operating systems, and thus worth adding to your tool-chest of projects. Two types of systems programming projects; these projects are great for those who are new to C and UNIX and want to learn how to do low-level C programming. The second type are based on a real operating system kernel developed at MIT called xv6 [CK+08]; these projects are great for students that already have some C and want to get their hands dirty inside the OS. At Wisconsin, we've run the course in three different ways: either all systems programming, all xv6 programming, or a mix of both.

We are slowly making project descriptions, and a testing framework, available. See our repository^3 for more information. If not part of a class, this will give you a chance to do these projects on your own, to better learn the material. Unfortunately, you don't have a TA to bug when you get stuck, but not everything in life can be free (but books can be!).

The course divides fairly well across a 15-week semester, in which you can cover most of the topics within a reasonable level of depth. Cramming the course into a 10-week quarter requires dropping some detail from each piece. There are also a few chapters on virtual monitors, which we usually squeeze in sometime the semester, either right at the large section on virtualization, or near the end as an aside.

One slightly unusual aspect of the book is that concurrency, a topic at the front of many OS books, is pushed off until the student has built an understanding of virtualization of the CPU and of memory. In our experience teaching this course for nearly 20 years, students have a hard time understanding how concurrency arises, or why they are trying to solve it, if they don't understand what address space is, what a process is, or why context switches occur at arbitrary points in time.
