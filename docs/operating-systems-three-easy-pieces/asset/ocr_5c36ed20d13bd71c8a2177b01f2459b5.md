There are two types of locality that programs tend to exhibit. The first is known as spatial locality, which states that if a page P is accessed, it is the pages around it (say P - 1 or P + 1) which will likely be accessed. The second is temporal locality, which states that pages that have been accessed in the near past are likely to be accessed again in the future. The assumption of the presence of these types of locality plays a large role in the caching hierarchies of hardware systems, which deploy many levels of instruction, data, and address caching to help programs run when such locality exists.
Of course, the principle of locality, as it is often called, is no hard-fast rule that all programs must obey. Indeed, some programs access memory (disk) in random fashion and do not exhibit or any locality in their access streams. While locality is a good thing to keep in mind when designing caches of any kind (hardware or software), it does not guarantee success. Rather, it is a heuristic that proves useful in the design of computer systems.
