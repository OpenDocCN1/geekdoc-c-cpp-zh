TIP: COMPARING AGAINST OPTIMAL IS USEFUL
Although optimal is not very practical as a real policy, it is incredibly useful as a comparison point in simulation or other studies. Saying that your fancy new algorithm has a 80% hit rate isn't meaningful in isolation; saying that optimal achieves an 82% hit rate (and thus your approach is quite close to optimal) makes the result more meaningful and gives it context. Then, in any study you perform, knowing what the optimal is lets you perform a better comparison, showing how much improvement is still possible, and when you can stop making your policy better, because it is enough to match the ideal [AD03].

Hopefully, the intuition behind the optimal policy makes sense. Think about it like this: if you have to throw out some page, why not throw out the one that is needed furthest from now? By doing so, you are essentially saying that all the other pages in the cache are more important than the furthest out. The reason for this is simple: you will refer to the other pages before you refer to the one furthest out.
Let's trace through a simple example to understand the decisions of the optimal policy. Assume a program accesses the following stream of virtual pages: 0, 1, 2, 0, 1, 3, 0, 1, 2. Figure 22.1 shows the behavior of optimal, assuming a cache that fits three pages.

In the figure you can see the following actions. Surprisingly, the first three accesses are misses, as the cache begins in an empty state; such a miss is referred to as a cold-start miss (or compulsory miss). We then refer again to pages 0 and 1, which both hit in the cache. Finally, we reach another miss (page 3), but this time the cache is full; a replacement must take place! The optimal policy examines the future for each page we replace? currently in the cache, and see that 0 is accessed almost immediately, and 2 is accessed furthest in the future.
