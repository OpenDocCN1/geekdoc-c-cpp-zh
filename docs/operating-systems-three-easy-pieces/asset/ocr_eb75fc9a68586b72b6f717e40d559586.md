# 8.4 Attempt #3: Better Accounting

We now have one more problem to solve: how to prevent gaming of our scheduler? The real culprit is, as you might have guessed, Rules 4a and 4b, which let job retain its priority by relinquishing the CPU before the time slice expires. So what should we do?

The solution is to perform better accounting of CPU time at each level of the MLFQ. The scheduler should keep track of how much of a time slice is used at its allotment, to demote it to the next queue. Whether it uses the time slice in long bursts or many small ones does not matter. We then rewrite Rules 4a and 4b to the following single rule:

+   * Rule 4: A job uses its allotment at a given level (regardless of how many times it has given up the CPU), its priority is reduced (i.e., it moves down one queue).

Let's look at an example. Figure 8.6 (page 91) shows what happens when a workload tries to game the scheduler with the old Rules 4a and 4b (on the left) as the new anti-gaming Rule 4. Without any protection from gaming, a process can introduce an I/O burst before a time slice ends and dominate CPU time. With protections in place, regardless of the I/O behavior of the process, it slowly moves down the queues, and cannot gain unfair share of the CPU.

# 8.5 Tuning MLFQ and Other Issues

A other issues arise with MLFQ scheduling. One question is how to parameterize such a scheduler. For example, how many queues should there be? How big should the time slice be per queue? How often priority be boosted in order to avoid starvation and account for changes in behavior? There are no easy answers to these questions, and thus only experience with workloads and subsequent tuning of the scheduler will lead to a satisfactory balance.
