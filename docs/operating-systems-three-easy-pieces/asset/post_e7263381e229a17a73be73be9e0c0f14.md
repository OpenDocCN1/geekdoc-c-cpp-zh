Sectors are often skewed like this because when switching from one track to another, the disk needs to reposition the head (even to neighboring tracks). Without such skew, the head would have to move to the next track but the desired next block would have already rotated under the head, and the head would have to wait almost the entire rotational delay to access the next block.

Another reality is that outer tracks tend to have more sectors than inner tracks, which is a result of geometry; these tracks are often referred to as *multi-zoned* disk drives, where the disk is organized into multiple zones, and where a zone contains the number of sectors per track, and outer zones have more sectors than inner zones.

Finally, an important part of any modern disk drive is its *cache*, which is just small amount of memory (usually around 8 or 16 MB) which the drive can use to hold data read from or written to the disk. For example, when reading a sector from the disk, the drive might need to decide to read in all of the sectors on that track and cache them in its memory; doing so allows the drive to quickly respond to any subsequent requests to the same track.

On writes, the drive has a choice: should it acknowledge the write has completed when it has put the data in its memory, or after the write has actually been written to disk? The former is *write back* caching (or sometimes *immediate reporting*), and the latter *write through*. Write back caching sometimes makes the drive “faster”, but can be dangerous; if the file system or applications require that data be written to disk in a certain order for correctness, write-back caching can lead to problems (read the chapter on file-system journaling for details).

## ASIDE: DIMENSIONAL ANALYSIS

Remember in Chemistry class, how you solved virtually every problem by simply setting the units so that they canceled out, and somehow the answers popped out as a result? That chemical magic is known by the highfalutin name of dimensional analysis and it turns out it is useful in computer systems analysis too.

Let's do an example of how dimensional analysis works and why it is useful. In this case, assume you have to figure out how long, in milliseconds, a single rotation of a disk takes. Unfortunately, you are given only the RPM of the disk, or rotations per minute. Let's assume we're talking about a 100 RPM disk (i.e., it rotates 10,000 times per minute). How do we set up the dimensional analysis so we get the time rate in milliseconds?

To do so, we write by starting the desired units on the left; in this case we wish to obtain the time (in milliseconds) per rotation, so that is exactly what we write: making sure to cancel units where possible. First, we obtain 10,000 Rotations (keeping rotation on the bottom, as that is on the left), then transform minutes into seconds with seconds, and then finally transform seconds into milliseconds with milliseconds nicely): The final result is the following (with units nicely):

| Time(ms) | 1 minute | 60 seconds | 1000 ms | 60,000 ms | 6 ms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Rot. | 10,000 Rot. | 1 Rot. | 1 minute | 60 Rot. | Rotation |

As you can see this dimensional analysis makes what seems intuitive into a simple and repeatable process. Beyond the RPM calculation above, it comes handy with I/O analysis regularly. For example, you will often be given the transfer rate of a disk, e.g., 100 MB/second, and then ask: how long does it take to transfer a 512 KB block (in milliseconds?) With dimensional analysis, it's:

| Time(ms) | 512 KB | 1 MB | 1 second | 1000 ms | 5 ms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Request | 1 Request | 1024 KB | 100 MB | 1 second | Request |

## 37.4 I/O: Doing The Math

Now that we have an abstract model of the disk, we can use a little analysis to understand disk performance. In particular, we can represent I/O time as one of the three major components:

```
T_{I/O} = T_{seek} + T_{rotation} + T_{transfer}
```

Note that the rate of I/O ($R_{I/O}$), which is often more easily used for comparison between drives (as we will do below), is easily computed from the time. Simply divide the transfer by the time it took:

> $$R_{I/O} = \frac{Size_{Transfer}}{T_{I/O}}$$

To get a better feel for I/O time, let us perform the calculation. Assume there are two workloads we are interested in. The first, known as the random workload, involves small (e.g., 4KB) reads to random locations on the disk. Random workloads are common in many applications, including database management systems. The second, the sequential workload, simply reads a large number of sectors consecutively without jumping around. Sequential access patterns are common and as important as well.

To understand the difference in performance between random and sequential workloads, we need to make a few assumptions about the disk drive. First, let's look at a couple of modern disks from Seagate. The first, known as the Cheetah 15K.5 [09b], is a high-performance SCSI drive. The second, the Barracuda [09a], is a drive built for capacity. Details on both are found in Figure 37.5.

As you can see, the drives have different characteristics, and in many ways summarize two important components of the disk drive market. The first is the “high performance” drive market, where drives are engineered to spin as fast as possible, deliver low seek times, and transfer data quickly. The second is the “capacity” market, where drives are engineered to pack as much as possible.
