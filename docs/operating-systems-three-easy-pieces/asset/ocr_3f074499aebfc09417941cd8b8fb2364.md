On average, the disk will encounter a half rotation and 2 ms is the average time. Thus, from our equation, $T_{IO}$ for the Cheetah roughly equals 6 ms. To compute the rate of I/O, we divide the size of the transfer by the average time, and thus arrive at $T_{IO}$ for the Cheetah under the workload of about 0.66 MB/s. The same calculation for the Barracuda yields a $T_{IO}$ of about 13.2 ms, not twice as fast, and thus a rate of 0.31 MB/s.

Now let's look the sequential workload. Here we can assume there is a single seek and rotation before a very long transfer. For simplicity, assume the size of the transfer is 100 MB. $T_{IO}$ for the Cheetah and Barracuda is about 800 ms and 95 ms, respectively. The rates of I/O are thus nearly the peak transfer rates of 125 MB/s and 105 MB/s, respectively. Figure 37.6 summarizes these numbers.

| | Cheetah | Barracuda |
|---|---|---|

Figure 37.6: Disk Performance: SCSI Versus SATA

The figure shows us number of important things. First, and most importantly, there is a huge gap in drive performance between random and sequential workloads, almost a factor of 200 or so for the Cheetah and more than a 300 for the Barracuda. And thus we arrive at the most obvious design tip in the history of computing.

A second, most subtle point: there is a large difference in performance between high-end “performance” drives and low-end “capacity” drives. For this reason (and others), people are often willing to pay top dollar for the former while trying to get the latter as cheaply as possible.
