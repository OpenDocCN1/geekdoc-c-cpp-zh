FLASH-BASED SSDs

> TIP: BE CAREFUL WITH TERMINOLOGY
You may have noticed that some terms we have used many times before (blocks, pages) are being used within the context of a flash, or in slightly different ways than before. New terms are not created to make your life harder (although they may be doing just), but arise because there is no central authority where terminology decisions are made. What is a block to you be a page to someone else, and vice versa, depending on the context. Your job is simple: to know the appropriate terms within each domain, and use them so that people well-versed in the discipline can understand what you are talking about. It's one of those times where the only solution is simple but sometimes painful: use your memory.

Of course, there are many details to explain exactly how bit-level storage operate, down to the level of device physics. While beyond the scope of this book, you can read more about it on your [J10].

# 44.2 From Bits to Banks/Planes

As they say in ancient Greece, storing a single bit (or a few) does not have a storage system make. Flash chips are organized into banks or planes which consist of a large number of cells.

A bank is accessed in two different sizes: blocks (sometimes erase blocks), which are of typically 128 KB or 256 KB, and pages, which are each KB in size (e.g., 4KB). In each bank there are a large number of blocks; within each block, there are a large number of pages. When thinking about flash, you must remember this new terminology, which is different from the blocks we refer to in disks and RAIDs and the pages we refer to virtual memory.

Figure 44.1 shows an example of a flash plane with blocks and pages; there are three blocks, each containing four pages, in this simple example. We'll see below how we distinguish blocks and pages; it turns out this distinction is critical for flash operations such as reading and writing, and even more for the overall performance of the device. The most important (and weird) thing you will learn is that write to a page within a block, you first have to erase the entire block; this tricky detail makes building a flash-based SSD an interesting and worthwhile challenge, and subject of the second-half of the chapter.
