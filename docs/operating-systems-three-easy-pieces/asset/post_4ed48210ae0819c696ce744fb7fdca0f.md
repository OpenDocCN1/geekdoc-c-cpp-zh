## Flash-based SSDs

After decades of hard drive dominance, a new form of persistent storage has recently gained significance in the world. Generally referred to as solid-state storage, such devices have no moving parts like hard drives; rather, they are simply built out of transistors, much like memory. However, unlike random-access memory (e.g., DRAM), solid-state storage devices (i.e., SSD) retain information despite power loss, and thus are ideal candidates for use in persistent storage. The technology we focus on is known as flash (specifically NAND flash), which was created by Fujio Masuoka in the 1980s. Flash, as we'll see, has some unique properties. For example, to write a chunk of it (i.e., a page), you first have to erase it (i.e., a block), which can be quite expensive. In addition, writing to a page will cause it to wear out. These two properties make construction of a flash-based SSD an interesting challenge: HOW TO BUILD A FLASH-BASED SSD
How can we build a flash-based SSD? How can we handle the expensive nature of erasing? How can we build a device that lasts given repeated overwrite? Will the progress in technology ever cease?

### 44.1 Storing a Single Bit

Flash chips are designed to store one or more bits in a single transistor; the level of charge trapped within the transistor is mapped to a binary value. In a single-level cell (SLC) flash, only a single bit is stored within a transistor (i.e., 0 or 1); with multi-level cell (MLC) flash, two bits are encoded into different levels of charge, e.g., 00, 01, 10, and 11 are represented by low, medium, and high levels. There is even triple-level cell (TLC) flash, which encodes 3 bits per cell. Overall, SLC chips achieve higher performance and are more expensive.

### 44.2 From Bits to Banks/Planes

As they say in ancient Greece, storing a single bit (or a few) does not make a storage system. Flash chips are organized into banks or planes which consist of a large number of cells.

A bank is accessed in two different sizes: blocks (sometimes erase blocks), which are typically 128 KB or 256 KB, and pages, which are each KB in size (e.g., 4KB). In each bank there are a large number of blocks; within each block, there are a large number of pages. When thinking about flash, you must remember this new terminology, which is different from the blocks we refer to in disks and RAIDs and the pages we refer to in virtual memory.

Figure 44.1 shows an example of a flash plane with blocks and pages; there are three blocks, each containing four pages, in this simple example. We'll see below how we distinguish blocks and pages; it turns out this distinction is critical for flash operations such as reading and writing, and even more for the overall performance of the device. The most important (and weird) thing you will learn is that write to a page within a block, you first have to erase the entire block; this tricky detail makes building a flash-based SSD an interesting and worthwhile challenge, and subject of the second half of the chapter.
