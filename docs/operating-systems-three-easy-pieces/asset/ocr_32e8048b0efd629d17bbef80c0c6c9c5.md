# 44.4 Flash Performance And Reliability

Because we're interested in building a storage device out of raw flash chips, it is worthwhile to note their basic performance characteristics. Figure 44.2 presents a rough summary of some numbers found in the popular press [V12]. There, the author presents the basic operation latency of reads, and across SLC, MLC, and TLC flash, which store 1, 2, and 3 bits per cell, respectively.

As we can see from the table, read latencies are quite good, taking 10s of milliseconds to complete. Program latency is higher and variable, as low as 200 milliseconds for SLC, but higher as you pack more bits into each cell; to get write performance, you will have to make use of multiple flash chips in parallel. Furthermore erases are quite expensive, taking a few milliseconds typically. Dealing with this cost is central to modern flash storage design.

Let's consider reliability of flash chips. Unlike mechanical disks, which can fail for a wide variety of reasons (including the gruesome and physical head crash, where the drive actually makes contact with the recording surface), flash chips are pure silicon and in that sense have fewer reliability issues to worry about. The primary concern is wear when a flash block is erased or programmed, it slowly accumulates a little bit of extra crash. Over time, as extra crash builds up, it becomes increasingly difficult to differentiate between a 0 and a 1. At the point where it becomes impossible, the block becomes unusable.

The lifetime of a block is not well known. Manufacturers rate MLC-based blocks as having a 10,000 P/E (Program/Erase) cycle lifetime; as each block can be erased and programmed 10,000 times before failing. SLC-based chips, because they store only a single bit per transistor, are rated longer. However, recent research has shown that lifetimes are much longer than expected [BD10].

One other reliability problem within flash chips is known as disturbance. When accessing a particular page within a flash, it is possible that some bits get flipped in neighboring pages; such flips are known as read disturbs or program disturbs, depending on whether the page is being read or programmed, respectively.
