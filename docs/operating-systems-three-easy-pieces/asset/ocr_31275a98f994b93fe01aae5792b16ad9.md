# SCHEDULING: INTRODUCTION

FIFO has a number of positive properties: it is clearly simple and thus easy to implement. And, given our assumptions, it works perfectly well.
Let's do a quick example. Imagine three jobs arrive in the system, A, B, and C, at the same time ($T_{arrival} = 0$). Because FIFO has to put some job first, let's assume that they arrived simultaneously, A arrived just a hair before B which arrived just a hair before C. Assume that each job runs for 10 seconds. What will the average turnaround time be for these jobs?

![](bbox=[0.215, 0.275, 0.725, 0.395])

Figure 7.1: FIFO Simple Example

From Figure 7.1, you can see that A finishes at 10, B at 20, and C at 30. Thus, the average turnaround time for the three jobs is simply $rac{10+20+30}{3} = 20$. Computing turnaround time is as easy as that.
Now let's relax one of our assumptions. In particular, lets assumption 1, and thus no longer assume that each job runs for the same amount of time. How does FIFO perform now? Presumably you've figured this out, but just in case, let's do an example to show how jobs of different lengths can lead to trouble for FIFO scheduling. In particular, lets assume three jobs (A, B, and C), but this time A runs for 100 seconds while B and C run for 10.

![](bbox=[0.215, 0.645, 0.725, 0.755])

Figure 7.2: Why FIFO Is Not That Great

As you can see in Figure 7.2, A runs first for the first 100 seconds before B or C even get a chance to run. Thus, the average turnaround time for the system is high: a painful $rac{100+110+120}{3} = 110$.
