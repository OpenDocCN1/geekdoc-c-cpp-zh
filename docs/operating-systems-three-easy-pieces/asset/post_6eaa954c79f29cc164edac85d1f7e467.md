Figure 22.6 plots the results of the experiments for optimal, LRU, Random, and FIFO. The y-axis of the graph shows the rate that each policy achieves; the x-axis varies the cache size as described above.

We can draw a number of conclusions from the graph. First, when there is no locality in the workload, it doesn't matter how realistic policy you are using; LRU, FIFO, and Random perform the same, with the hit rate exactly determined by the size of the cache. Second, when the cache is large enough to fit the entire workload, it also doesn't matter which policy you use; all policies (even Random) converge to a 100% hit rate when the referenced blocks fit in cache. You can see that optimal performs noticeably better than the realistic policies; peaking into the future, it seems possible, a much better job of replacement.

The next example is the "80-20" workload, which exhibits locality: 80% of the references are made to 20% of the pages ("hot" pages); the remaining 20% of the references are to remain the remaining 80% of the pages ("cold" pages). In our workload, there are a total of 100 pages again; "hot" pages are referred to most of the time, and "cold" pages the remainder. Figure 22.7 (page 266) shows how the policies perform with this workload.

As you can see, both Random and FIFO do reasonably well, LRU does better to hold onto the hot pages; as those pages have been referred to frequently in the past, they are likely to be referred to again in the near future. Optimal once again does better, showing that LRU's historical information is not perfect.

You might be noting: is LRU's improvement over Random and FIFO really that big of a deal? The answer, as usual, is, "it depends." If each miss is very costly (not uncommon), then even a small increase in hit rate (reduction in miss rate) can make a huge difference in performance. If misses are not so costly, then of course the benefits possible with LRU are not nearly as important.

Let's look at one final workload. We call this the "looping-sequential workload," as it, we refer to 50 pages in sequence, starting at 0, then 1 ... up to page 49, and then loop, repeating those accesses, for a total of 10,000 accesses to 50 unique pages. The last graph in Figure 22.8 shows the behavior of the policies under this workload.

This common, in many applications (including important commercial applications such as databases [CD85]), represents a worst case for both LRU and FIFO. These algorithms, under a looping-sequential workload, kick out old pages; unfortunately, due to the looping of the workload, these older pages are going to be accessed sooner than the pages that they prefer to keep in cache. Indeed, even with a cache of 49, a looping-sequential workload of 50 pages results in a 0% hit rate. Interestingly, Random fares notably better, not quite approaching optimal, but at least achieving a non-zero hit rate. Turns out that random has some nice properties; one property is not having weird corner-case behaviors.

### 22.7 Implementing Historical Algorithms

As you can see, an algorithm like LRU can generally do a better job than simpler policies like FIFO or Random, which may throw out important pages. Unfortunately, historical policies present us with a new challenge: how do we implement them?

Let's take, for example, LRU. To implement it, we need to do a lot of work. Specifically, upon each page access (i.e., each memory access, whether an instruction fetch or a load or store), we must update some data structure to move this page to the front of the list (i.e., the MRU side). Contrast to FIFO, where the FIFO list of pages is only updated when a page is evicted (by removing the first-in) or when a new page is added to the list (to the last-in side). To keep track of which pages have been least- and most-recently used, the system has to do some accounting on every memory access. Clearly, without great care, such accounting could greatly reduce performance.

One method that could help here is to add a little bit of hardware support. For example, a machine could update, on each page access, a time field in memory (this could be in the per-process page table, or in some separate array in memory, with one entry per physical page of the system). Thus, when a page is accessed, the time field would be set, by hardware, to the current time. Then, when replacing a page, the OS could simply scan all the time fields in the system to find the.

Figure 22.9: The 80-20 Workload With Clock

The behavior of a clock algorithm variant is shown in Figure 22.9. This variant randomly scans pages when doing replacement; when it encounters a page with a reference bit set to 1, it clears the bit (set to 0); when it finds a page with the reference bit set to 0, it chooses it as its victim. As you can see, it doesn't quite as well as perfect LRU, it does better than approaches that don't consider history at all.
