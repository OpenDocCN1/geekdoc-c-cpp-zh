# ASIDE: DIMENSIONAL ANALYSIS

Remember in Chemistry class, how you solved virtually every problem by simply setting the units so that they canceled out, and somehow the answers popped out as a result? That chemical magic is known by the highfalutin name of dimensional analysis and it turns out it is useful in computer systems analysis too.

Let's do an example of how dimensional analysis works and why it is useful. In this case, assume you have to figure out how long, in milliseconds, a single rotation of a disk takes. Unfortunately, you are given only the RPM of the disk, or rotations per minute. Let's assume we're talking about a 100 RPM disk (i.e., it rotates 10,000 times per minute). How do we set up the dimensional analysis so we get the time rate in milliseconds?

To do so, we write by starting the desired units on the left; in this case we wish to obtain the time (in milliseconds) per rotation, so that is exactly what we write: making sure to cancel units where possible. First, we obtain 10,000 Rotations (keeping rotation on the bottom, as that is on the left), then transform minutes into seconds with seconds, and then finally transform seconds into milliseconds with milliseconds nicely): The final result is the following (with units nicely):

| Time(ms) | 1 minute | 60 seconds | 1000 ms | 60,000 ms | 6 ms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Rot. | 10,000 Rot. | 1 Rot. | 1 minute | 60 Rot. | Rotation |

As you can see this dimensional analysis makes what seems intuitive into a simple and repeatable process. Beyond the RPM calculation above, it comes handy with I/O analysis regularly. For example, you will often be given the transfer rate of a disk, e.g., 100 MB/second, and then ask: how long does it take to transfer a 512 KB block (in milliseconds?) With dimensional analysis, it's:

| Time(ms) | 512 KB | 1 MB | 1 second | 1000 ms | 5 ms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Request | 1 Request | 1024 KB | 100 MB | 1 second | Request |
