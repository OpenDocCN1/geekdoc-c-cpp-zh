You may also know why this type of lock is usually referred to as a spin lock. It is the simplest type of lock to build, and simply spins, using CPU cycles, until the lock becomes available. To work correctly on a single processor, it requires a preemptive scheduler (i.e., one that will interrupt a thread via a timer, in order to run a different thread, from time to time). Without preemption, spin locks don't make much sense on a single CPU, as spinning on a CPU will never relinquish it.

# 28.8 Evaluating Spin Locks

Given our basic spin, we can now evaluate how effective it is along our previously described axes. The most important aspect of a lock is correctness: does it provide mutual exclusion? The answer here is yes: the lock only allows a single thread to enter the critical section at a time. Thus, we have a correct lock.

The next axis is fairness. How fair is a spin lock to a waiting thread? Can you guarantee that a waiting thread will ever enter the critical section? The answer here, unfortunately, is bad news: spin locks don't provide any guarantees. Indeed, a thread spinning may spin forever, under contention. Simple spin locks (as discussed thus) are not fair and may lead to starvation.

The final axis is performance. What are the costs of using a spin lock? To analyze this correctly, we suggest thinking about a few different cases. In the first, imagine threads competing for the lock on a single processor; in the second, consider threads spread out across multiple CPUs.

For spin locks, imagine the case where the thread holding the lock could be painful; if preempted within a critical section. The scheduler might then run every other thread (imagine there are N-1 others), each of which tries to acquire the lock. In this case, each of those threads will spin for the duration of a time slice before giving up the CPU, a waste of CPU cycles.
