# 36.1 System Architecture

To begin our discussion, let's look at a “classical” diagram of a typical system like this (Figure 36.1, page 444). The picture shows a single CPU attached to the main memory via some kind of **bus** or interconnect. Some devices are connected to the system via a general **I/O bus**, which in many modern systems would be **PCI** (or one of its many derivatives); graphics and some higher-performance I/O devices or more of what we call a **peripheral bus**, such as **SCSI, SATA, or USB**. These connect devices to the system, including **disks, mice,** and **keyboards**.

One question you might ask: why do we need a hierarchical structure like this? Simply: physics and cost. The faster a bus is, the shorter it must be; high-performance bus does not have much room to plug in devices. In addition, engineering a bus for high performance is costly. Thus, system designers have adopted this hierarchical approach, where components that demand high performance (such as the graphics card) are nearer the CPU. Lower-performance components are further away. The benefits of placing disks and other devices on a peripheral bus are manifold; in particular, we can place a large number of devices on it.
