# 7.7 Round Robin

To solve this problem, we will introduce a new scheduling algorithm, classically referred to as Round-Robin (RR) scheduling [K64]. The basic idea is simple: instead of running jobs to completion, RR runs a job for a time slice (called a scheduling quantum) and then switches to the next job in the run queue. It repeats until the jobs are finished. For this, RR is sometimes called time-slicing. Note that the length of a time slice must be a multiple of the timer-interrupt period; thus if the timer interrupts every 10 milliseconds, the time slice could be 10, 20, or any other multiple of 10 ms.

> TIP: AMORTIZATION CAN REDUCE COSTS
The general technique of amortization is commonly used in systems when there is a fixed cost to some operation. By incurring that cost less often (i.e., by performing the operation fewer times), the total cost to the system is reduced. For example, if the time slice is 10 ms, 10% of time is spent switching. If we want to amortize this cost, we can increase the time slice, e.g., to 100 ms. In this case, less than 1% of time is spent context switching, and time-slicing is amortized.

As you can see, the length of the time slice is critical for RR. The shorter the time slice, the better the performance of RR under the response-time metric. However, making the time slice too small is problematic: suddenly the cost of switching will dominate overall performance.

Note that the cost of context switching does not arise from the OS actions of saving and restoring a few registers. When programs run, they build up a set of CPU caches, TLBs, branch predictors, and other on-chip hardware. Switching to another job causes this cost to be brought in, which may make a noticeable performance cost [MB91].

RR, with a reasonable time slice, is thus an excellent scheduler if response time is our only metric. But what about our friend turnaround time? Let's say A, B, and C each with running 5 seconds, arrive at the same time and RR is the scheduler with a (long) 1-second time slice. We can see from the picture above that A finishes at 13, B at 14, and C at 15, for an average of 14. Perfectly awful!

It is not surprising that RR is indeed one of the worst policies for turnaround time. Intuitively makes sense: what RR is doing is stretching each job as long as it can, by only running each job for a short time before moving to the next. Because turnaround time cares about when jobs finish, RR is nearly dismal, even worse than simple FIFO in.

More generally, any policy (such as fair, i.e., that evenly divides the CPUs among active processes on a small time scale, will perform poorly on metrics such as turnaround time.
