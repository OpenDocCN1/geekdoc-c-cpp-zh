
# Back To RAID-0 Analysis

Let us now evaluate the capacity, reliability, and performance of striping. From the perspective of capacity, it is perfect: given $N$ disks of each size $B$ blocks, striping deletes $N-B$ blocks of useful capacity. From the standpoint of reliability, striping is also perfect, but in the bad way: any disk failure will lead to data loss. Finally, performance is excellent: all disks are utilized, often in parallel, to service user I/O requests.

# Evaluating RAID Performance

In analyzing RAID performance, one can consider different performance metrics. The first is single-request latency. Understanding the latency of a single I/O request to a RAID is useful as it reveals how much parallelism exists during a single logical I/O operation. The second is steady-state throughput of the RAID, i.e., the total bandwidth of concurrent requests. Because RAIDs are used in high-performance environments, the steady-state bandwidth is critical, and thus will be the main focus of our analysis.

To understand throughput in more detail, we need to put forth some workloads of interest. We will assume for this discussion, that there are two types of workloads: **sequential** and **random**. With a sequential workload, we assume that the requests to carry the array in contiguous chunks; for example, a request of 1 MB of data, starting at block $x$ and ending at block $(x+1)$, would be deemed sequential. Sequential workloads are common in many environments (think of searching through a large file for a keyword), and thus are considered important.

For random workloads, we assume that each request is smaller, and that each request is a different random location on disk. For example, a random stream of requests may first access 4KB at logical address 10, then at logical address 550,000, then at 20,100, and so forth. Some workloads, as transactional workloads on a database management system (DBMS), exhibit this type of access pattern, and thus is considered an important workload.

Of course, real workloads are not so simple, and often have a mix of sequential and random-seeking components as well as behaviors between the two. For simplicity, we just consider these two possibilities.

As you tell, sequential and random workloads will result in different performance characteristics from a disk. With sequential access, a disk operates in its most efficient mode, spending little time seeking and waiting for rotation and most of its time transferring data. With random access, the opposite is true: most time is spent seeking and waiting for rotation and relatively little time spent transferring data. To capture this difference in our analysis, we will assume that a disk can transfer data at $S$ MB/s under a sequential workload, and $R$ MB/s under a random workload. In general, $S$ is much greater than $R$ (i.e., $S \gg R$).

# REDUNDANT ARRAYS OF INEXPENSIVE DISKS (RAIDS)

To make sure we understand this difference, let's do a simple exercise. Specifically, let's calculate $S$ and $R$ given the following disk characteristics. Assume a sequential transfer of 10 MB on average, and a random transfer of 10 KB on average. Also, assume the following disk characteristics:

| Average seek time | 7 ms |
| :--- | :--- |
| Average rotational delay of disk | 3 ms |
| Transfer rate of disk | 50 MB/s |

To compute $S$, we need to figure out how much time is spent in a 10 MB transfer. First, we spend 7 ms seeking, and then 3 ms rotating. Finally, we spend 10 MB @ 50 MB/s to 1/5th of a second, or 200 ms, spent in transfer. Thus, for each 10 MB request, we spend 210 ms completing the request. To compute $S$, we just divide:

```
$S = \frac{\text{Amount of Data}}{\text{Time to access}} = \frac{10\text{ MB}}{210\text{ ms}} \approx 47.6\text{ MB/s}$
```

For random workloads, the transfer is much smaller. We spend 7 ms seeking, and then 3 ms rotating. Finally, we spend 10 KB @ 50 MB/s to 1/5000th of a second, or 0.2 ms, spent in transfer. Thus, for each request, we spend 7.2 ms completing the request. To compute $R$, we just divide:

```
$R = \frac{\text{Amount of Data}}{\text{Time to access}} = \frac{10\text{ KB}}{7.2\text{ ms}} \approx 1.39\text{ MB/s}$
```

As we see, $S$ is much greater than $R$.

# RAID-1 Analysis

Let us assess RAID-1. From a capacity standpoint, RAID-1 is expensive; with the mirroring level of 2, we only obtain half of our capacity. With $N$ disks of $B$ blocks, RAID-1 useful capacity is $(N \cdot B)/2$.

From a reliability standpoint, RAID-1 does well. It can tolerate the failure of any one disk. You may notice RAID-1 can actually do better than this, with a lucky charm. In the figure above, that disk 0 and disk 2 failed. In such a situation, there is no data loss! More generally, a mirrored system (with mirroring level of 2) can tolerate 1 disk failure up to N/2 failures depending on which disk fails. In practice, we generally don't like leaving things like this to.

Finally, we analyze. From the perspective of the latency of a single request, the read latency is the same as the latest on a single disk, as RAID-1 is direct to read either one of its copies. A write is a different: it requires two physical writes to complete before it is done. These two writers happen in parallel, and thus the time will be roughly equivalent to the time of a single write; however, because the logical write must wait for both writes to complete, it suffers the worst-case seek and rotational delay of the two requests, and thus (on average) be slightly higher than a write to a single disk.
