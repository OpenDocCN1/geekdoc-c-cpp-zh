where TM represents the cost of accessing memory, $T_D$ the cost of accessing disk, and $P_{Miss}$ the probability of not finding the data in the cache (a miss); $P_{Miss}$ varies from 0.0 to 1.0, and we refer to a percent miss rate instead of a probability (e.g., a 10% miss rate means $P_{Miss} = 0.10$). Note you pay the cost of accessing memory; when you miss, however, you must additionally pay the cost of fetching from disk.

For example, let us imagine a machine with a (tiny) address space: 4KB, with 256-byte pages. Thus, a virtual address has two components: a 4-bit VPN (the most-significant bits) and an 8-bit offset (the least-significant bits). Thus, in this process the acquire generates memory references ($2^4$ or 16 total virtual pages. i.e., virtual addresses: 0x000, 0x100, 0x200, 0x400, 0x500, 0x600, 0x700, 0x800). These virtual addresses refer to the first byte of each of the first pages of the address space (the page number being the first digit of virtual address)

Let us assume that every virtual page is in memory. Then, our acquire of memory references will encounter the following behavior: hit, hit, miss, hit, hit, hit, hit, hit. We compute the hit rate (the percent of references found in memory): 90%, as 9 out of 10 references are in memory. The miss rate is then 10% ($P_{Miss} = 0.1$). In general, the hit rate may sum to 100%.

To calculate AMAT, we need to know the cost of accessing memory and the cost of accessing disk. Assuming the cost of accessing memory ($T_M$) is around 100 nanoseconds, and the cost of accessing disk ($T_D$) is around 10 milliseconds, we have the following AMAT: $100ns + 0.1 \cdot 10ms$, which is $100ns + 1ms$, or 1 millisecond. If our hit rate had 99.9% ($P_{Miss} = 0.001$), the result is quite different: AMAT is 10.1 microseconds, or 100 times faster. As the hit rate approaches 100%, AMAT approaches 100 nanoseconds.

Unfortunately, as you can see in this example, the cost of disk access is so high in modern systems that even a tiny miss rate will quickly overwhelm the AMAT of running programs. Clearly, we need to avoid as many misses as possible or run slowly the disk. One way to help with this is to carefully design a smart policy, as we do now.

# 22.2 The Optimal Replacement Policy

To understand how a particular replacement policy works, it would be nice to compare it to the best possible replacement policy. As it turns out, an **optimal** policy was developed by Belady many years ago [B66] (which he called MIN). The optimal replacement policy leads to the fewest number of misses overall. Belady showed that a simple (but, unfortunately, difficult to implement!) approach that replaces the page that will be *furthest* in the future is the optimal policy, resulting in the fewest-possible cache misses.
