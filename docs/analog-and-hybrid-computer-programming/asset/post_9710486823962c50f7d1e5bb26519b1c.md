
![](img/91599d88757ed8c75960eb83f4f85a12_187_0.png)

*Fig. 6.76. Indirect approach to solving a system of linear equations on an analog computer*

A clever solution to this difficulty is to transform the original problem \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) into a corresponding system of linear equations with a coefficient matrix that is always positive definite.[114] This could be easily achieved by multiplying (6.62) by the transposed matrix \(\mathbf{A}^T\) from the left yielding

\[
\mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{b}. \tag{6.68}
\]

The matrix \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{A}\) is positive definite and Hermitian[115] so that this modified system of linear equations can be solved by the approach shown above. A direct approach might convert (6.62) into (6.68) by means of a preliminary step performed on a stored-program digital computer.

![](img/91599d88757ed8c75960eb83f4f85a12_188_0.png)

*Fig. 6.77. Divergent behaviour of the computer setup shown in figure 6.76*

Nevertheless, a purely analog implementation of this approach can be achieved by computing the error resulting from an initial setting of \(\mathbf{x}\) by

\[
\mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{b} = \tilde{\zeta}
\]

and setting

\[
\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}} = -\tilde{\zeta}
\]

as before. Factoring out \(\mathbf{A}^T\) yields

\[
\mathbf{A}^T(\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{b}) = \tilde{\zeta} = -\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}.
\]

This can now be split into two sets of coupled equations:

\[
\varepsilon_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}x_j - b_i \qquad (6.69)
\]

\[
\dot{x}_i = - \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ji}\varepsilon_i \qquad (6.70)
\]

with \(1 \le i \le n\). These equations can now be transformed into an analog computer setup as shown in figure 6.78. Compared with the simple program shown in figure 6.75, this one requires twice the number of coefficient potentiometers (note the transposed indices!) and twice the number of summers. This seemingly profligate use of computing elements is nevertheless justified given the very good convergence behaviour of this setup.

## 6.25 Inverted pendulum

This section describes the simulation of an inverted pendulum, i.e. a pendulum that has its mass above its pivot point which is mounted on a cart capable of moving horizontally. Figure 6.87 shows the configuration.

![](img/91599d88757ed8c75960eb83f4f85a12_194_0.png)

*Fig. 6.87. Configuration of the inverted pendulum*

The Lagrangian \(L = T - V\) is used where \(T\), as usual, represents the total kinetic energy while \(V\) is the potential energy of the system.[122] The total potential energy is

\[
V = mgl\cos(\varphi), \tag{6.72}
\]

with \(g\) representing the gravitational acceleration. The total kinetic energy is the sum of the kinetic energies of the pendulum bob having mass \(m\) and the moving cart with mass \(M\), i.e.

\[
T = \frac{1}{2}\left(Mv_c^2 + mv_p^2\right). \tag{6.73}
\]

where \(v_c\) is the velocity of the cart, and \(v_p\) is the velocity of the pendulum bob. Obviously,

\[
v_c = \dot{x} \tag{6.74}
\]

and (a bit less obviously)

\[
v_p = \sqrt{\left(\frac{d}{dt}(x-l\sin(\varphi))\right)^2 + \left(\frac{d}{dt}(l\cos(\varphi))\right)^2}
\]

The left term under the square root is

\[
(\dot{x}-l\dot{\varphi}\cos(\varphi))^2 = \dot{x}^{\,2} - 2\dot{x}l\dot{\varphi}\cos(\varphi) + l^2\dot{\varphi}^{\,2}\cos^2(\varphi)
\]

> [122] The following derivation follows basically https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverted_pendulum, retrieved March 29th, 2019.

![](img/91599d88757ed8c75960eb83f4f85a12_198_0.png)

*Fig. 6.90. Display of the pendulum mounted on its cart*

To generate a flicker-free display, an amplitude-stabilized quadrature signal pair \(\sin(\omega t)\) and \(\cos(\omega t)\) of some kHz is required; this can be derived from the computer setup shown in figure 4.11 or 4.13 in section 4.2. This signal is used to draw a circular or elliptical cart as well as the rotating pendulum rod mounted on the cart.

The length of the rod is set by the potentiometer labelled \(l\). Since the pendulum rod has one of its ends fixed at the center of the cart figure, the \(\sin(\omega t)\)-signal has to be shifted by an amount \(r\). The actual rotation is done by multiplication with \(\sin(\varphi)\) and \(\cos(\varphi)\). The circuit yields two output signal-pairs: \(P_x\) and \(P_y\) are used to draw the pendulum rod while \(C_x\) and \(C_y\) display the moving cart. Therefore, an oscilloscope with two independent (multiplexed) \(x,y\)-inputs is required for this simulation, if both, cart and pendulum, are to be displayed. A screenshot of a typical resulting display with the pendulum just tipping over is shown in figure 6.91.

What happens when the mass of the pendulum bob is no longer negligible? In this case equation (6.77) can no longer be simplified as before but must be fully taken into account to implement the influence of the swinging pendulum on the cart. Solving this equation for \(\ddot{x}\) yields

\[
\ddot{x} = \frac{1}{M + m} \left( F + m l \ddot{\varphi} \cos(\varphi) - m l \dot{\varphi}^2 \sin(\varphi) \right)
\]

which can be easily transformed into a computer setup as shown in figure 6.92.

## 6.26 Double pendulum

Even more complicated but also more mesmerizing is the simulation on an analog computer of the double pendulum arrangement shown in figure 6.93.[125]

The equations of motion will be derived as before by determining the Lagrangian \(L = T - V\) with the two generalized coordinates \(\varphi_1\) and \(\varphi_2\). \(T\) represents the total kinetic energy while \(V\) is the potential energy of the system. This potential energy is

\[
V=-g\left((m_1+m_2)l_1\cos(\varphi_1)+m_2l_2\cos(\varphi_2)\right) \tag{6.79}
\]

with \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) representing the masses of the two bobs mounted on the tips of the two pendulum rods (which are assumed as being weightless). As always, \(g\) represents the gravitational acceleration.

> [125] This has also been done by [Mahrenholtz 1968, pp. 159–165] which served as an inspiration for this application note, especially for the display circuit.
