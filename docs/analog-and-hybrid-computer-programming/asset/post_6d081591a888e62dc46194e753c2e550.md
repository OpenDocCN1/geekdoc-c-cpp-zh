## 6.7 Charged particle in a magnetic field

This example uses an analog computer to simulate the path of a charged particle traversing a magnetic field.[88] Basically, a particle with charge \(q\) moving in a magnetic field \(\vec{B}\) is subjected to a force \(\vec{F}_{\mathrm{Lorentz}}\) which is perpendicular to both the magnetic field \(\vec{B}\) and the direction in which the particle moves, yielding
\[
\vec{F}_{\mathrm{Lorentz}} = q\left(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}\right) = q\left(\dot{\vec{r}} \times \vec{B}\right).
\]

\(\vec{v}\) denotes the velocity and \(\vec{r}\) the position of the particle under consideration. Also, \(\vec{F}_{\mathrm{particle}} = m\ddot{\vec{r}}\) holds. In addition to this, some form of friction exerting a force \(\vec{F}_{\mathrm{friction}} = -\mu\dot{\vec{r}}\) is assumed to act on the particle.

Combining these three equations finally yields
\[
m\ddot{\vec{r}} = \vec{F}_{\mathrm{Lorentz}} + \vec{F}_{\mathrm{friction}} = q\left(\dot{\vec{r}} \times \vec{B}\right) - \mu\dot{\vec{r}}. \tag{6.20}
\]

## 6.8 Rutherford scattering

In the early 20th century Ernest Rutherford performed a seminal experiment in which he bombarded a thin gold foil with \(\alpha\)-particles.[89] He observed that the vast majority of these particles just went through the gold foil without being deflected or absorbed. Nevertheless, some of these particles were deflected through large angles with even fewer \(\alpha\)-particles being reflected by the gold foil, a phenomenon called Rutherford scattering. This led to the planetary Rutherford model of the atom, which then became the basis of the Bohr model.

The following analog computer simulation was motivated by [BORCHARDT]. It is assumed that the gold atom’s nucleus is at a fixed position — specifically the point of origin of the underlying coordinate system to simplify the distance calculation for the \(\alpha\)-particle. With the \(\alpha\)-particle’s position being \((x, y)\), its distance to the gold nucleus is then
\[
r^2 = x^2 + y^2.
\]

89. These are helium nuclei and thus rather large.

The force exerted on the \(\alpha\)-particle is described by Coulomb’s law
\[
F = k_e \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \tag{6.26}
\]
where \(k_e = 8.9875 \cdot 10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\) is Coulomb’s constant and \(q_1, q_2\) are the magnitudes of the charges involved. Since the following simulation will be a qualitative one, all of these constants, including masses, can be gathered together in a new variable \(\gamma\).

The force \(F\) acts proportionally to \(\cos(\varphi)\) and \(\sin(\varphi)\) on \(x\) and \(y\), with
\[
\cos(\varphi) = \frac{x}{r} \quad\text{and}\quad \sin(\varphi) = \frac{y}{r}.
\]

Together with (6.26) and \(\gamma\), this yields the following two coupled differential equations which govern the path of the \(\alpha\)-particle:
\[
\ddot{x} = \gamma \frac{x}{r^3},\qquad
\ddot{y} = \gamma \frac{y}{r^3}.
\]

The setup for this problem, which is pretty straightforward, is shown in figure 6.27. It should be noted that there is an explicit additional feedback path for the summer adding \(x^2\) and \(y^2\), which gives a scaling factor of \(1/2\). Accordingly, the resulting sum satisfies \(0 \le r_s \le 1\), so no overload condition can occur here. As mentioned above, all constants, including this factor \(1/2\), are gathered into the parameter \(\gamma_s\). A typical value for this parameter — yielding nice scattering behaviour — is \(\approx 0.01\).

The only other free parameter is the initial height of the \(\alpha\)-particle’s trajectory \(y_0\). This can be either set manually by means of a free potentiometer connected to \(+1\) and \(-1\) or automatically as shown in figure 6.28 by means of a triangle wave generator consisting of an integrator working in conjunction with two comparators.

To run this program, the analog computer is set to repetitive operation with an operation time \(t_{\mathrm{OP}} = 30\ \mathrm{ms}\). The integrator of the triangle wave generator must be disconnected from the central timing control by patching its Mode C-input to \(+1\) (only the third or fourth integrator of an INT4 module can be used for that purpose), so that it will not be reset between two successive computing runs.

The picture shown in figure 6.29 was obtained with time constants \(k_I = 100\) on the four integrators in figure 6.27 and \(k_0 = 1\) in the triangle-wave generator. The camera was set to ISO 100 with an exposure time of 8 seconds.

## 6.9 Celestial mechanics

Simulating (or determining) the movement of three (celestial) bodies with given parameters, such as masses, gravitational constant, initial conditions for the bodies’ coordinates and velocities, is known as the *three-body problem*.[90] The first one to tackle this problem was Sir Isaac Newton in the first book, section XI, of his *Principia*, which was considered “the most valuable chapter that was ever written on physical science” according to Sir George Biddell Airy.[91]

Although an analytical solution is possible in the simpler case of two bodies, Joseph-Louis Lagrange was able to give some particular solutions for the much more difficult three-body problem. Since these are beyond the scope of this section, the reader is referred to the standard texts [BATTIN 1999, pp. 365 ff.] and [MOULTON 1923, p. 363].

90. This is a special case of the *n*-body problem which will not be investigated further here.
