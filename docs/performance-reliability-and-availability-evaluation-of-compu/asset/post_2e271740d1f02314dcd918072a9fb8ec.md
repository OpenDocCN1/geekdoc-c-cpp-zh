## 26 Fault Injection and Failure Monitoring

Dependable systems are designed to cope with faults through mechanisms that constrain errors propagation and avoid system failures. Therefore, obtaining statistical data from the operational environment after implementing the system or from an equivalent system already operating on the field may not be a suitable alternative to the time required to obtain such data.

Indeed, fault injection has two aims: system validation and system evaluation. The first objective concerns comparing the specified behavior with the implemented system’s behavior and gaining confidence if the system behaves as expected in the presence of faults [32, 70]. The second objective aims to check the implemented system copes with faults appropriate as specified. In other words, fault methods provide means for measuring the impact of faults in the systems’ functions and performance for evaluating effective fault-tolerant mechanisms on models, working prototypes, and systems in the field. Hence, fault injection tools provide means to improve the confidence in the systems’ ability to deliver the proper service [20, 109].

Already by 1972 fault injection was adopted to test the Self-Testing And Repair computer (JPL-STAR) [28]. A major concern when adopting fault injection for a system is to ensure the generated faults represent actual faults to obtain meaningful results [251]. Fault injection methods range, from voltage glitches to electromagnetic interference, impacting light attack, heavy-ion radiation, and injected faults [60, 70, 82, 135, 217, 231, 262, 266, 300, 399, 408].

This chapter aims to characterize some important fault injection procedures and then introduce the concept of fault acceleration and fault acceleration models. Later, some noteworthy fault injection tools are briefly described, and finally, we examine a generic architecture of a software-based fault injection tool.

The faults may be applied in an existing system or functional prototype or in a model [85, 181]. This classification takes into account two perspectives: 1) the target to which faults are injected or 2) the implementation mechanism. The aim target could be: a) specifications (models) or b) systems (actual systems or system-functional prototypes). The implementation mechanism may be divided as a) hardware-based, b) software-based, and c) hardware-based fault injection mechanisms. Hardware-implemented fault injection is accomplished by disturbing the hardware with fluctuations in the environment parameters’ values. Such variations may be produced by heavy-ion radiation, electromagnetic interference, voltage glitches at the system’s specific points, power supply disturbances, luminous exposures, etc. Hardware fault injection mechanisms are only applied to existing systems or functional hardware prototypes. Software-based fault injection mechanisms, on the other hand, may be employed i) to inject actual software faults in a system, functional prototype, ii) to emulate hardware faults on existing systems or functional prototypes, iii) to simulate hardware and software faults in the models. Hybrid strategies combine two or more fault injection techniques to exercise the system under analysis. For example, employing hardware and fault injection techniques may improve the model's validation process and verify how effective a fault-tolerant mechanism is by joining the versatility of software fault injection and the accuracy of hardware monitoring. Fault injection may also be classified as **invasive** or **non-invasive** methods. For example, optical discharge such as laser-shooting on a particular hardware module may require depacking the device to direct the laser beam to the device's surface. In such a case, clear evidence of depacking and packing are likely to be unavoidable. On the other hand, lowering the power voltage or increasing the hardware module's clock rate may not require depacking the module; thus, it does not damage the device copying. Software fault may also be injected by changing variables or memory location's value or might be emulated by disabling communication interfaces between systems' components and the environment.

## 26.1 FAULT ACCELERATION

The reliability of physical devices is greatly subject to the working environment. Consequently, testing such devices should take the environmental factors into account. The testing times are usually considerably shorter than the devices' expected lifetimes. Environmental stress promotes the device's system to hasten faults that are presumed to evolve to failures during the operational phase regarding the intended working environment attributes [60, 115, 160, 204, 269, 279, 337, 429].

### Fault Injection and Failure Monitoring

There are numerous accelerated stress models. Most of these models are either statistical or physics-based models [124, 269, 469].

Environmental stresses are generally related to (1) natural stress, circumscribed by the geographic area, meteorological conditions, and (2) induced stress situations. The latter may be directly or indirectly induced to mimic stress exposures due to environmental conditions. These environmental stimuli bait the creation of harsh circumstances that lead to changes in failure or lifetime under a normal state. The most common scenarios of interest are that represent failure factors. For example, if corrosion failures occur at typical temperatures and humidities, then identical correlation is likely to arise much faster in a humid laboratory at high temperatures. The testing activity that accelerates the fault phenomenon is called fault acceleration. It is worth noting that physical models may be appealing choices for representing well-known failure phenomena. However, the relations between accelerating factors and the actual failure are usually complex. In practical cases, simpler models may suffice when plausible constraints are adopted. On the other hand, empirical models may be an attractive modeling approach when the chemical or physical failing processes are not well known. However, the analyst should be careful when adopting models for extrapolation.

Scaling factors (usually called acceleration factors AF) or its inverse, time scaling factors (usually called CF = 1/AF), are functions that assign to faults' events which represent primary parameters to accelerated life testing. The acceleration factors speed up systems' or devices' degradation and allow one to estimate the failure rate - λ - (or the time to failure - TTF) under stress circumstances due to temperature, voltage, humidity, mechanical wear-out, memory leaking, etc. A suitable choice of such factors, AF or CF, supports extrapolating the failure rate (or the time to failure) to a given specific operation. The acceleration factor is defined as the ratio between the average failure rate at state condition 1 - stress (λ₁ or λ₂) and the average failure rate at state condition 2 - possibly normal operation condition (λ₂ or λ₀); hence

$$AF = \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} = \frac{\lambda_s}{\lambda_o}; \quad (26.1.1)$$

which is equivalent to

$$AF = \frac{TTF_1}{TTF_2} = \frac{TTF_o}{TTF_s}, \quad (26.1.2)$$

where TTF₁ (TTFo) and TTF₂ (TTFs) are time to failure at condition 1 (possibly normal operation condition) and the time to failure at condition 2 (stress), respectively.
