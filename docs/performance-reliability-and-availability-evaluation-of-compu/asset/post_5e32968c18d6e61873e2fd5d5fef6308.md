# Confidence Interval for the Mean and Rate of Exponentially Distributed Data

As the exponential distribution plays a very important role in performance and reliability modeling, this section presents a parametric method for estimating the parameter and its expected value. Assume a random variable $T$ follows an exponential distribution with parameter $\lambda$; thus its density function is $f_T(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}$. Also consider the ordered failure time $\{t_1, t_2, ... t_n\}$ and $Y = t_1 + t_2 + ... + t_n$, where each $t_i$ is a time value assigned to a random variable $T_i$, where every $T_i$ is independent and identically distributed according to $f_T(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}$. Therefore $Y = \sum_{i=1}^n T_i$ is a gamma distribution with parameters $n$ and $\lambda$. The second parameter of the gamma distribution is a rate; not then, multiplying $Y$ by a constant changes the same shape and rate by that constant [207, 254, 461]. We choose to multiply by $\lambda$;

$$$$W = 2\lambda Y$$

and

$$$$W = 2\lambda (T_1 + T_2 + ... + T_n). \quad (25.3.41)$$

Thus,

$$$$W = 2n\lambda\overline{T}, \quad (25.3.42)$$

where $\overline{T} = (\sum_{i=1}^n T_i)/n$. The random variable $T$ is distributed according to $Gamma(n, \lambda)$. Hence, the random variable $W = 2n\lambda\overline{T}$ is distributed according to $Gamma(n, \lambda/(2\lambda)) = Gamma(n, 1/2),$ that is

$$
W = 2n\lambda\overline{T} \sim Gamma(n, \frac{\lambda}{2\lambda})$$
$$W = 2n\lambda\overline{T} \sim Gamma(n, \frac{1}{2}).$$
$$

Since

$$$$Gamma(n, \frac{1}{2}) = \chi^2_{2n}.$$

then,

$$$$W = 2n\lambda\overline{T} \sim \chi^2_{2n}. \quad (25.3.43)$$

Therefore, the confidence interval for $\lambda$ can be estimated by

$P(\chi_{2n,\alpha/2}^2 < W < \chi_{2n,1-(\alpha/2)}^2) = 1 - \alpha.$

$P(\chi_{2n,\alpha/2}^2 < 2n\bar{T} < \chi_{2n,1-(\alpha/2)}^2) = 1 - \alpha.$

Hence the confidence interval for $\lambda$ is estimated by

$\frac{\chi_{2n,\alpha/2}^2}{2n\bar{T}} < \lambda < \frac{\chi_{2n,1-(\alpha/2)}^2}{2n\bar{T}} \quad (25.3.44)$ considering $1 - \alpha$ degree of confidence.

As the expected value of $W$ ($MTTF$) = $E(W) = 1/\lambda$, then the confidence interval for the mean is estimated by

$\frac{2n\bar{T}}{\chi_{2n,1-(\alpha/2)}^2} < MTTF < \frac{2n\bar{T}}{\chi_{2n,\alpha/2}} \quad (25.3.45)$

Consider the experiment when $k$ out of $n$ devices fail, that is, right-censoring-type data the respective confidence intervals can be estimated by the above equations, but $n\bar{T}$ should be changed by $n\bar{T} = \sum_{i=1}^k T_i + (n-k)T_k$, so

$\frac{\chi_{2n,\alpha/2}^2}{2(\sum_{i=1}^k T_i + (n-k)T_k)} < \lambda < \frac{\chi_{2n,1-(\alpha/2)}^2}{2(\sum_{i=1}^k T_i + (n-k)T_k)} \quad (25.3.46)$

and

$\frac{\sum_{i=1}^k T_i + (n-k)T_k}{\chi_{2n,1-(\alpha/2)}^2} < MTTF < \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k T_i + (n-k)T_k}{\chi_{2n,\alpha/2}^2} \quad (25.3.47)$ where $T_k$ is the time of failure of the $k$ device. $1-\alpha$ is the degree of confidence. These expressions may also be adopted to provide a reasonable estimate for right-censoring-type data. In such a case, $n\bar{T}$ is changed by $n\bar{T} = \sum_{i=1}^k T_i + (n-k)T_k$, where $T_j$ is the closest, smaller or equal, to $T_k$, where $T_k$ is the duration of the experiment.

$\frac{\chi_{2n,\alpha/2}^2}{2(\sum_{i=1}^j T_i + (n-j)T_k)} < \lambda < \frac{\chi_{2n,1-(\alpha/2)}^2}{2(\sum_{i=1}^j T_i + (n-j)T_k)} \quad (25.3.48)$
