**17.12 RAID 5 (a) and RAID 6 (b).

storage capacity efficiency is 0.5. However, for RAID, the fault tolerance is different. Consider, for an instance RAID 10 with four disks. This system is composed of two groups of disks, $G_1$ and $G_2$. $G_1$ is composed of Disk 1 and Disk 2, whereas $G_2$ is composed of Disk 3 and Disk 4. Two blocks of data, $A_1$ and $A_2$, are stored in them. In RAID 10, when writing these blocks of data, first $A_1$ is written in Disk 1; then it is mirrored in Disk 2. After, the other ($A_2$) is written (stripping) in Disk 3 and mirrored in Disk 4. $A_1$ is stored in Disk 1 AND Disk 2, and $A_2$ is stored in Disk 3 AND Disk 4. If, for instance, Disk 2 fails, the system still suffers a failure in Disk 3 or Disk 4, $A_1$ is in Disk 1. However, if Disk 1 fails, let us assume a RAID 01 system composed of two groups of disks also known as $G_1$ and $G_2$. When the data, $A_1$ and $A_2$, are stored in this system, we have the following: first, $A_1$ and $A_2$ are written to Disk 1 AND Disk 2 (stripping); respectively; then $A_1$ and $A_2$ are mirrored in Disk 3 AND Disk 4. If Disk 2 fails, the system neither allows a failure in Disk 3 nor in Disk 4. Chapter 18 discusses these aspects in detail.

RAID 50 is implemented by striping data (RAID 0) across two 5 arrays (see Figure 17.14.b). RAID 5 requires at least 6 disks, and tolerates to one disk failure in each sub-array (for more details Chapter 18). The storage capacity efficiency is specified by

```
$SCE = \frac{ESC}{TDC} = \frac{n-2}{n},$$
```

$ESC = (n-2) \times DC$.
