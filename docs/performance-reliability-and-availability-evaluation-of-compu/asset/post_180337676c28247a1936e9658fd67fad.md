## 25.1.1 RELIABILITY DATA SOURCES

As we have, lifetime data may be obtained from operational systems or reliability tests. Device manufacturers may also reliability tests of their products and make the corresponding lifetime data available as handbooks, data books, databases, and some in-house data collections that are not generally available. This section provides a few widely known reliability data sources.

Such lifetime data sources can be categorized as Company-specific, Industry-specific, and Generic lifetime data. Company-specific data are collected from comparable or similar systems or components manufactured by the company. Industry-specific reliability data comes from data books of distinct companies that produce similar products. Generic lifetime data sets are assembled from extensive data sources that combine multiple assets and sources. Company-specific data should be used in preference to Industry-specific data, which, in turn, should be used in preference to Generic data. It is worth noting that reliability are strongly related to specific modes of failure. Unfortunately, lifetime data sources specify the failure modes. Some of these data sources are published as handbooks and databases [10].

The US Military Handbook 217 (MIL-HDBK-217) is a well-known source of generic electro-electronic device data for military and commercial enterprises. MIL-HDBK-217F fosters a predictive process concerning of two parts: (1) part count method and (2) part stress method. The part count method assumes typical operational conditions, i.e., complexity, temperature, stresses, operating mode, and environment.

$$\lambda_p = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_{ref,i}, \quad (25.1.1)$$

where $\lambda_{ref,i}$ is the component $i$'s failure rate under the reference conditions, and $n$ is the number of elements that compose the part $p$. Since the parts may not operate under the reference conditions, the actual operating conditions will result in failure rates different from those given by the parts count method. Therefore, the stress method requires the specific factors so that

$$\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_{ref,i} \times \pi_S \times \pi_T \times \pi_E \times \pi_Q \times \pi_A, \quad (25.1.2)$$

where $\pi_S, \pi_T, \pi_E, \pi_Q,$ and $\pi_A$ are the stress, temperature, environment, quality and adjustment factors.

OREDA is a project constituted of oil and gas corporations, that other objectives, aims to produce and maintain a reliability database to sustain reliability information interchange among members. This project started in 1981 as an initiative of the Norwegian Petroleum Directorate (Petroleum Safety Authority). Non-members may access the reliability data by purchasing OREDA handbook licenses [320, 321, 406].

Reliability prediction methods have a long and distinguished history of application inside and outside the telecommunications business. The Bellcore/Telcordia standards-SR-332 - support reliability models for components applications. SR-332 methodology is similar to the MIL-HDBK-217. SR-332 employs a series of models for several classes of electronic, electrical and electromechanical parts to prevent infant mortality and steady-state failure rates, considering environmental circumstances, quality levels, electrical stress factors, and other parameters affected. It is worth noting that Bellcore (a spin-off of AT&T Bell Labs) became Telcordia Technologies. Later on, Ericsson acquired Telcordia [425]. SR-332 Issue 4 provides hardware reliability prediction process deployed from prominent industries [411].

FARADIP reliability data is represented as ranges. Overall, the lower values in these ranges are employed when assessing reliability design. In other words, these estimates might reasonably represent the device failure rates after some experience and presumed reliability growth. Failure mode percentages are also considered as impact factors. This database includes microelectronics, discrete elements, passive instruments and analyzers, electromechanical, power, mechanical, pneumatics, hydraulics, communication, alarms, and fire devices. The data supplied are for a typical ground-fixed environment$^1$. Variation to other environments and quality arrangements is dealt with employing factors [401, 402].

> $1$Typical ground-fixed environments are those moderately controlled settings as devices in maine racks, boxes, and shelves, with satisfactory cooling and unheated buildings and communications facilities. The term cooling air airflow over devices at or below the devices' temperature to draw heat away and cool down or stabilize the temperature to that of the air [402].

SN 29500 was conceived and has been supported and updated by Siemens company. This standard is adopted by Siemens and its associates as the source for reliability of electronic and electromechanical components. It gives component failure rates expressed in FIT$^2$ for a list of device classes having a reference state.

The process for estimating the failure rate (FIT units) of a device involves looking up the device's failure rate (in FIT) at reference state ($rs$) by a table. The SN 29500 standard also provides a method for estimating the failure rate at operating state ($\lambda_{os}$ - in FIT) from at reference state ($\lambda_{rs}$ - in FIT).

$$\lambda_{os} = \lambda_{rs} \times f_{temp} \times f_v \times f_c \times f_{stress}, \quad (25.1.3)$$

where $f_{temp}$ is the temperature factor, $f_v$ is the velocity factor, $f_c$ is the current factor, and $f_{stress}$ is the stress factor [348].

The British Handbook of Reliability Data for Electronic Components in Telecommunication Systems (HRD-5) provides definitions, failure rates and outlines a methodology for assessing system (sub-systems, circuit) reliability from electronic and electromechanical component failure information. This handbook has strive in the light of components' field data gathered by British Telecom, France Telecom, and laboratory-derived data. The reliability estimation has been adopted to (1) afford a FIT rate unity expressed as the number of failures in one billion hours (2) identify critical reliability components, and (3) to provide means of assessing the reliability impact on system design and analyzing alternative options [402].

The Automotive Electronics Council (AEC) was initially established by Chrysler, Ford, and GM to establish common part-qualification and quality-system standards. The AEC Technical Committee is responsible for establishing standards for pursuing highly reliable systems. The reliability data produced are primarily concerned with electronic parts employed in military, transport, manufacturing, integrated circuits, discrete semiconductors, passive electronic components, telecommunication, and information systems. The documents produced by AEC are available on the website [5, 10].

Xilinx publishes reports to reliability product manufacturing. Xilinx executes reliability programs in internal requirements for supporting reliability qualities of new devices or adjusting manufacturing processes to sustain quality standards. The manufacturing process is supervised by a reliability monitor that considers the maturity of the manufacturing process, the number of device hours, and the failure in time rate. The reliability tests are periodically conducted, but the number of tests can be reduced or eliminated based on the maturity of the process [473].
