## Hyperexponential Distribution

Let us assume a device of the specified type is produced in $n$ different plants. These devices have constant failure rates; their failure rates are different as the production processes are slightly different. The produced devices are mixed up before being delivered to retailers. Now, we pick one item at random; the reliability of this item is

$$R(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i R_i(t)$$

$R_i(t) = e^{-\lambda_i t}$ is the reliability of an item produced in plant $i$, $-\lambda_i$ is the constant failure rate, $p_i$ is the fraction of items coming from plant $i$, and $\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i = 1$. Thus,

$$R(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i e^{-\lambda_i t} \quad t \geq 0. \quad (16.3.16)$$

Hence

$$F(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i(1-e^{-\lambda_i t}) \quad t \geq 0. \quad (16.3.17)$$

which is the hyperexponential distribution (see Chapter 2, Section 14.3.4 and Section 13.3.8.3). The density function is

$$f(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \lambda_i e^{-\lambda_i t} \quad t \geq 0. \quad (16.3.18)$$

The MTTF is $\int_{0}^{\infty} R(t) dt = \int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i e^{-\lambda_i t} dt$, so

$$MTTF = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{p_i}{\lambda_i}; \quad \text{where } \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i = 1.$$

Applying 16.2.7, we get the hazard rate function

$$\lambda(t) = -\frac{dR(t)}{dt} R(t)$$

$$\lambda(t) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i e^{-\lambda_i t} / dt}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i e^{-\lambda_i t}}$$

$$\lambda(t) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \lambda_i e^{-\lambda_i t}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i e^{-\lambda_i t}}; \quad \text{where } \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i = 1 \text{ and } t \ge 0.$$

## Erlang Distribution

Consider a device is subjected to hits that occur as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$. Assume this device fails after $k$ hits (increasing failure rate). This model leads to a reliability function that belongs to the gamma distribution with $k$ integer. The rate is the same for every hit, this gamma distribution is an Erlang distribution; otherwise, it is a hypoexponential distribution (see Chapter 2 and Section 13.3.8.3). The Erlang CDF is

$$F(t) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{i!} \dots \text{(corrected)}$$

The reliability is defined by

$$R(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{i!} \dots \text{(corrected)}$$

The density function is specified by

$$f(t) = \frac{\lambda^k t^{k-1} e^{-\lambda t}}{(k-1)!}; \quad t \ge 0.$$

The hazard function is

$$\lambda(t) = \frac{\lambda^k t^{k-1} e^{-\lambda t}}{(k-1)!} \dots \text{(corrected)}$$

The mean time to failure is

$$MTTF = \frac{k}{\lambda} \quad (16.3.25)$$

The variance of time to failure is

$$Var(TTF) = \frac{k}{\lambda^2}. \quad (16.3.26)$$

## Cox Distributions

As mentioned in Chapter 5 and Section 13.3.8, the advantage of using phase-type distributions is the fact that they can approximate any non-negative distribution closely. Cox distributions can be adopted to match the first central moment (mean) and the second central moment (variance) of devices' lifetime.

The Weibull Distribution is widely used in reliability data analysis due to its versatility. In general, it is defined by three parameters, $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ ("shape parameter"), $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^+$ ("scale parameter"), and $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^+$ ("location parameter"). In addition, the distribution has support on the interval $[\mu, \infty)$.

The value of $\alpha$ modifies the shape of the failure rate (see Figure 16.10). Values below one ($\alpha<1$) specify a failure rate that decreases with time. If $\alpha = 1$, the failure rate is constant (exponential distribution). Failure rates increase with time when $\alpha > 1$.

$$\lambda(t) = \frac{\alpha(t-\mu)^{\alpha-1}}{\beta}; \quad t \geq \mu. \quad (16.3.27)$$

CDF:

$$F(t) = 1 - e^{-\left(\frac{t-\mu}{\beta}\right)^\alpha}, \quad t \geq \mu.$$

Reliability:

$$R(t) = e^{-\left(\frac{t-\mu}{\beta}\right)^\alpha}, \quad t \geq \mu.$$

PDF:

$$f(t) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} e^{-\left(\frac{t-\mu}{\beta}\right)^\alpha} \left(\frac{t-\mu}{\beta}\right)^{\alpha-1}, \quad t \geq \mu.$$

Mean time to failure:

$$MTTF = \beta\Gamma \left(1 + \frac{1}{\alpha}\right) + \mu.$$

**Example 16.3.3.** Consider a system with a hazard function to $\lambda(t) = bt^a, a > 0, b > 0$ (a monotonically increasing function). As the cumulative hazard function is $H(t) = \int_0^t \lambda(t) dt$, then

$$H(t) = \frac{b}{a+1}t^{a+1}.$$

If $a = \alpha - 1$, then

$$H(t) = \frac{bt^\alpha}{\alpha}.$$

If $b = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}$, then

$$H(t) = \left(\frac{t}{\beta}\right)^\alpha.$$

Therefore,

$$R(t) = e^{-\left(\frac{t}{\beta}\right)^\alpha},$$

$$F(t) = 1 - e^{-\left(\frac{t}{\beta}\right)^\alpha},$$

which is a Weibull distribution with parameters $\alpha, \beta$ and $\mu = 0$.

Normal Distribution (also known as the Gaussian distribution) is defined over the real numbers. The distribution is parametrized by a real number $\mu$, and a positive real number $\sigma$, where $\mu$ is the mean of the distribution, $\sigma$ is the standard deviation, and $\sigma^2$ is the variance. The normal distribution is certainly the most widely adopted general-purpose distribution. This is the reason for including it in the lifetime distributions. Some authors argue that the normal distribution is not suitable for modeling lifetime data since its domain encompasses negative numbers, resulting in modeling negative times to failure. However, if the distribution has a relatively high mean and a small standard deviation, negative failure times should not present a problem.

PDF:

$$f(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-\frac{(t-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \quad (16.3.32)$$

$$F(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} dx. \quad (16.3.33)$$

$$R(t) = \int_{t}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} dx. \quad (16.3.34)$$

Mean time to failure:

$$MTTF = \mu. \quad (16.3.35)$$

Standard deviation of time to failure:

$$SD(TTF) = \sigma. \quad (16.3.36)$$

**Log-Normal Distribution** is supported over the interval $[0, \infty)$, parametrized by a real number $\mu$ and by a positive real number $\sigma$. The lognormal distribution is usually adopted as a model of the lifetime of components whose fail due to fatigue-stress. A random variable is lognormally distributed if the logarithm of the random variable is normally distributed.
