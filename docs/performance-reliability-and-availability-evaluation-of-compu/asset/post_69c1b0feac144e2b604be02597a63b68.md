## Constant Scaling

In many practical cases, simple constant scaling factors represent the environment’s dynamics that affect the fault events for reasonable value ranges. Hence, as the time to failure of an event in stress conditions may be estimated from

```
$TTFs = TTFo \times CF$,
```

where $CF$ is constant, the cumulative distribution function is obtained from

```
$F_s(t) = F_o\left(\frac{t}{CF}\right). \quad (26.1.3)
```

As $f(t) = dF(t)/dt$, then

```
$f_s(t) = \frac{1}{CF} \times f_o\left(\frac{t}{CF}\right). \quad (26.1.4)
```

As the hazard function (see Equation 16.2.6)

```
$\lambda(t) = \frac{f(t)}{R(t)} = \frac{f(t)}{1 - F(t)}$,
```

then

```
$\lambda_s(t) = \frac{\frac{1}{CF} \times f_o(\frac{t}{CF})}{1 - F_o(\frac{t}{CF})}$,
```

Therefore

```
$\lambda_s(t) = \frac{1}{CF} \times \lambda_o(t). \quad (26.1.5)
```

Thus, applying transformation factor $CF$ (constant), the Equations 26.1.3, 26.1.4, and 26.1.5 do not change their respective shapes. On the other hand, if the scaling factor $(AF, CF)$ is not constant, the shape of the distribution $TTFs$ may change with relation to the shape of the distribution $TTF_o$.

As the mean time to failure may be calculated from (see Equation 16.2.11)

```
$MTTF_s = \int_{0}^{\infty}(1 - F(t))dt,
```

then, the mean time to failure ($MTTF_s$) may be obtained from

```
$MTTF_s = \int_{0}^{\infty} \left(1 - F_o\left(\frac{t}{CF}\right)\right) dt, \quad (26.1.6)
```

**Example 26.1.1.** Assume a stress experiment was executed and a sample of hundred times to failure collected. The mean time calculated from the sample was $MTTF_s = 548.3h$. If the acceleration factor is $AF=12$, the mean time to failure at normal operation condition may be estimated using Equation 26.1.2. Hence,

```
$MTTF_o = AF \times MTTF_s$
$MTTF_o = 12 \times 548.3h$
$MTTF_o = 6579.6$.
```

There is a subtle assumption when multiplying test lifetime data by a constant factor since it assumes the lifetime distribution of the system under stress conditions is the same as the lifetime distribution of the system in normal operating conditions. Consequently, the two distributions are considered to have the same shape, which may not be the case [469].

## Non-Constant Scaling

For non-constant scaling factors, the failure rate (and the time to failure) parameters represented in the model. Failure rate increases according to the stresses are commonly related to aging, environmental conditions, and operation stress levels. The following general model

```
$AF = \sum_{i=1}^n B_i \times A_i^{(D_i - F_i \times s_i)^{E_i}} + C_i$,
```

Where $B_i, D_i$, and $F_i$ are constant values, and $s_i$ is a stress parameter, such that $s_i \in [0, D_i]$. Besides, $A_i$ and $E_i$ are functions of the stress parameter $s_i$, which may also be constant, and $n$ is the number of stress factors. A such model may be configured to represent many scaling factors' dynamics.

**Example 26.1.2.** Consider the failure factor model defined in Expression 26.1.8 of a specific device customized with $n=1, A_1=B_1=E_1=1, C_1=0$, and $D_1=2$. Thus, the specific failure factor is

```
$AF = A_0^{2-s},
```

where $A_0 = 4$; $s \in [0,2]$ denotes the stress level, and $A_0$ is failure factor when no acceleration is applied. The maximal stress level is ($s=2$) and when no stress is applied $s=0$. An accelerated experiment was conducted for a sample of devices and the mean time to failure $MTTF_s=548.3h$. Thus the mean time to failure at normal operation conditions ($s=0$) is estimated by

```
$MTTF_o = MTTF_s \times AF$,
$MTTF_o = MTTF_s \times A_0^{2-s}$,
$MTTF_o = 548.3h \times 4^{2} = 8772.8h$.
```

Figure 26.2.a and 26.2.b depict the failure rate and the mean time as function of $AF=A_0^{2-s}$ for $A_0=4$ and $s \in [0,2]$.

A power law is a relationship between quantities a change in one quantity is inversely proportional to the power of another. In a mechanical system, the lifetime of component is expected to decrease as stress increases. One of the most widely used models for reliability analysis of mechanical systems is the **Inverse Power Law (IPL)** [37]. The IPL model may be represented by customizing Model 26.1.8. For instance, assuming $n=1$, and $B_1=B$ is a real constant, $A_1=s, C_1=0, E_1=1$ and $F_1=n$, we obtain

```
$TTF = Bs^{-n}, (26.1.9)
```

which is an IPL model for the time to failure ($TTF$).

**Example 26.1.3.** Assume a mechanical system whose time to failure is dependent on electrical tension, $V$, provided by the power, and obeys IPL

$TTF = BV^{-2}$,

where $B$ is a dimensional constant equal to $10^8 h^2$. If the mean time to failure, $MTTF_2$, for $V_2=120V$ is $7000h$, the mean time to failure, $MTTF_1$ (and the respective failure rate, assuming it is constant at that voltage) taking into account electrical tension at $V_1=140V$ may be estimated using

$MTTF_1 = BV_1^{-2}$,

and

$MTTF_2 = BV_2^{-2}$.

Hence,

$\frac{MTTF_1}{MTTF_2} = \frac{BV_1^{-2}}{BV_2^{-2}} = \left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)^2, (26.1.10)$

$MTTF_1 = MTTF_2 \times \left(\frac{120}{140}\right)^2 = 7000h \times \frac{14400}{19600} = 5142.86h$,

and

$\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 V_1^2 V_2^{-2}, \quad (26.1.11)$

```

```

## Regression

An alternative strategy is adopting curve fitting methods as those introduced in Section 6.7 to estimate parameters (failure rates, time to failure statistics, etc.) at several different high-stress levels, and then fitting the respective model, and extrapolating the found parameter values to the normal stress level. Hence, a strategy aims to find out trends that allow predictive analytic functions through the available data set to estimate failure parameters at normal operating conditions.

**Example 26.1.4.** Let us consider five samples of ten devices that were tested under distinct stress levels. The stress levels in the experiment accelerate the devices' failures by 10, 12, 14, 16, and 18. The whole experiment finished when every device of each sample failed.
