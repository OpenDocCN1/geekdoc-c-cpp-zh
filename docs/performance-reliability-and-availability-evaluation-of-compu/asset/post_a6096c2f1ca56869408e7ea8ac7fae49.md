It is worth stressing again; our goal is to maximize the probability term by finding the optimal $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$. Hence, we seek the "$argmax$ of this term concerning $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$. Therefore

$$\hat{\theta}_1 = argmax_{i=1}^n P(t_i \mid \Theta)$$

and

$$\hat{\theta}_2 = argmax_{i=1}^n P(t_i \mid \Theta)$$

Hence, find $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ by finding the roots of the derivatives of $\prod_{i=1}^n P(t_i \mid \Theta)$ with respect to each component of $\Theta$, in this case, $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$.

$$\frac{\partial \prod_{i=1}^n P(t_i \mid \Theta)}{\partial \theta_1} = 0$$

and

$$\frac{\partial \prod_{i=1}^n P(t_i \mid \Theta)}{\partial \theta_2} = 0$$

However, obtaining a derivative to calculate the parameter value, $\theta_j$, that maximizes $\prod_{i=1}^n P(t_i \mid \Theta)$ is often not an easy task. It usually is easier to obtain the derivative of

$$\ln(\prod_{i=1}^n P(t_i \mid \Theta))$$

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \ln(P(t_i \mid \Theta))$$

In such cases, we solve

$$\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln(P(t_i|\Theta))\right)}{\theta_1} = 0$$
$$\leftrightarrow$$
$$\sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\left(\ln(P(t_i|\Theta))\right)}{\theta_1} = 0$$

and

$$\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln(P(t_i|\Theta))\right)}{\theta_2} = 0$$
$$\leftrightarrow$$
$$\sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\left(\ln(P(t_i|\Theta))\right)}{\theta_2} = 0$$

to find $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$. Obviously, in $\prod_{i=1}^{n} P(t_i|\Theta)$ is a different value from $\prod_{i=1}^{n} P(t_i|\Theta)$, but both functions have the same global maximum with respect to $\Theta$ since $\ln(t)$ does not change the curvature of $f(t)$. ## Normal Distribution

Now, let us think about a distribution with two parameters we want to infer, $\mu$ and $\sigma$, rather than the symbolic representation $\theta$. Assume a sample of only one point was collected from a normal population with $\mu$ and $\sigma$ as its true, unknown values. Assume the likelihood function is the density function of distribution; hence

$$L(\mu, \sigma|t_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_1-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

Figure 25.25 shows the value of likelihood $L(\mu, \sigma|t_1)$ given the data point $t_1$.

![](img/2d1450514bb14a0cdf0eb5048614afab_598_0.png)

Figure 25.25 $L(\mu, \sigma|t_1)$ for Sample of Only One Point.

Now assume $\{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n\}$ as independent points collected from a population normally distributed with the unknown parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma$. Therefore

$$L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) = L(\mu, \sigma|t_1) \times L(\mu, \sigma|t_2) \times \dots \times L(\mu, \sigma|t_n)$$

$$L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_1-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_2-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \times \dots \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_n-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

Now, let us solve $L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)$ for the maximum likelihood for $\mu$ and $\sigma$. Hence, we have to calculate two derivatives of $L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)$, one with respect to $\mu$, and the other with respect to $\sigma$.

$$\frac{\partial L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)}{\partial\mu} = 0$$

and

$$\frac{\partial L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)}{\partial\sigma} = 0$$

and solve for $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

But before taking the derivative of $L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)$, let us consider the $\ln(L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n))$ since it transforms the products into summations and $\ln e$ makes the derivative much easier. Thus,

$$\ln(L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)) = \ln\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_1-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_2-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \times \dots \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_n-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\right)$$

Hence,

$$\ln(\mu, \sigma | t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) = \ln\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_1-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\right) + \ln\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_2-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\right) + \dots + \ln\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_n-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\right)$$

Let us use one of these terms above, for instance, $\ln\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_1-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\right)$, hence,

$$\ln(\mu, \sigma | t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi) - n\ln(\sigma) - \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{(t_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$$

Therefore, we started from

$$L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) = \ln\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_1-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_2-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \times \dots \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_n-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\right)$$

and arrived at

$$\ln(L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)) = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi) - n\ln(\sigma) - \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(t_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \quad (25.3.20)$$

Now, let us take

$$\frac{\partial \ln(L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n))}{\partial\mu} = -0 + 0 + \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(t_i-\mu)}{\sigma^2}$$

Hence,

$$\frac{\partial \ln(L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n))}{\partial\mu} = \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(t_i-\mu)}{\sigma^2}$$

$$\frac{\partial \ln(L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n))}{\partial\sigma} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(t_i-\mu)$$

Therefore

$$\frac{\partial \ln(L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n))}{\partial\sigma} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(t_i-\mu) \quad (25.3.21)$$

Now, taking the Equation 25.3.20, that is

$$\ln(L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)) = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi) - n\ln(\sigma) - \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(t_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$$

and deriving it with respect to $\sigma$

$$\frac{\partial \ln(L(\mu, \sigma|t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n))}{\partial\sigma} = -\frac{n}{\sigma}-\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(t_i-\mu)}{\sigma^2}$$
