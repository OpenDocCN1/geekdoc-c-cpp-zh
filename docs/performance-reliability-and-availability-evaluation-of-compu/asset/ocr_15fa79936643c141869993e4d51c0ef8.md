Another possible drawback is when RAID 1 solutions do not accept the failed disk hot-swapping. In such case, the disk can only be replaced after powering the system, which may not be acceptable in many circumstances. However, this difficulty can be overcome by the use of hardware controllers that support hot-swapping.

RAID 2 is seldom used; data are organized at the bit level rather than in block, and it uses a Hamming code for error correction. RAID 3 is also rarely used. It is a modification of RAID 2 with a dedicated parity disk. RAID 3 consists of a bank of data disks with one parity disk. Neither RAID 2 nor RAID 3 is discussed here.

RAID 4 stripes across two or more disks and adds a dedicated parity disk. RAID 4 tolerates one disk failure in an array of disks. Hence, RAID 4 requires at least three disks to be implemented. Figure 17.10.b shows a RAID 4 system with data disks and a parity disk. For instance, if the parity disk fails, the remaining disks are not affected, but redundancy is lost. If a data disk fails, the RAID controller uses the remaining data disks and the parity disk to recover the missing data on the fly.
