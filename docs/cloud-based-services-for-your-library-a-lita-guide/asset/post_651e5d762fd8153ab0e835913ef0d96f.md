With so many measures it can be difficult to understand which are essential and what the real meaning of uptime assurances is. As a good example, Conry-Murray (2009) documented uptime guarantees by different cloud providers in a 2009 report and found them ranging from 99.7 percent to 100 percent depending on provider. Though 99.7 percent might seem good enough, it leaves room for over two hours of downtime per month. Reese (2009, 5.5) rightly observes that even a short period of downtime may have major implications for information services. In addition, because cloud services are often based on multiple service providers, measuring service quality can be very uncertain. This difference in perspective became apparent when Amazon experienced service downtime in one zone early in 2011 and caused downtime for services that had not been designed to operate in multiple availability zones (Leong 2011). In fact, Amazon’s availability definition is only for services that operate in multiple availability zones, meaning that organizations are not fully supported unless they configure their cloud services in the specified manner.

An additional challenge with SLAs is the difficulty of working with multiple vendors and agreements while trying to implement a sustainable and consistent service architecture. Making this process more difficult is the fact that libraries can find it difficult, if not impossible, to negotiate the SLA with large cloud providers. IaaS providers in particular provide an SLA for a broad system infrastructure and are not in the business of providing tailored support. Although not always realistic, there are ways around this one-size-fits-all approach. First, government organizations are gaining access to specialized IaaS environments that operate under specialized terms through Amazon and other IaaS providers. It may pay to find a community cloud provider that recognizes issues unique to your institution. Additionally, educational organizations can often negotiate with corporations for enterprise-wide solutions or can choose to (in the case of Amazon) pay for a higher level of service support. Finally, paying attention to built-in support levels and the availability of customer service can help libraries find the right fit in an IaaS provider. These providers all offer similar services, but their approaches to customer service vary considerably.

## Interpreting Contracts

Interpreting a cloud computing contract may involve a new area of expertise for IT staff. As always, consulting legal counsel, collections management, or administrative staff can be helpful. Some key areas to focus on in SLAs and terms of service include these:

- Duration of contract and termination procedures: What constitutes subscription and termination? How are renewals handled?
- Payment methods: What payment methods are supported, and what payment terms are extended? What is the procedure for termination if payment lapses?
- Legal jurisdiction: What jurisdiction and laws apply to the contract, and what limits are placed on issues of arbitration and liability?
- Indemnification, warranty, and service assurance: What warranties does the provider agree to and how do these assurances relate to the SLA? What liabilities does the provider recognize?
- Privacy policy: Under what conditions are customer data made available to external organizations?
- Data ownership and retention upon service discontinuation: Who owns data located on the cloud service, and what happens to that data when the service agreement is discontinued?
- Service integrity: What does the provider define as a complete service, and how does it assure that its service remains operational?
- Data disclosure and service monitoring: Does the provider regularly disclose data regarding customers? Does it monitor traffic, stored data, or processes?
- Data and service location: Where are the data housed by the cloud provider physically located? What implications does this have for your organization’s adherence to organizational policy and legal regulations?

A thorough exploration of these topics would easily fill a chapter, but good resources include policy papers by Jaeger et al. (2008, 2009) and Bradshaw et al. (2010).

## Service Capacity

Libraries are unique adopters of cloud computing in that their main focus is on providing information services rather than on managing information technology. Libraries also tend to adopt multiple platforms to fill the needs of their patrons and may encounter challenges when attempting to gain support for these systems within their organizations. Though virtualized and cloud solutions can help with this situation, they can also create an environment in which library IT service is fragmented onto multiple providers and platforms.

IT service organizations familiar with service management approaches such as ITIL or ITSM may have ways of measuring the impact of a selected information system on an organization's IT support capacity. Common measures include available IT infrastructure, IT staff support availability, and application administration capacity. These departments may view virtualization and cloud computing as a way to improve local capacity from the perspective of either infrastructure or IT staff time. As a library or IT department turns toward cloud or virtualized solutions that involve direct system or application management, it is important that it take a systematic approach that leverages literature on IT service management. For example, before implementing multiple IaaS or PaaS solutions, libraries should also design a service management and replication plan that accounts for staff resources, IT resources, and ongoing expenses. A core part of this consideration is workflow integration and operational overlap. In regards to service capacity, it is important to consider challenges to sharing data, compatibility issues in workflow integration, and loss of efficiency that may come when working with multiple virtualized or cloud solutions. For example, data access over an Internet-based network connection is likely to see more latency than when accessed over an internal network connection. This may not be problematic for short bursts of data, but it can be challenging when interacting with large amounts of data or when streaming audio or video materials over a cloud connection. To work around the latency issue in particular, cloud providers may offer dedicated network connections for upload/download, may allow the subscriber to ship large datasets on physical storage media to a data-loading department, and may feature

high-speed internal networks to facilitate transfer of data inside the provider's cloud network.

Likewise, if libraries turn to cloud solutions, it is important that they carefully consider the implications related to system interoperability. Because many library administrative services are being offered via browser-based web services, traditional efficiency techniques such as client macros, application switching, and keyboard shortcuts may not be usable for connecting disparate applications.

## ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS

### Infrastructure, Platform, and Service Costs

The ease with which costs can be projected varies with cloud platform, but estimates often involve more than accounting for direct run-time and licensing costs. Determining direct costs for SaaS solutions and entirely local virtualized or nonvirtualized solutions may prove to be easier to calculate than for public or community PaaS and IaaS solutions, if only because the purchasing approach for private IT service and SaaS service follows capital investment or yearly subscription models. In contrast, nonprivate PaaS and IaaS solutions are often charged at per-hour or per-bit levels, and estimating the costs for each of the elements (e.g., CPU time, network traffic, storage, backups, reserved IP addresses) can affect the reliability of these projections.

### Considerations for Calculating Cost

Financial factors relevant to moving to cloud environments include a need to support ongoing instead of capital costs, an ability to pay for cloud services using the payment terms available from a given cloud provider, and the ability to plan for and support long-term costs associated with cloud computing (e.g., long-term preservation of digital resources and migration costs). The ability to pay for cloud services using a supported payment method, as it turns out, can be significant for some organizations. As of this writing, Amazon EC2 services are available only via credit card transactions, except for large subscribers. This can create problems in small organizations where a payment method might be tied to a specific individual's corporate card; it can be a deal breaker in organizations where credit card transactions are not supported at all. In these cases, alternate cloud brokers can be selected that support a common payment method.
