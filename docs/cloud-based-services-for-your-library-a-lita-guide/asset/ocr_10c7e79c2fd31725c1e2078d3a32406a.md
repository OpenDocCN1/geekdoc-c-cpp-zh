The “as-a-service” approach to cloud computing includes database as a service (DaaS; Howe et al. 2011), security as a service (SECaaS; Hussain and Abdulsalam 2011), and identity as a service (Krüger and Grabski 2009). The reuse of acronyms as well as a broadening of definitions in cloud computing can make it difficult for information organizations to identify and select services. At the same time, companies are able to provide niche services that would be difficult or too expensive for smaller organizations to implement. For example, CloudFlare (www.cloudflare.com) protects against denial of service and traffic load spikes by managing domain name service (DNS) and service replication across multiple cloud providers without the required load balancing and security configuration required for this type of service. By taking advantage of such a service, an information organization can built robustness into its IT configuration without the costs that would have been a necessity in more traditional IT environments. As our exploration of these service models in chapters 4 and 5 shows, the model a library chooses largely depends on factors such as local expertise, IT service requirements, organizational policy, and local preference.

# Deployment Models

NIST’s four deployment models (Fang et al. 2011) differentiate internal and external environments and examine approaches to cloud computing that serve specific communities. In this model, private clouds are defined as an environment in which the customer has exclusive physical or virtual access to its computing services. These services may be on-site or remotely hosted by a cloud provider, but typically they involve dedicated hardware and are available only via private physical or virtual networks. In community cloud environments a group of related customers have shared access to computing services. Although the actual computing services may be run in-house or by the cloud provider, community clouds tend to feature shared access to some or all resources in the cloud environment. Government clouds or educational clouds are good examples of community and private clouds. These environments may be run on dedicated hardware and networks, as in the case of private clouds, but may also be run on hardware that serves other cloud customers but with virtual isolation. The Amazon government computing cloud is just one of many community/private cloud solutions available. In contrast to private and community clouds, public cloud solutions feature a cloud-computing service and infrastructure that is made available to the general public.
