## Results

In total, the survey received 111 responses. All questions in the survey were optional, so response totals for each question may differ. The survey data were grouped into three main areas: demographic information, quantitative results on cloud computing adoption, and qualitative results on cloud computing adoption.

Survey data were coded with guidance from the framework represented in figure 2.1 but were open to addition and interpretation based on participant-generated themes. Coding focused on identifying themes and constructs that influence adoption of cloud computing and virtualized technologies. Codes were refined and contributed to the development of an expanded framework focused on the exploration of factors and constructs important in considering new IT services. Quantitative data were analyzed to provide both descriptive statistics on cloud computing adoption and inferential statistics on relationships between institution type and size, service type (e.g., digital library, reference service), and technology platform adopted (e.g., cloud, virtualized, traditional).

## Demographics

The majority of libraries that responded to the survey were academic (n=57). Other libraries included public (n=13) and law or medical (n=7). Of note, six participants represented consortial IT support for libraries, and there were libraries that served multiple populations including academic/public (n=2) and academic/medical (n=1). The low number of responses from nonacademic libraries limits generalizability of these findings. In fact, the demographics point to a study population dominated by medium-sized academic institutions, and the organizational and environmental issues raised should be considered within that context.

A majority of respondents also indicated that their libraries have relatively small environments of one to three IT support staff (n=47). The overall size of library staff showed a more equal distribution, with an emphasis on slightly small and medium-sized libraries (29.9 percent with 40 or fewer staff) versus large (14.9 percent with 41–119 staff) and very large (19.4 percent with 120 or more staff). Patron population size varied, with over 32 percent of respondents serving 15,000 or more patrons and 22.4 percent of libraries serving between 3,000 and 15,000 patrons. There was also a mix of branch sizes, with 27 percent (n=37) of respondents supporting a single library branch and 21 percent (n=29) supporting two to five branches. The number of participants supporting larger branch organizations was much smaller (6–20 branches, n=13; 21–30 branches, n=4; 31 or more, n=8).

## State of Adoption of Cloud Services for Library IT Services

The survey found a variety of adoption rates and adoption platforms for each system. In some cases (e.g., ILS), adoption trended toward nonvirtualized solutions; in others (e.g., research guides), internally and externally virtualized/cloud solutions predominated. Table 2.4 shows service/platform groupings used by 10–19 percent of participants. Services are listed multiple times (e.g., website) if their adoption rate met the range associated with the table in multiple categories.

These adoption levels indicate some common trends in the libraries surveyed. For example, older systems such as ILS and library OPACs tended to be both widely adopted and run only in a nonvirtualized environment. Likewise, client computing tended to be nonvirtualized (e.g., public client computers and staff client computers), but there were instances of desktop virtualization for both client and staff solutions. In contrast, more recently developed systems (e.g., discovery services) and data-centric systems (e.g., OpenURL resolvers) tended to be virtualized using in-house or external cloud systems or outsourced completely. For example, Table 2.2 shows that 24 percent of respondents ran a discovery service as an outsourced service, an additional 11 percent ran the service using some form of virtualized or cloud platform, and 3 percent indicated that these services were run as a nonvirtualized service. Although several systems grouped around nonvirtualized and outsourced solutions, it was interesting to note that the only service that showed a substantial locally or cloud-based (but not outsourced) virtualized solution was the library website (31 percent).

As table 2.3 shows, some systems fit into multiple implementation platforms. For example, both OPAC and library website systems fit under both internally virtualized and nonvirtualized platforms. It is interesting that this view of the data shows a grouping of systems under either nonvirtualized or outsourced solutions. This gap in adoption points to an interesting trend in cloud adoption toward outsourced solutions. Table 2.4, which shows that fewer libraries use internal and external virtual/cloud systems, supports this trend and indicates that libraries less frequently adopt internal virtualization and external PaaS/IaaS cloud solutions as compared to nonvirtualized or SaaS solutions. The lack of use of externally virtualized (e.g., PaaS or IaaS) services raises questions about the factors that influence library adoption of technology platforms and could have a considerable impact on the development of new library systems (particularly open-source solutions) in the coming years.

A second trend in the data is the prevalence of data-intensive and new genres of systems in the outsourced category while traditional systems (e.g., the ILS) remain nonvirtualized. Participant responses regarding the motivations for implementing systems in nonvirtualized environments indicate that libraries are shifting these systems toward cloud-based solutions but are still considering options or have yet to find cloud solutions that meet all of their needs. The ILS is the most representative system of this type, seeing relatively low levels of implementation in each of the three virtualized categories, with 14 percent of libraries reporting using an ILS on a virtualized platform, 10 percent using some form of external cloud provider, and just over 16 percent using an outsourced or SaaS solution. Though individual approaches showed relatively low percentages, it is worth noting that over 40 percent of libraries had turned to some internally or externally virtualized solution. This trend was seen across most systems. When grouped into virtualized and nonvirtualized solutions, it was common, with the exception of client computing, to see a relatively high level of virtualized use. This trend included server virtualization as well, with 41 percent of libraries using internally virtualized resources.

## Perceptions of Cloud Computing and Virtualization

In addition to exploring the landscape of cloud computing adoption, the survey also explored the participants’ view of the potential impact of cloud computing and virtualization. Participants were asked to indicate their agreement with two statements about both cloud computing and virtualization: “Cloud computing/virtualization is a current priority of the administration,” and “Cloud computing/virtualization has the potential to transform library IT in the short term.” Figures 2.2 through 2.5 show participant responses for these questions.

The data from these questions show a largely positive view of the impact of both cloud computing and virtualization. Likewise, survey participants largely viewed cloud computing and virtualization as priorities of the administration. There was some variation in participant views of the priority of cloud computing and virtualization, indicating that participants are bullish on cloud computing and virtualization but more positive about the latter.

## Motivating and Inhibiting Factors of Cloud Computing and Virtualization

The survey also collected qualitative data on the motivating and inhibiting factors related to the use of cloud computing and virtualization resources. For each series of qualitative responses, codes were generated from responses and then mapped onto the survey analysis framework. Statements were coded into TAM areas (value and ease of use) when the participant stated a perception (e.g., nonvirtualized is easier) as opposed to a situation (e.g., current IT policy inhibits cloud migration).
