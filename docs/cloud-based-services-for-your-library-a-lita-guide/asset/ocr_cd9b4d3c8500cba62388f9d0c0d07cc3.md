Another factor that complicates cloud cost calculation is the expected duration of the service being implemented. Reese recommends projecting costs over a three-year period; Feldman differentiates “start-up” and “enterprise” adoptions and argues that as organizations move to an enterprise level they are likely to find more cost savings in running a private cloud environment with public cloud bursting capacity. Rather than think in terms of start-up versus enterprise, libraries may want to consider the projected longevity of an information service instead. This can help an organization understand the potential cost savings from up-front capital investments versus ongoing cloud investments in short- and long-term services. It also can make the calculation of ROI more complex. This likely involves choosing or adhering to a depreciation schedule and having a detailed understanding of the technology costs that will and will not go away in a cloud service. To help with these calculations, Reese provides formulas to calculate cost over the life of the service and to determine current technology value (2009, 53, 12). Cloud providers, particularly IaaS providers, may provide cost calculators—for example, Amazon (http://calculator.s3.amazonaws.com/calc5.html), Microsoft Azure (www.microsoft.com/windowsazure/pricing-calculator/), and RightScale (www.rightscale.com/tco-calculator/).

Another important factor in cost calculation, particularly for libraries, is data storage and data transfer costs. A common method for planning for backup costs over time is to estimate the amount of change in data expected over a month and multiply total storage by that percentage (e.g., if you expect a 15 percent change in a month, your backup charges are 1.15 times storage costs). The fact that backed-up data are entirely replicated on cloud services can make storage costs seem very expensive, but many cloud services including Amazon’s S3 and Glacier services include automatic replication and redundancy as part of the storage service. This does not eliminate the need for backups, but for organizations comfortable with this approach to storage the use of version control and unique backups with limited archiving history can lower the overall costs of storage.
