**Elasticity:** Elasticity refers to the ability of an IT service to scale up or down in response to demand. Elasticity should be evaluated for both PaaS and SaaS services to ensure that the platform selected is both properly sized and scalable for an organization's size.

**Environment:** Often discussed in the context of containers, environments define the software stack on which services in a PaaS service run. Environments are typically defined by their scripting/programming platform (e.g., PHP, Java, Ruby) and web server platform (e.g., Apache, Nginx, Tomcat).

**Events:** SaaS, PaaS, and IaaS services should offer some form of event logging as part of the service. This tends to be a log of the actions taken and system response to those actions.

**Plug-in/add-on:** SaaS and PaaS environments make value-added services available as resources or add-ons in a pluggable application architecture. Plug-ins are designed to be added to a service with minimal configuration and may include database, messaging, or logging services.

**Repository:** In PaaS environments, this term tends to refer to software source repositories for developed code. The most popular distribution repository for PaaS solutions explored in this book is Git as implemented on a hosted service called github.

**Version control:** PaaS platforms tend to rely on version control to ensure the ability to control distributions easily. PaaS providers typically support distribution of a software service via a plug-in to popular version control platforms.

## SAAS LIBRARY SOLUTIONS

SaaS solutions are often attractive to libraries seeking to offload the risk, expertise, and management issues associated with IT services and are increasingly popular given the use of centrally managed data sets (e.g., link resolver knowledge base and bibliographic databases). Libraries are turning to cloud platforms for data-intensive and new services such as link resolvers, digital reference services, research guides, and large-scale discovery systems, with slower adoption of complex systems such as online catalogs, websites, and digital asset management systems; in general, the tendency is to adopt SaaS solutions. And although SaaS solutions clearly provide benefits—such as enterprise level IT, fast implementation, and minimized impact on library IT staff—they may be more expensive, offer limited customization (compared to PaaS and IaaS solutions), and feature long-term commitments not found in other cloud service platforms. The SaaS library-specific solutions discussed below reflect these characteristics, which are markedly different from the examples discussed for PaaS and IaaS solutions; for this reason, case studies here are grouped into system types rather than vendor platforms.

## SaaS Provider Examples

More so than other cloud solutions in this chapter, SaaS solutions tend to focus on library-specific applications. Although there are nonlibrary-specific services, such as hosted content management systems (e.g., wordpress.com) and customer relations systems (e.g., Salesforce.com), these systems are not as widely adopted as library-specific SaaS services. Many of the services discussed here more closely fit the broad terms hosted and outsourced; their inclusion here reflects the idea that they are made available without any infrastructure, platform, or application-level management requirements. In general, SaaS is distinguished from PaaS in that it more closely fits the “turnkey” approach to systems development.

A good taxonomy of library system types and vendors can be drawn from Breeding’s website survey of library automation (www.librarytechnology.org). Breeding categorizes systems into eleven types: library services platforms, discovery products and services, federated search, mobile products, digital library management systems, electronic resource management support, institutional repositories, OpenURL linking applications, resource sharing systems, RFID systems, and archives management systems. In addition to these, libraries tend to use knowledge bases, digital reference service tools, content management systems, and, of course, subscriptions to a host of aggregated information resources that are delivered in an SaaS environment. This review examines systems from three broad categories: next generation cloud-based ILS, DAMS, and public-facing services.

## Next Generation Cloud-Based ILS

It is increasingly common to see ILS vendors advertise cloud computing as a key aspect of their ILS. This may include a wholly cloud-based solution or simply virtualized hosting or knowledge base synchronization. By far the most common cloud feature is the provision of SaaS hosting for traditional ILS catalogs by vendors and ILS hosting companies. Hosting for ILS systems can follow either a PaaS or SaaS model, depending on the division of management tasks between provider and customer. These systems are typically implemented as dedicated machines or on a virtualized server that is accessible only by the service provider and customer. In addition to commercial ILS vendors, there are also cloud-based support services for open-source ILS systems including Evergreen (http://open-ils.org) and Koha (http://koha.org). There are also consortiums that have used the open-source ILS platform as an opportunity to engage in statewide hosting. Of note are the Georgia Pines system (http://gapines.org) and an Indiana consortium of libraries (www.in.gov/library/evergreen.htm) that is using Evergreen on virtualized platforms.

SaaS hosting of traditional ILS platforms is different from the new comprehensive resource management platforms (CRMPs). These resource management systems more closely model a web-service-based SaaS environment. They often feature a shared bibliographic database, emphasize interoperability among systems and data sets, and provide access to both public and staff tools via graphical web services. Key vendors in this market include OCLC (WorldShare Management Services, www.oclc.org/webscale/), Ex-Libris (Alma, www.exlibrisgroup.com/category/AlmaOverview), and SerialsSolutions (Intota, www.serialssolutions.com/en/services/intota). OCLC’s foray into ILS and CRMP platforms comes from its long history of providing network-based services for libraries. Its introduction of a cloud-based ILS in 2010 marked a shift from providing elemental services such as MARC record management and authority services to providing comprehensive librarywide services. Recent examples of this service include systems at Pepperdine (Bryant and Ye 2012) and the University of Delaware (OCLC 2012).

Just as the information industry has found considerable value in data hosting and ownership, library automation vendors have found not only that libraries are willing to pay for a managed, cloud-based knowledge base but that this knowledge base can be repurposed for a variety of uses. OCLC’s product not only leverages the existing bibliographic and holdings database it has built over the past forty years but also uses community-created programs and externally subscribed data sets to bring together the features of the traditional ILS and the new management demands brought on by electronic resources. As our exploration of PaaS and IaaS services shows, shifting away from wholly traditional IT practice to “webscale” means blurring the lines between individual and community databases and data sets, redesigning services to optimize web interfaces, and embracing community-shared content.

Cloud-based digital archival management systems (DAMS) include products such as ContentDM (www.oclc.org/contentdm/), hosted open-source archiving services such as DuraCloud (http://duracloud.org), and DSpace hosting from Open Repository (www.openrepository.com), as well as digital publishing tools such as Omeka (http://omeka.net). As with ILS systems, commercial offerings are available as hosted services but are not necessarily designed as an SaaS platform. SaaS cloud support for these systems has not yet reached the maturity of ILS, CRMS, and other systems, perhaps due to the complexity and rapid evolution of DAMS platforms. At the same time, the DAMS poses challenges for libraries due to its relative complexity. In the DAMS open-source market it has become more common for development communities to publish test platforms using client-based virtual machines. These machine images are typically not designed for production installation because they hard-code passwords and integrate database, administrative, and public service functions into a single virtual machine. They are, however, an increasingly popular way for open-source projects to connect with potential adopters. Systems including Islandora (http://islandora.ca), Archivematica (http://archivematica.org), and DAITSS Digital Preservation Repository Software (http://daitss.fcla.edu) are available as simple downloads and can easily be imported into a virtual machine environment.

As use of digital preservation services has evolved, SaaS options for storage, bit checking, and other preservation services have been developed to help libraries manage digital objects. For example DuraCloud (http://duracloud.net) offers cloud broker, data preservation, data replication, and online sharing services for libraries based on the DSpace and Fedora platforms. Both DSpace and Fedora have built-in services that facilitate integration with DuraCloud.

Whereas DuraCloud focuses on preservation services, other platforms such as Omeka and Viewshare (http://viewshare.org) provide a platform for easy-to-use hosted digital publishing.
