With so many measures it can be difficult to understand which are essential and what the real meaning of uptime assurances is. As a good example, Conry-Murray (2009) documented uptime guarantees by different cloud providers in a 2009 report and found them ranging from 99.7 percent to 100 percent depending on provider. Though 99.7 percent might seem good enough, it leaves room for over two hours of downtime per month. Reese (2009, 5.5) rightly observes that even a short period of downtime may have major implications for information services. In addition, because cloud services are often based on multiple service providers, measuring service quality can be very uncertain. This difference in perspective became apparent when Amazon experienced service downtime in one zone early in 2011 and caused downtime for services that had not been designed to operate in multiple availability zones (Leong 2011). In fact, Amazon’s availability definition is only for services that operate in multiple availability zones, meaning that organizations are not fully supported unless they configure their cloud services in the specified manner.

An additional challenge with SLAs is the difficulty of working with multiple vendors and agreements while trying to implement a sustainable and consistent service architecture. Making this process more difficult is the fact that libraries can find it difficult, if not impossible, to negotiate the SLA with large cloud providers. IaaS providers in particular provide an SLA for a broad system infrastructure and are not in the business of providing tailored support. Although not always realistic, there are ways around this one-size-fits-all approach. First, government organizations are gaining access to specialized IaaS environments that operate under specialized terms through Amazon and other IaaS providers. It may pay to find a community cloud provider that recognizes issues unique to your institution. Additionally, educational organizations can often negotiate with corporations for enterprise-wide solutions or can choose to (in the case of Amazon) pay for a higher level of service support. Finally, paying attention to built-in support levels and the availability of customer service can help libraries find the right fit in an IaaS provider. These providers all offer similar services, but their approaches to customer service vary considerably.
