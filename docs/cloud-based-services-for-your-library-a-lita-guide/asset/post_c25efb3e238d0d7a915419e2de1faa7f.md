## Public-Facing Services

Libraries are increasingly turning to SaaS options for public-facing discovery, reference, and fulfillment services. Discovery platforms such as SerialsSolutions Summon, WorldCat Local (www.oclc.org/worldcatlocal/), and EBSCO Discovery Service (www.ebscohost.com/discovery) offer an integrated search platform that includes cloud-indexed print and electronic (licensed) resources. Although libraries have yet to abandon traditional catalogs entirely, the relative popularity of these new systems poses real challenges to locally managed discovery systems including the OPAC.

In addition to discovery services, libraries are turning to virtualized reference services such as OCLC’s QuestionPoint (http://questionpoint.org) or SpringShare’s LibAnswers (http://springshare.com/libanswers/) or to statewide services such as OhioLINK’s KnowItNow service (http://knowitnow.org). Libraries are also turning to self-service information portals such as LibGuides (http://libguides.com) and are leveraging consumer-grade multimedia services such as YouTube and Vimeo to integrate streaming video into online information resources. By deconstructing a complex information service into SaaS-based discrete components, librarians are able to create complex information services with little overhead. Companies including SpringShare are working to provide a diverse set of web services that fit this approach and could easily fill in the gaps left from enterprise DAMS and ILS. The most common form this approach takes is video-based instruction or tutorials. For example, Loo (2010) created and published a series of video tutorials on doing research using PubChem. The video series was recorded using simple client-based screen casting, is hosted on YouTube, and is tightly integrated into multiple web services on an as-needed basis.

As our review of SaaS solutions illustrates, it is realistic for libraries to assemble an entire IT suite using SaaS components. Each of the services represented in figure 4.1 has multiple robust SaaS cloud options, though options may be limited. It is worth noting that, although there are library-specific commercial providers in this

space, it is equally common to find open-source SaaS options for specific products. For example, there are hosted open-source products in the ILS, DAMS, and virtual reference service (VRS) product categories. It is less common to find open-source link resolvers and cloud-based discovery services, primarily because these services rely on licensed data sets that are difficult if not impossible to obtain outside of vendor contracts. In particular, these applications that are data-management intensive are good candidates for SaaS approaches because they result in savings at both the IT level and staffing levels in the rest of the organization.

## Community Virtualization/Cloud Providers

Cloud hosting for library-specific applications is offered by commercial providers such as Equinox (http://equinox.com) and ByWater Solutions (http://bywaterwsolutions.com) and by nonprofit providers such as Lyrasis (http://lyrasis.org). Many of these providers support multiple systems and open-source platforms. This approach is also being explored by Amigos Library Services, which is piloting a virtualized deployment of the Koha open-source library system with partner libraries (www.amigos.org/node/837). The Amigos project is considering both the technical aspects of virtualized service hosting and the logistics of providing data migration and other ILS-specific services in a hosted environment. Another library cooperative, Lyrasis, provides support for Evergreen, DSpace, and Drupal in

a community-based virtualization platform. Lyrasis started its work in virtualized hosting of these services for libraries based on the results of a member survey that found both an interest in open-source software and a need from libraries for infrastructure and hosting support for these software platforms. This work, which has also grown into an open-source site registry known as FOSS4LIB (http://foss4lib.org), has been directed at supporting library adoption of open-source software and filling member demand for specific platform needs.

It is less typical for providers like Lyrasis to provide on-demand or rapidly elastic services. Instead, Lyrasis works with clients to define a customized SLA, negotiate specific access terms and application management agreements, and work with more traditional payment and subscription terms than may be available in other cloud or virtualized platforms. These techniques are much more common from community cloud providers such as Lyrasis. These benefits can be a motivating factor for libraries seeking to leverage cloud-based and virtualized resources to implement new services or manage existing services. Another important advantage of community-specific cloud and virtualization providers of open-source software is improved ability to migrate between providers. For example, FOSS4LIB has four providers listed for the open-source ILS Koha, and Evergreen has multiple providers. Libraries seeking to change providers would likely not have significant issues with data migration and service setup as libraries faced with choosing new systems in addition to new providers.

## Issues

Despite the fact that there are a wide range of SaaS platforms for libraries seeking to move to the cloud, adoption of SaaS platforms is not without its own issues. Moving large elements of IT services to SaaS solutions can lead to a fragmented IT environment in which it is difficult to make systems work well together. This can lead to loss of data but more likely to loss of service control and data ownership or access. Likewise, as libraries move toward more data-rich systems, their ability to migrate away from these solutions diminishes. Because there are few standards governing the exiting of a library from these cloud services, vendor lock-in is becoming a significant concern, particularly given the fact that a new breed of tightly integrated knowledge bases and service layers is being developed and licensed to libraries as “black boxes.” In this licensing model it is easy for libraries to lose control of the resources that are included and excluded in the subscription. Libraries have become tolerant of this situation, but the growing centrality of

subscription resources means that these shifts are poised to have greater impact in the coming years.

Whether cost is an enabling or inhibiting factor depends on a wide range of issues including subscription scale, negotiations, local firm/soft costs, and other IT infrastructure elements. In fact, many libraries are using free services to test and in some cases deploy staff and public-facing services. Although free services are appealing, if libraries adopt them for production-level work they also need to engage in a careful study of the terms of use, exit options, and security system. There are, of course, cloud services such as Facebook, Flickr, and YouTube that are already common in libraries and are known for offering somewhat controversial terms of service to their users.

As libraries opt to spend more resources on outsourced or SaaS services as opposed to developing and maintaining these services in-house, they create an environment in which they may not have the expertise to migrate to new systems or completely capitalize on current systems. There are companies that offer IT support in an as-a-service subscription environment, but these services are often focused on common IT services such as network and client support services. Further, as libraries shift to SaaS solutions they increasingly need monitoring and auditing services that help them ensure that they are both receiving all of their subscribed services and in compliance with organizational policy and legal regulations. As of now there is no service that offers this type of support and few models in the library world that would support such as service. We see some examples of these types of services in chapter 5 as we explore the implementation of IaaS solutions.

## Expertise Required

Expertise in adopting and managing SaaS solutions is rather different from that of other platforms. Familiarity with license negotiation and organizational policy becomes a key issue. By turning to a vendor for a wholly hosted service, libraries open themselves up to more legal and privacy issues and need a more solid foundation in negotiating skill. In addition, the key IT skills required for libraries that make extensive use of SaaS solutions include the ability to design and manage metadata and web services that enable system interoperability. This may include working with campus IT to enable system authentication or access and is likely to include staff training and project management tasks. Finally, SaaS solutions often require libraries to have a firm understanding of authentication, access (e.g., proxy access for off-campus users), service management, and problem resolution processes and third-party negotiation.
