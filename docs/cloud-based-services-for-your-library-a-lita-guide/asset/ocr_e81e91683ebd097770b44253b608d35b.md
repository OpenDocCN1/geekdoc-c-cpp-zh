Addy (2007, 50) provides a full list of areas that require definition, including users, user requirements, expected deliverables, information or data provided by user, methods of service delivery, roles and responsibilities, contractual requirements, measures of success, definitions of service availability, measures of service capacity, security standards, and cost requirement/fulfillment. Although readers who are at the stage of incorporating formal IT service management approaches are best served by exploring each of these areas in more depth, it is important to be aware of these issues, if even informally, as your organization explores cloud solutions.

Each of the IT service models mentioned here work well in a cloud environment, but some are exploring issues specific to cloud adoption. The Microsoft Operations Framework (2011), for example, explores which system elements (e.g., networking, storage, data, applications) are locally or remotely managed in SaaS, PaaS, and IaaS environments. The best-practice recommendations for adopting common IT service guidelines to a cloud environment include changes to communication, servicing, and problem resolution. For example, the Microsoft guidelines suggest adding elemental cloud services to the service catalog to ensure that users understand what core cloud services are available and what their implementation costs and parameters are. Having documentation about the cloud platforms with which an organization has experience can help guide discussions and decisions surrounding IT service adoption and may prevent the use of multiple cloud service providers in a disjointed way. Microsoft's guidelines also suggest automating service and leveraging the self-service features of cloud platforms where possible.

For libraries engaged in a reappraisal of IT services, exploring a shift to cloud resources, or simply interested in finding ways to redesign their IT service approach, an understanding of IT service models and standards can help guide organizational change. This may lead to a centralized library IT, particularly in organizations that turn to PaaS or IaaS solutions, or it may lead to a more decentralized IT environment. In both cases the use of some form of service catalog, knowledge management system, and service ticket system can help libraries keep track of IT developments and make well-informed decisions about changes to their IT services. Despite the motivation to design the perfect system, system administrators should be tuned to the risk of overcommitting to complex system configurations and management practices.
