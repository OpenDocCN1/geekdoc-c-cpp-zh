# Next Generation Cloud-Based ILS

It is increasingly common to see ILS vendors advertize cloud computing as a key aspect of their ILS. This may include a wholly cloud-based solution or simply virtualized hosting or knowledge base synchronization. By far the most common cloud feature is the provision of SaaS hosting for traditional ILS catalogs by vendors and ILS hosting companies. Hosting for ILS systems can follow either a PaaS or SaaS model, depending on the division of management tasks between provider and customer. These systems are typically implemented as dedicated machines or on a virtualized server that is accessible only by the service provider and customer. In addition to commercial ILS vendors, there are also cloud-based support services for open-source ILS systems including Evergreen (http://open-ils.org) and Koha (http://koha.org). There are also consortiums that have used the open-source ILS platform as an opportunity to engage in statewide hosting. Of note are the Georgia Pines system (http://gapines.org) and an Indiana consortium of libraries (www.in.gov/library/evergreen.htm) that is using Evergreen on virtualized platforms.

SaaS hosting of traditional ILS platforms is different from the new comprehensive resource management platforms (CRMPs). These resource management systems more closely model a web-service-based SaaS environment. They often feature a shared bibliographic database, emphasize interoperability among systems and data sets, and provide access to both public and staff tools via graphical web services. Key vendors in this market include OCLC (WorldShare Management Services, www.oclc.org/webscale/), Ex-Libris (Alma, www.exlibrisgroup.com/category/AlmaOverview), and SerialsSolutions (Inota, www.serialssolutions.com/en/services/intota). OCLC’s foray into ILS and CRMP platforms comes from its long history of providing network-based services for libraries. Its introduction of a cloud-based ILS in 2010 marked a shift from providing elemental services such as MARC record management and authority services to providing comprehensive librarywide services. Recent examples of this service include systems at Pepperdine (Bryant and Ye 2012) and the University of Delaware (OCLC 2012).

Just as the information industry has found considerable value in data hosting and ownership, library automation vendors have found not only that libraries are willing to pay for a managed, cloud-based knowledge base but that this knowledge base can be repurposed for a variety of uses. OCLC’s product not only leverages the existing bibliographic and holdings database it has built over the past forty years but also uses community-created programs and externally subscribed data sets to bring together the features of the traditional ILS and the new management demands brought on by electronic resources.
