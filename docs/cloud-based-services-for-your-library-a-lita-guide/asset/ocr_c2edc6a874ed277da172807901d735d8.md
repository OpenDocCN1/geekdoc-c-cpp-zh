For example, the AWS platform operates largely as a public cloud solution but is designed so that customer IT resources are virtually isolated from Amazon employees and other subscribers. Hybrid clouds are the “catch-all” of models in that they include features of two or more types of cloud systems (public, private, or community) but are bound together for data or application sharing.

There are both risks and advantages of scale that go with each deployment approach. For instance, in environments where physical security and virtual privacy are of utmost importance, public clouds are likely not the best solution. Likewise, where economies of scale and interoperability are important, private clouds are not likely to be best suited. It is important to be familiar with these models; understanding them helps contextualize options for cloud computing adoption. Good explanations that focus on the intersection between service models and deployment models have been prepared by various authors (Mell and Grance 2011; Metz 2011) and further inform on the differences between them.

In addition to understanding the distinction between service and deployment models, it is important to understand the roles of cloud providers and subscribers. These include common roles such as cloud adopter and cloud provider as well as emerging roles such as cloud auditor and cloud broker.
