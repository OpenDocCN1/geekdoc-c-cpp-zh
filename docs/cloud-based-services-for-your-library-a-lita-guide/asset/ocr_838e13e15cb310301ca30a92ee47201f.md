# REFERENCES

An important element of cloud computing design and adoption is a full understanding of the additional IT architecture elements required in a cloud environment. Unfortunately, libraries that choose to take on their own IT service management responsibilities run the risk of failing to capitalize on the benefits of cloud computing. Regardless of the solution adopted, it is important to understand the service assurances a provider makes, the requirements a platform must meet to be protected under those assurances, the backup and service restoration options available, and the required administrative activities (e.g., infrastructure, platform, application). For example, because cloud computing often uses commodity hardware, extra attention should be paid to system architecture to ensure that system migration, failover, and restoration are replicable and sustainable. Likewise, it is important to design cloud services that automate these processes as much as possible. These elements are important; still, it is worth keeping in mind the adage “The perfect is the enemy of the good.”

Although the process of adopting cloud computing or virtualized solutions is largely similar to the selection and implementation of any IT service, evaluating cloud-based alternatives and fitting the selected service into the wider library IT infrastructure are important steps that tend to add complication. In general, a new cloud platform or service should be evaluated by considering the goal of an information service; available staff, IT, and financial resources to support the service; availability of cloud-based (e.g., SaaS, PaaS, IaaS, DaaS) platforms; and how these alternatives fit with local resources and goals.

The issues explored here can seem overwhelming, but testing a small and minimal-impact service can mitigate the technical complexities, legal/policy risks, and service costs. For those interested in systematic system implementation and management guides, detailed plans are available in Limoncelli et al. (2001), Velte et al. (2009), and Rittinghouse and Ransome (2009).
