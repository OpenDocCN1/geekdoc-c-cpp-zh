The NIST model also defines the limits of expertise and responsibility that should be expected of the various players in a cloud computing solution. It is worth noting that the roles of consumer, auditor, provider, carrier, and broker may not apply in all cloud computing adoption frameworks; nevertheless, they are valuable roles to consider when exploring new IT systems. For example, in many cases libraries (e.g., the cloud consumer) interact directly with cloud providers rather than working through a cloud broker. In this case the process of working with and auditing service layers, security, privacy, and service-level agreements (SLAs) falls on the cloud consumer. The NIST model also provides a taxonomy of cloud features (Fang et al. 2011) that describes the issues cloud consumers should be aware of when selecting and implementing a cloud solution. This taxonomy defines the activities (e.g., service creation, publishing, auditing), components (e.g., processes, actions, tasks), and subcomponents (e.g., service provision, service decommission) that may be helpful for organizations seeking to understand relationships and interactions important in cloud computing adoption.

## Virtualization

In contrast to cloud computing, virtualization is defined as “the abstraction of IT resources in a way that masks the physical nature and boundaries of those resources from resource users” (Gartner 2011). Virtualization enables creation of multiple virtual machines on a shared hardware platform; enhances flexibility in CPU, memory, and storage allocations between machines; and is designed to be portable across hardware platforms (Smith and Nair 2005). This is a broad definition that includes the virtualization of servers, clients, network-attached storage, and any number of other computing resources. Common server platforms include Xen (http://xen.org), Citrix (http://citrix.com), and VMware (http://vmware.com). Each virtualization platform has historically supported its own virtualization format (e.g., Kernel Virtual Machine, Bare Metal, VMware VMDK), but there is also a movement to support an open virtualization standard that would enable easy interoperability among virtualization platforms. These solutions can be a valuable tool for libraries, but they often come with a requirement for technical infrastructure and expertise that commonly is not held by libraries.

## Factors Influencing Service Choices

The shift in IT implementation practices away from traditional in-house solutions to cloud-based, hosted, or outsourced solutions means that libraries must consider new issues when making IT selections. Fortunately, libraries have practice in working with contract negotiation, online service evaluation, data access and preservation, subscription pricing models, and network-based authentication practices from their experience in migrating to digital subscription-based research tools and e-journals (Zambare et al. 2009). This is particularly true in the case of library electronic content licenses, which often include assurances on technical elements of the service. Key aspects of the standard license terms for resources licensed by the California Digital Library, for example, include server availability and performance criteria, authentication and authorization practices, confidentiality and privacy policies, customer support provisions, and access to data criteria (Lavery 2010). Similar provisions in cloud computing and hosting licenses include SLAs; security and access provisions; data integrity guarantees; data export and migration support; and confidentiality, privacy, and legal indemnification policies (Zhou et al. 2010).

Although libraries seeking to implement these new data-rich and network-centric services can benefit from being able to look outside traditional in-house IT solutions for hosting and platform support, they often find themselves exchanging one set of complications for another. New factors to be considered when evaluating a cloud-based service provider include security, confidentiality and privacy, legal liability, and organizational stability. It is important to recognize that these technologies are still in their early stages and are defined more by “best-practice” approaches and less by accepted standards. In addition to NIST, organizations including the Cloud Security Alliance (https://cloudsecurityalliance.org), Open Grid Forum (www.ogf.org), Open Cloud Computing Interface (OCCI; http://occi-wg.org), and Organization for the Advancement of Structured Information Standards (www.oasis-open.org) are working on turning these best-practice approaches into industry standards. At this point libraries are likely to have to make choices with an understanding of the impact proprietary and nonstandard practices will have as they migrate to cloud solutions. Although libraries have historically worked with proprietary systems, the rapid deployment and migration that often accompanies virtualized and cloud services means that adherence to standards and practices that ensure data and service portability, as well as data backup and recovery, are considerations that need to be part of the discussion of cloud and virtualization adoption.

## Genres of Cloud-Based or Virtualized Information Services

Cloud computing can be grouped into categories by service type (e.g., SaaS, PaaS, IaaS, DaaS), described using specific features (e.g., subscription, metered, scalable), and used in different deployment models (e.g., public, private, community, hybrid). As our initial exploration of these relationships shows, it can be difficult to compare IT systems from a functional perspective, let alone understand the implications of choosing an IT platform along with a system. In addition, libraries turning to outsourced and hosted solutions need to be prepared to evaluate the technological foundation of those services. Therefore, having a firm understanding of the basic functions of these cloud platforms is crucial in making an informed decision.

Libraries support a wide range of information systems including ILSs, link resolvers, digital asset management systems (DAMS), websites, research guides, internal intranets, and archival information systems. Cloud computing works well for most libraries and library services, but the right balance of platform, deployment environment, and adoption goals naturally drives libraries toward different solutions. It can be difficult to understand how virtualized and cloud services support functional IT needs such as preservation-quality network storage, development and production computing platforms, and acquisition and data management needs.
