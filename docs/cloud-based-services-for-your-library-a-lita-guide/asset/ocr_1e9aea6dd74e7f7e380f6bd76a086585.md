Whether cost is an enabling or inhibiting factor depends on a wide range of issues including subscription scale, negotiations, local firm/soft costs, and other IT infrastructure elements. In fact, many libraries are using free services to test and in some cases deploy staff and public-facing services. Although free services are appealing, if libraries adopt them for production-level work they also need to engage in a careful study of the terms of use, exit options, and security system. There are, of course, cloud services such as Facebook, Flickr, and YouTube that are already common in libraries and are known for offering somewhat controversial terms of service to their users.

As libraries opt to spend more resources on outsourced or SaaS services as opposed to developing and maintaining these services in-house, they create an environment in which they may not have the expertise to migrate to new systems or completely capitalize on current systems. There are companies that offer IT support in an as-a-service subscription environment, but these services are often focused on common IT services such as network and client support services. Further, as libraries shift to SaaS solutions they increasingly need monitoring and auditing services that help them ensure that they are both receiving all of their subscribed services and in compliance with organizational policy and legal regulations. As of now there is no service that offers this type of support and few models in the library world that would support such as service. We see some examples of these types of services in chapter 5 as we explore the implementation of IaaS solutions.

# Expertise Required

Expertise in adopting and managing SaaS solutions is rather different from that of other platforms. Familiarity with license negotiation and organizational policy becomes a key issue. By turning to a vendor for a wholly hosted service, libraries open themselves up to more legal and privacy issues and need a more solid foundation in negotiating skill. In addition, the key IT skills required for libraries that make extensive use of SaaS solutions include the ability to design and manage metadata and web services that enable system interoperability. This may include working with campus IT to enable system authentication or access and is likely to include staff training and project management tasks. Finally, SaaS solutions often require libraries to have a firm understanding of authentication, access (e.g., proxy access for off-campus users), service management, and problem resolution processes and third-party negotiation.
