Libraries interested in using free cloud services may find that the default terms of the service pose significant barriers in relation to FERPA, HIPPA, or USA PATRIOT Act regulations. In paid cloud services there are opportunities to negotiate these terms, and libraries can minimize risk by adopting cloud platforms that avoid thorny privacy policy terms or by blocking provider access to subscriber data.

Navigating this range of terms, policies, and agreements can be difficult, if only because of the number of ways agreements can be published or made available. In fact, organizations do not always publish distinct documents and may include privacy policy issues in terms of service document and vice versa (Bradshaw et al. 2010). Outside of the terms of service and acceptable use policies, libraries face legal and policy issues when examining cloud-based or virtualized IT solutions. Although these issues are not largely different from those encountered in a traditional IT environment, cloud computing brings the complications of working with an external vendor. A survey of new IT solutions should include examining government regulations (e.g., HIPPA, FERPA, Patriot Act), software and service licensing issues, and IT standards compliance (Reese 2009, 63). In addition, Gartner’s Hype Cycle report (Smith 2010) focuses on privacy issues including personal data and the implications of hardware ownership in public clouds. Jaeger et al. (2009) add data confidentiality and privacy, liability, intellectual property, ownership of data, and auditability as concerns. Trappler (2010) mentions other legal and policy issues including the disposal of data, handling of security breaches, geographic or political location of data, and compliance with legal or governmental requests for data.

These issues are international in scope and complicated by the fact that cloud computing environments can span multiple jurisdictions. Resse (2009, 104) explores three broad factors that should be considered for a given regulation: “How” (e.g., how does an application need to operate to comply with a standard), “Where” (e.g., where can data be stored), and “What” (e.g., what specific elements need to be included in an infrastructure). This approach can help libraries dissect an agreement and better understand the implications.

Cloud computing is also creating difficulties for organizations seeking to use tools that may be located in other countries.
