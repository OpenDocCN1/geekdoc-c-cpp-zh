## MODEL FOR EVALUATING VIRTUALIZED TECHNOLOGY ADOPTION IN LIBRARIES

Our data show that libraries are using cloud and virtualized platforms in different ways, but that overall the view of these technologies is positive. Whereas our review of literature found a wide range of issues one should consider when moving to an internally or externally sourced virtual solution, participants in this study focused on issues of vendor compliance, cost-benefit analysis, and positive impacts of cloud computing on efficiency and flexibility rather than on security, business continuity, privacy, or liability. Likewise, the high frequency of libraries citing a positive view of virtualization and cloud platforms but expressing hesitancy given current local IT infrastructure points to a real need for library and perhaps organizational IT departments to expand their capacity to support cloud-based services. Some other common barriers to cloud adoption included the presence of legacy systems, vendor requirements that prevent virtualization or cloud adoption, internal organizational issues, current investment in technology, and lack of expertise. Despite these inhibiting factors, primary motivations tended to be perceived improvement in service, interest in resource (cost, staff time) efficiency, and interest in new services. These factors could prove to be good measures of whether a cloud or virtualized platform is a good fit for a given service and could certainly help libraries make educated decisions about the potential advantage or liability of cloud choices.

Perhaps the most difficult part of moving to any IT solution is understanding how to weigh the positive and negative issues surrounding a platform. Some participants used their qualitative response to discuss a decision process or compare issues in this manner. For example, one library recognized the risk of vendor lock-in when moving to SaaS platforms but indicated that service efficiency outweighed the risk. Likewise, several libraries indicated interest in migrating but were moving slowly to be sure that they adopted a platform only after the market had tested its viability. Despite some rich exploration of organizational and environmental issues, there was little discussion of the roles of networked data, open data, or open-source software in making IT decisions. Likewise, security and privacy issues, though often represented as key concerns in the literature (Grant 2011, 47; Velte et al. 2009, 35), were not discussed at the same level of specificity as technical and organizational issues. This emphasis may well be due to the population or the style of the questions, but it raises a good question about professional perceptions of legal, policy, and security concerns in virtualized and cloud computing environments.

Although the primary goal of this research was to help identify a model that libraries could use to evaluate their own level of cloud adoption and consider the factors that are influencing their IT decisions, it is worth comparing our results with other cloud computing adoption studies. For example, a 2011 study by the Aberdeen Group found that among cloud adopters 68 percent had turned to private cloud and internal virtualization services and 50 percent had turned to SaaS solutions (Brink 2011, 2). Like Brink’s study, the research presented in this chapter also found higher rates of private cloud and SaaS adoption and much lower rates of PaaS (22 percent) and IaaS (31 percent) adoption. Overall, Brink found much higher overall adoption of cloud computing (81 percent) than was found in published studies that included assessment of library cloud computing use. For example, the Library Resource Guide report on funding priorities in libraries found an overall cloud computing adoption level of 7 percent in libraries in 2011 and 12 percent in 2012 (McKendrick 2012). In contrast, we found an adoption rate over 90 percent.

The Library Resource Guide survey also found that libraries were tending not to turn to IT as a means for saving money; only 13 percent of respondents indicated that they would reduce IT expenditures in 2011 and 11 percent in 2012. Similarly, that study found that only 1 percent of libraries were planning to increase spending on cloud computing in the coming year (McKendrick 2012, 11, 15).

The variation between findings, at the least, points to a need for more formal study of cloud computing adoption rates. The results may also indicate a gap between library views of cloud computing and their movement in funding cloud initiatives.
