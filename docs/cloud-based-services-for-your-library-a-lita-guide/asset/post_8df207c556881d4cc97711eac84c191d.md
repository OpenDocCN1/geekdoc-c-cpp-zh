## SURVEY FRAMEWORK

Understanding the factors that enable and inhibit IT change can be an important element of change management and can facilitate the exploration of tools and their fit with your library. There are several ways to approach this discussion, but two models in particular inform organizational understanding of technology adoption: the “technology acceptance model” (TAM; Davis 1989) and the “technology, organization and environments model” (TOE; Tornatzky et al. 1990). These models provide frameworks for understanding the relationship between technologies and their use by individuals or organizations. Another model often used in technology adoption studies is “diffusion of innovation theory” (Rogers 2003), which explores technology adoption by examining factors including advantage, compatibility with other systems, and system complexity and focuses on qualifying adoption patterns. In contrast, the TOE and TAM models explore factors that influence technology adoption and more directly speak to the types of organizational decision making that are key in library IT decisions.

These models take somewhat different approaches to understanding technology adoption. The TAM model explores the natural tension between the perceived usefulness and ease of use of technologies. In contrast to TAM’s perception-based model, the TOE model examines various contexts that influence technology adoption (e.g., organizational and environmental). These two perspectives help shed light on factors that influence individual and organizational assessment of the value of technologies (e.g., ease of use, perceived value, social context, organizational values). Specifically, the TAM model provides a structure for understanding the individual, social, and technological context surrounding technology adoption. In contrast, the TOE model provides a framework for understanding the interaction of organizational, technological, and external (to the organization) factors.

Previous studies have also used these models to explore cloud adoption in educational and technology environments. For example, the TAM model has been used to examine the factors that influence cloud computing adoption in community college systems (Behrend et al. 2011), and the TOE model has been used to explore the organizational factors that influence the adoption of technology in different contexts (Daylami et al. 2005). The inclusion of both models helps frame the research in terms of the participant’s perceptions of cloud computing and situates their use of cloud computing within the organization. The analysis of survey responses looked for elements of each model, including personal views of ease of use/value (i.e., TAM) and personal views of environmental, organizational, and technical factors (i.e., TOE) in relation to four computing platforms. Figure 2.1 includes four categories of virtualized/cloud computing that were created using the NIST model. These four models (nonvirtualized, internal noncloud or private cloud virtualization, public or community cloud virtualization, and outsourced) represent a mix of deployment contexts (e.g., public, community, private) and service models (SaaS, PaaS, IaaS) and were selected because they have common real-world examples in library IT environments.

One of the challenges with this study framework was finding a suitable way to categorize cloud platforms that made sense to a large population of participants. Although the study focused on library IT personnel, the population surveyed included a mix of highly technical and nontechnical librarians. To control for this variation, the categories were informally tested with technical and nontechnical librarians and refined to be as understandable as possible. In this study framework, nonvirtualized refers to traditional library IT systems that run on dedicated hardware within the organization. In contrast, internally virtualized or private cloud refers to IT systems that are run within the organization but use a specific form of virtualization. It is important to differentiate the virtualization of multiple machines on a single hardware stack (virtualization) from a new range of software platforms that “unify server, storage and network resources into a common resource pool” (Marko 2012, 4). The study grouped external cloud computing adoption into two genres, “public/community cloud” and “outsourced.” In category testing with librarians the term outsourced, though not truly representative of the SaaS technical concept, was more representative of the form of SaaS solutions adopted by libraries and was a commonly understood concept among nontechnical librarians. ## The Landscape of Cloud Computing and Virtualization Adoption

Three research questions examined the adoption of cloud computing and virtualization in libraries and explored the factors IT librarians consider when evaluating these technologies and their use in libraries:

- What is the landscape and state of cloud computing and virtualized technology adoption in libraries? What services are libraries providing using cloud- and virtual-based platforms? What types of cloud- and virtual-based platforms are being used in libraries?
- What enabling and inhibiting factors are identified by librarians in relation to the adoption of cloud computing?
- What actions would enhance the adoption of cloud computing in libraries? How could these actions be designed or implemented?

## METHODS

The study was designed and administered using an online survey platform (see www.alaeditions.org/webextras for the full survey). Participants were solicited from a range of library IT discussion boards including Code4Lib, Lita-L, SYSLIB-L, and WEB4LIB. The total number of participating libraries was 111. Participants answered a range of questions including demographic information on library size and focus, questions about the implementation of information services (e.g., ILS and OpenURL resolver), and motivations/concerns regarding cloud computing adoption. Survey data was analyzed to discover common themes using the framework presented in figure 2.1.

A mixed-method approach was valuable in this study because it provided both baseline descriptive data on the state of cloud computing and virtualized technology adoption in libraries and higher-level data surrounding the subjective motivations and enabling/inhibiting factors for librarians making decisions about technology adoption. The survey included quantitative data on the type of services provided by libraries using cloud platforms and qualitative data on the enabling and inhibiting factors related to decision making in IT for libraries.
