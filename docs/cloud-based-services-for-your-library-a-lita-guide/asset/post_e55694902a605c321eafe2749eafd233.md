## CONCLUSION

This survey of virtualization, cloud computing, and outsourcing adoption in libraries both underscores the current interest in cloud computing and virtualization and demonstrates which views are prevalent among adopters. The gaps found between library and IT literature and survey participants indicate that the questions about cloud computing and virtualization may be different for different audiences or perhaps are shifting as cloud computing matures. The survey also demonstrates a growing reliance on outsourced library IT as opposed to PaaS or IaaS approaches and indicates a slow but growing trend to migrate older and core library systems (e.g., the ILS) to these environments. These survey results should not be overgeneralized, but the evaluative model represented in figure 2.1 and the grouped themes presented in tables 2.4 and 2.5 may help libraries understand their own IT environment in more detail.

## Selecting and Implementing Solutions

In this chapter we look at technical, organizational, legal, and staffing/expertise issues in more detail and consider library-specific factors that influence adoption of cloud-based services. This examination is based on the responses of participants in the chapter 2 survey, on approaches described in the literature on cloud computing (e.g., Reese 2009; Rittinghouse and Ransome 2009), and on case studies of library adoption of cloud computing and virtualization (Fox 2009; Han 2010; Mitchell 2010).

Our survey found variation in cloud computing adoption rates and different motivating and inhibiting factors with regards to organizational issues, technical/functional requirements, and perceived benefits. This points to the likelihood that there is not a “one-size-fits-all” solution for cloud computing in libraries but rather a need to find the best fit for each institution. Although it was difficult to compare the context of cloud adoption between libraries and other information organizations, there was a clear emphasis on privately virtualized or completely outsourced (e.g., SaaS) approaches in both our survey and other research reported in chapter 2. There were common themes mentioned that contextualized this trend, including lack of IT support or local expertise and concerns over the ability to sustain complex IT systems. These concerns are significant given the fact that as libraries are adopting new information services they are often faced with choices that require distinguishing between cloud, virtualized, and traditional IT approaches. Breeding (2011) work in particular has focused on this issue in regards to the ILS. He found not only a continued shift toward cloud solutions using

SaaS and PaaS platforms but also a re-envisioning of the structure of these systems including changing approaches to bibliographic and other data management and a focus on data and service interoperability. In evaluating these systems libraries must understand both the IT service and functional implications of their decisions. In addition, as libraries adopt or migrate to cloud-based platforms, new issues such as service quality and service-level agreements (SLAs) and new takes on traditional issues such as vendor lock-in and system functionality need to be considered.

## Chapter Three

One goal of this book is to give librarians the tools they need to assess and compare cloud, virtualized, and nonvirtualized IT environments. By having a strong understanding of the types of virtualization and cloud computing that are available along with the required support services that come with these platforms, librarians are better prepared to make well-informed system selections. This chapter focuses on issues related to technology adoption as opposed to system functionality, but the latter is a very real concern as well. In weighing the technical differences between systems and organizational/environmental factors it is also worth considering the relationships between organizational units, IT systems, IT services, and end-user information needs. In IT service environments, for example, there is considerable focus on the relationships between capacity (both technical and organizational), risk management, and service or end-user goals (Addy 2007, 45). Libraries should not assume that their view of these elements, or even their definition, is identical to their counterparts’ in organizational IT. Schaffhauser (2010) observes that libraries define service and assess value, impact, and risk using different measures than IT departments and differentiates library and IT perspectives on an “end user.” This difference can have a cascading effect on library IT and can be a cause of discord between library-centric and organization-wide IT departments. Although the move to cloud or virtualized platforms may be seen as a way to bridge these differences, it can also lead to more complicated and interdependent relationships.

One way of bridging this gap is for libraries to select service assessment models that share common ground with IT departments while also leaving room for these differences. Despite the fact that they may start with an IT-centric world view, IT service models are useful in this area because they include ways to differentiate internal and external customers and include considerations for capacity and risk while keeping a focus on user service expectations.
