These four approaches emphasize different strengths and weaknesses in cloud solutions and can serve as a starting point for libraries turning to the cloud. Approach one emphasizes the simplicity and control that comes with using a single cloud provider but recognizes the risk associated with being locked in through extensive use of provider APIs. Approach two seeks to mitigate some of this risk by using multiple providers and leveraging SaaS solutions to monitor, manage, and audit these providers. This approach illustrates the benefit of keeping track of cloud infrastructure and expenditures and suggests that SaaS solutions are a way to provide infrastructure management support. Approach three focuses on services that seek to abstract system design and implementation from IaaS providers and demonstrates the benefits of being able to use multiple cloud platforms easily; still, it is complex and likely to require IT and monetary resources beyond those of approaches one and two. Approach four recognizes that libraries may very likely turn to entirely SaaS-based solutions to address technology needs. In this case operational, administrative, and service quality issues are more important than technical issues.

In the real world, it is likely that libraries will employ more than one of these approaches. In fact, most libraries already rely to some extent on an SaaS provider or outsourced information service. In deciding whether to expand those roles or to consider other approaches when moving new services to the cloud, issues of local resources, expertise, IT support capacity, organizational policy, and service need are useful factors that can make one approach more viable than another. Regardless of the approach selected, IT service elements such as capacity planning, quality of service monitoring, service integration, and resource utilization monitoring are important elements of a sustainable IT infrastructure. The remainder of this chapter examines these issues and provides suggestions for getting started with cloud computing.

## PREPARING FOR CLOUD COMPUTING ADOPTION

Library IT departments have grown in response to specific library needs such as client support, ILS support, and DAMS support. It is still common to find IT librarians with backgrounds outside IT disciplines, but there is also a trend documented in library conferences and literature surrounding the adoption of industry-standard IT software development and service management approaches (Chang 2010; Dougherty 2009). These articles discuss the fact that libraries are adopting IT service models including formal models such as Information Technology Infrastructure Library (ITIL) (www.itil-officialsite.com) and less formal models such as ITSM (www.itsm.info/ITSM.htm). Even for libraries that have not used such models, there is use of service management portals (McHale 2010) and a growing awareness of the responsibility of community-driven software development (Reese 2012).

As our exploration of cloud approaches emphasizes, there is a need for formal IT management practices. For libraries turning mostly to SaaS solutions, the emphasis on IT management should be on maintaining an intranet of service providers and support information. For libraries turning more to PaaS and IaaS solutions, a more formal support strategy helps make the implementation and management of solutions sustainable. There are a variety of models from which to choose, including ITIL, the Systems Development Lifecycle (Department of Justice 2003), Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology (ISACA 2011), and Microsoft Operations Framework (Microsoft 2011). These models help IT organizations approach system selection, development, implementation, management, and decommissioning of IT services in systematic ways and are helpful for libraries of any type. Although each model has its own distinct features, there have been attempts to find parallel processes and common ground (Pollard et al. 2009; Susanto et al. 2011) among them. Critics of these models point out that IT service model adoption has not demonstrated improvements in IT service and assert that they create administrative overhead and can lead to the creation of arbitrary boundaries (Addy 2007, 4).

Service management is a broad concept, defined as "a set of specialized organizational capabilities for providing value to customers in the form of services" (ITIL 2011, 11). The ITIL service life cycle describes the process of developing, delivering, and managing services and focuses in particular on knowledge management, SLAs, and standardization. Service management models can provide standardized measures of success including time to resolution, ratio of open and closed incidents, number of supported services, and time commitment of IT staff.

The ITIL service model, for example, discusses three stages of service: development, transition to production, and operational support. Each of these processes involves a wide range of discrete service components including defining service-level requirements, appraising IT support capacity, defining security requirements, and documenting user expectations. Addy (2007, 50) provides a full list of areas that require definition, including users, user requirements, expected deliverables, information or data provided by user, methods of service delivery, roles and responsibilities, contractual requirements, measures of success, definitions of service availability, measures of service capacity, security standards, and cost requirement/fulfillment. Although readers who are at the stage of incorporating formal IT service management approaches are best served by exploring each of these areas in more depth, it is important to be aware of these issues, if even informally, as your organization explores cloud solutions.

Each of the IT service models mentioned here work well in a cloud environment, but some are exploring issues specific to cloud adoption. The Microsoft Operations Framework (2011), for example, explores which system elements (e.g., networking, storage, data, applications) are locally or remotely managed in SaaS, PaaS, and IaaS environments. The best-practice recommendations for adopting common IT service guidelines to a cloud environment include changes to communication, servicing, and problem resolution. For example, the Microsoft guidelines suggest adding elemental cloud services to the service catalog to ensure that users understand what core cloud services are available and what their implementation costs and parameters are. Having documentation about the cloud platforms with which an organization has experience can help guide discussions and decisions surrounding IT service adoption and may prevent the use of multiple cloud service providers in a disjointed way. Microsoft's guidelines also suggest automating service and leveraging the self-service features of cloud platforms where possible.

For libraries engaged in a reappraisal of IT services, exploring a shift to cloud resources, or simply interested in finding ways to redesign their IT service approach, an understanding of IT service models and standards can help guide organizational change. This may lead to a centralized library IT, particularly in organizations that turn to PaaS or IaaS solutions, or it may lead to a more decentralized IT environment. In both cases the use of some form of service catalog, knowledge management system, and service ticket system can help libraries keep track of IT developments and make well-informed decisions about changes to their IT services. Despite the motivation to design the perfect system, system administrators should be attuned to the risk of overcommitting to complex system configurations and management practices. In fact, by using cloud environments to host service catalogs and knowledge management and ticket management systems, library IT departments can both capitalize on the efficiency benefits of SaaS solutions and demonstrate that they are willing to "eat their own dog food." The exercise of moving these internal services to the cloud or of deploying them in the cloud in the first place can also be a good testing ground for the implications of cloud service adoption in a library environment.

Although it is difficult to quantify the service capacity in an IT service department, the measures and frameworks that are part of IT service models can help measure the use of applications and resources. Likewise, when applied judiciously, these IT service models can expand the strengths of an IT department, enabling a small IT staff to manage an onslaught of requests more easily. Perhaps the most important principle of these service models is the view that IT service providers set boundaries and expectations that work for both customers and service providers. Although this is often formalized in SLAs, those agreements do not necessarily need to be complex and at the least can help avoid miscommunication and mismatches in customer expectations.

### Make Room for New Services

For organizations exploring the cloud, one of the important questions is how cloud computing can help libraries support new or different services. There are several important IT and organizational issues to consider here, including service requirements, user expectations, IT platform options, cost considerations, and legal/organizational policies. Understanding where constraints and surpluses exist may help guide the discussion and help organizations understand where their energy is best allocated. For example, staffing constraints and lack of expertise may mean that libraries are tempted to turn to SaaS solutions. As the previous section points out, the use of SaaS solutions does not eliminate the need for IT service management tasks. In contrast, as libraries turn to more SaaS solutions, the need for a cross-departmental service catalog and knowledge/ticket management system increases. This need is even greater in organizations where the licensing, management, and deployment of SaaS services are distributed among multiple departments.
