
## CONGRUENTIAL METHODS

As an example, suppose $d=4$ and the initial 4-digit seed is $x_0=1234$. Then $x_0^2 = 01522756$ gives $x_1=5227$ as the next integer in sequence. This leads to $x_1^2 = 2732152$ that produces $x_2 = 3215$.

The middle-square algorithm does not generate numbers that are "random" in any sense. But, it does produce numbers with the characteristics of random numbers to have been made useful for Neumann's particular application.

Streams of numbers created with the middle-square technique would not meet today's standards for random number generation. The use of a deterministic algorithm to produce numbers that possessed many of the qualities of random numbers was the breakthrough that set the course for modern generation. The next step down this path was made by Lehmer (1944, 1954) who pioneered the development of the linear congruential random number generators, which represent the topic of this section.

A linear congruential generator requires an initial seed that will be seeded by $x_0$. A sequence of pseudo-random integers is then generated via recursion

```
$x_i = (ax_{i-1} + c) \pmod m, \ i=1, \dots,$
```

where $a, c$ and $m$ are all integers. The integer $m$ is called the modulus of the generator while $a$ and $c$ are referred to as the multiplier and increment, respectively.

For two integers $a$ and $b$ the expression $a \bmod b$ refers to the integer remainder after division of $a$ by $b$.

- $5 \bmod 2 = 1$; i.e., $5 = 2 \times 2 + 1$
- $6 \bmod 3 = 0$
- $5 \bmod 6 = 5$

In general, $(rm+k) \bmod m = k$ for $r$ an integer and $k=0,1,\dots,m-1$. So, for any integer $r$, $r \bmod m \leq m-1$.

Generators with $c \neq 0$ are sometimes called mixed-congruential generators while those with $c=0$ are referred to as multiplicative-random uniform $[0, 1)$ numbers (also referred to as random deviates). From generators of either variety, we may make such a transformation that $u_i = x_i/m$ produces values in the unit interval.

Construction of a linear congruential generator seems straightforward for that only the values for $a, c$ and $m$ need to be chosen. Unfortunately, not every choice will produce sequences of numbers that are satisfactory. An example of what can happen by making a poor choice for $a, c$ and $m$ is seen in Gentle (2003). He considers a multiplicative generator with $m=31, a=3$, and uses $x_0=9$ for the starting value. This produces the sequence.

Guidelines for making “good” choices for the parameters of linear congruential generators are given in Chapter 3 of Knuth (1998a). The first consideration should probably be the choice of the modulus as that provides an upper bound for the number of unique values that can be produced by a generator. Thus, $m$ should be large with $m = 2^k$ and $p = 31, 32, 63, 64$ being obvious for 32 and 64 bit architectures. Conditions under which the maximum possible period can be attained stem from number theory considering primality and relatively prime numbers.

A prime is a positive integer or natural number for which there are only two natural number divisors that produce another natural number as the quotient: i.e., for which the division has zero remainder. If there are only two, these numbers must be 1 and the number itself. Examples of prime numbers are 2, 3, 5 and 7. The integer 1 is not considered to be a prime.

Two natural numbers are relatively prime if they have only 1 as a common divisor.
