Chapters 5 is developed for the use of R. Some of the elementary aspects of R are presented in Appendix B. R and C++ are quite compatible in the sense that it is possible to use them in tandem, rather than to solve statistical problems. Chapter 5 contains a discussion of how one can tap into this powerful interface with C++ libraries that can be loaded into R.

R possesses a structure that has similarities to the one available in C++. On the other hand, the functional nature of R creates a number of important differences. Basically, C++ and R are similar in respect of how they deal with data members. The difference is that classes in R have no member functions. Instead, generic functions are created where the class of argument defines a function's behavior. The result is that the effective use of the OOP features of R requires that go beyond and in different directions than those were presented in Chapter 3 for C++. These new ideas are explored in Chapter 6 where we show the techniques for creating new classes and generic functions (and their associated method functions) in the R language are demonstrated.

Numerical linear algebra is a dominant area of scientific computing. While many problems are inherently linear, nonlinear problems are linearized with the consequence that linear equations, eigenvalue problems, etc., arise in a variety of contexts. In statistics, linear equations are frequently encountered when fitting linear models, and generally, least-squares problems. Eigenvalues and values play a crucial role in multivariate analysis, for example.

Chapter 7 covers some of the topics from numerical linear algebra that have particular relevance for statistical computing. The Cholesky decomposition features prominently in this development as a tool for solving both band and banded linear systems.ed matrices and the Cholesky method provide our first foray into the C++ inheritance mechanism. Chapter 7 represents a departure from its predecessors in C++ and R are used with ideas being presented in both languages.

Statisticians often encounter numerical optimization methods throughout nonlinear squares. Numerical optimization is also the key to practical implementation of the uniquely statistical maximum likelihood methodology. Chapter 8 introduces the golden section and Newton optimization algorithms from numerical analysis. Random number generation also comes into play through stochastic search algorithms that provide an effective tool for avoiding issues with functions multiple extrema.

Chapter 9 is dedicated to a high-level introduction to theoretical aspects of data storage. The treatment is in terms of abstract data structures that provide models for standard storage paradigms that are encountered in practice.

Perhaps the most fundamental data structure is an array of numbers. An abstract view of this concept leads to the use of dynamic array that can hold user-defined (abstract) data types and expand or contract its size to accommodate different storage needs. Course, arrays are not suited for every storage problem and there are many other options that will be more efficient for access and manipulation of data in some instances. Examples of such structures include the binary search trees, hash tables, lists and queues that are defined and in Chapter 9. This is also where we introduce an abstraction of the pointer concept called iterator that plays an important role in Chapter 10.
