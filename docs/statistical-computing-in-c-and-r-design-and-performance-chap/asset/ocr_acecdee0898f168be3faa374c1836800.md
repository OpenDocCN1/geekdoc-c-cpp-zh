Up to this point, only negative integers have been considered. To distinguish negative and positive integers, one must retain more information about the sign. For example, we could let the last bit for the sign. This is called the signed magnitude approach. To illustrate the idea, for simplicity we are working with one byte or eight-bit integer. For example, the binary representation for 12 will be 00001100 and -12 would be represented by 10001100. By reserving the last bit for the sign we have ourselves with only 7 "working" bits. Accordingly, the numbers that can be represented as fall $(-2^7 - 1) = -127$ and $2^7 - 1 = 127$.

Another way to store integers is the two's complement rule. Here the left-most bit is allowed to represent $-2^{m-1}$ in an $m$ bit storage allocation. For the case of $m=8$ in our example, the last bit corresponds to $-2^7 = -128$. One can work backward from this to get negative integers by adding the other (more generally, $m-1$) bits to coefficients for powers of two as before. With a slight shift of notation, we write

> -12 = -128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 4 = 1110100.

The two's complement approach with $m=8$ allows us to represent the negative integers between $-128$ and $-1(-128 + (2^7 - 1))$ and hence, the integers that are represented are now all in the range $-128$ to 127. There, one additional integer has been gained over the signed magnitude method.
