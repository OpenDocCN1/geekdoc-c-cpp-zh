Guidelines for making “good” choices for the parameters of linear congruential generators are given in Chapter 3 of Knuth (1998a). The first consideration should probably be the choice of the modulus as that provides an upper bound for the number of unique values that can be produced by a generator. Thus, $m$ should be large with $m = 2^k$ and $p = 31, 32, 63, 64$ being obvious for 32 and 64 bit architectures. It turns out that better results are attained by using $2^{31}-1$ and $2^{63}-1$ (Exercise 4.7). Even if $m$ is large, there is no assurance that the period of a generator, or the length of the pseudo-random sequences it produces before they repeat, will also be large. Conditions under which the maximum possible period can be attained stem from number theory considering primality and relatively prime numbers.

A prime is a positive integer or natural number for which there are only two natural number divisors that produce another natural number as the quotient: i.e., for which the division has zero remainder. If there are only two, these numbers must be 1 and the number itself. Examples of prime numbers are 2, 3, 5 and 7. The integer 1 is not considered to be a prime.

Two natural numbers are relatively prime if they have only 1 as a common divisor. For example, 7 and 9 are relatively prime with only 7 being prime. The numbers 9 and 10 are relatively prime and neither of these is a prime which demonstrates that primality and primality are distinct concepts.
