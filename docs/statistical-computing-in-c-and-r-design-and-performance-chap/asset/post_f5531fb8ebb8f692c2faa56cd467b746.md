To get samples from the chi-square distribution with an integer degrees-of-freedom $\nu$ we could use the fact that such a random variable has the same distribution as sum of $\nu$ squared normal random variables. So one could generate independent standard normal random variables $Z_1, \dots, Z_{\nu}$ and then $X = \sum_{i=1}^{\nu} Z_i^2$ could be taken as the desired value. This is not the most efficient approach and a superior alternative is provided by Kinderman and Monahan (1977) (see also Section 11.2C of Monahan 2001) and Cheng and Feast (1979). The idea is a consequence of our next theorem.

**Theorem 4.5.** *Let $f$ be a density on the real line and suppose that the random variables $U_1, U_2$ have a joint distribution that is uniform over*

$$A = \{(u_1, u_2) : 0 \le u_1 \le \sqrt{f(u_2/u_1)}\quad (4.15)\}$$

$U_2/U_1$ is a random variable with density $f$.

*Proof.* To evaluate the area of the region A to do so, make the change of variable $u_1$ and $u_2$ to $x_1 = u_2/u_1$ and $x_2 = u_1$. The Jacobian for this transformation is $x_2$ so that

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \sqrt{f(x_1)} x_2 dx_2 dx_1 = \frac{1}{2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x_1) dx_1 = \frac{1}{2}.$$

The joint density for $X_1 = U_2/U_1$ and $X_2 = U_1$ is therefore

$$h(x_1, x_2) = \begin{cases} 2x_2, & 0 \le x_2 \le \sqrt{f(x_1)},\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

The marginal density for $X_1$ is now to be $f$ which establishes the theorem.

To use Theorem 4.5 in practice we need to be able to bound the region A where $U_1$ and $U_2$ reside. Then, variables are uniformly distributed over the region laid by the bounds. Now, at least $U_1 \le \max_x x\sqrt{f(x)}$ and $U_2 = U_2/U_1$, it is also true that $\min_x x\sqrt{f(x)} \le U_2 \le \max_x x\sqrt{f(x)}$. Thus, random variables $U_1$ and $U_2$ can be generated with uniformly distributed over $[0, \max_x x\sqrt{f(x)}]$ and $[\min_x x\sqrt{f(x)}, \max_x x\sqrt{f(x)}]$, respectively. If $U_2^2 \le f(U_1)$ is accepted as a sample from the distribution corresponding to $f$ and otherwise the process is repeated.

As Chen and Feast (1979), we will apply this to the case of a gamma random variable with the density

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}\exp\{-x\}, & x \ge 0,\\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

This will allow us to simulate chi-square random variables as a random variable $X$ with a gamma distribution can be transformed to a chi-square random variable $Y$ with $\nu = 2\alpha$ degrees-of-freedom by taking $Y=2X$.

If $\alpha > 1$, we should assume that the case, $x^{\alpha-1}\exp\{-x\}$ and $x^{\alpha+1}\exp\{-x\}$ are maximized at $x = \alpha-1$ and $x = \alpha+1$. Thus, in our previous formulation $\max_x x\sqrt{f(x)} \le [(\alpha-1)/e]^{(\alpha-1)/2}/\Gamma(\alpha)$ and $0 = \min_x x\sqrt{f(x)} \le [(\alpha+1)/e]^{(\alpha+1)/2}/\Gamma(\alpha)$. Gamma random deviates can therefore be generated using Algorithm 4.3 (Exercise 4.23).

# 4.7 Generating random normals

Given the importance of the normal distribution to statistics it is no surprise that a number of methods have been developed for generation normal deviates. Notable examples include those proposed in Box and Müller (1958), Ahrens and Dieter (1973) and Kinderman and Monahan (1976). We will focus our attention on the Box-Muller approach. The basic result as stated in Box and Müller (1958) is if $U_1$ and $U_2$ are random variables with uniform distribution of $[0, 1]$ then

$$Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\ln(U_1)}\cos(2\pi U_2) \quad (4.16)$$
$$Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\ln(U_1)}\sin(2\pi U_2) \quad (4.17)$$

are independent normal variables (Exercise 4.3). This has the implication that pairs of normals can be generated from pairs of uniforms through the simple transformations in (4.16)-(4.17). A polar coordinates perspective leads to another method that employs the accept-reject method while using trigonometric functions.

**Theorem 4.6.** Let $(U_1, U_2)$ have a uniform distribution over the unit circle: i.e., the $(U_1, U_2)$ density is

$$f(u_1, u_2) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{4\pi}, & -1 \le u_1, u_2, u_1^2 + u_2^2 \le 1, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

Then, $R = U_1^2 + U_2^2$ and $V = U_1/\sqrt{R}$,

$$Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\ln(R)}V \quad (4.18)$$
$$Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\ln(R)}\sqrt{1-V^2} \quad (4.19)$$

are independent standard normal variables.

Proof. The proof is relatively straightforward, but somewhat tedious. The result will be established in two steps. First, it will be shown that the bivariate $(U_1, U_2)$ is uniformly distributed over the unit circle, $R$ and $V$ are independent with $R$ having a uniform distribution on $[0, 1]$.

To accomplish our first objective, let $r = u_1^2+u_2^2$ and take $v = u_1/\sqrt{r}$. This gives $u_1=rv$ and $v=\sqrt{r(1-v^2)}$ from which the Jacobian is found to be $(1-v^2)^{-1}/2$. The joint $(R, V)$ density can be written as

$$f(r, v) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{4\pi}(1-v^2), & 0 \le r \le 1, -1 \le v \le 1, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

which proves that $R$ is uniformly distributed on $[0, 1]$ and independent of $V$.

Now take $z_1 = \sqrt{-2\ln(r)}v$ and $z_2 = \sqrt{-2\ln(r)}\sqrt{1-v^2}$. The inverse of this transformation is $r = \exp(-(z_1^2+z_2^2)/2)$ and $v = (1+(z_1/z_2)^2)^{-1/2}$ which produces

$$\frac{2z_2}{\sqrt{z_1^2+z_2^2}} = \exp(-(z_1^2+z_2^2)/2)$$

as the Jacobian. Thus $z_1, z_2$ density is

$$f(z_1, z_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\exp(-(z_1^2+z_2^2)/2), -\infty < z_1, z_2 < \infty$$

and the theorem proves.

Theorem 4.6 suggests a strategy for generating numbers from the normal distribution. Generate two uniforms $U_1$ and $U_2$ from the interval $[-1, 1]$. If $U_1^2+U_2^2 \le 1$, accept the pair or use pseudo-random normal deviates (4.18)-(4.19). Otherwise, generate another pair and repeat the accept-reject evaluation. This approach discards $100(\frac{\pi}{4}-1)=27.3295\%$ of the uniform random number rejection (Exercise 4.35).
