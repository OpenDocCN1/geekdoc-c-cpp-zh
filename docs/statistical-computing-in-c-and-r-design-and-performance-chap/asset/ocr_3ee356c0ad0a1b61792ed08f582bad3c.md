# PREFACE

The serial and OpenMP programs in the book were tested on both Linux and Mac OS operating systems. The MPI code was tested on the ASU Sugaro cluster. No attempt has been made to test our programs in a Windows environment.

A rough timetable for teaching the topics in the book over a fifteen semester might allocate one week to each Chapters 2, 4, 9 and ten weeks to Chapters 3, 7, 8 and 11 weeks to Chapters 5-6. The material in Chapter 11 can be augmented by access to and use of high performance computing resources. The class that has taught ASU with students learning how to run code on the Sugaro cluster at the Fulton High Performance Computing Center. The ASU teaching environment has always been a Linux computer where both students and instructor wrote and ran programs during the course of each.

Many of the programs presented in this book can be downloaded from https://math.asu.edu/~eubank/ While we pay the resulting programs are (very) unlikely to provide optimal performance in any sense. Our goal is the illustration of concepts and the most direct (even brute force) formulations that follow this mandate are what we have used. We encourage readers to rework our programs to obtain alternative approaches to challenging.

Every chapter, except the first, includes a number of exercises. Their level of difficulty ranges from elementary to challenging. In this instance we have developed prototype solutions. But, complicated problems allow for different solutions and we would hope that student solutions would be varied and novel in many of these instance.

This book has gradually evolved over about 10 years. There are many people who have contributed significantly to its progress and final form. From Texas ACM, the fingerprints of James Hardin, John Newton and Sean Reese (no BYU) can be found at each turn of the page. Phil Smith at Texas Tech University and Andrew Karl, Steven Spiritini, Dan Stefano and Guoyi Zhang at ASU helped immeasurably in helping us to learn some of the ins and outs of parallel computing and random generation. Of course, none of this would have been possible without Henrik Schmiedche (at AKM) and Renata Mittleman and Vishnu Chintamaneni (at ASU) who helped us with all our hardware and software issues over the years. We gratefully acknowledge the input from a diligent reviewer that caught so many errors and inconsistencies while pointing out new (at least to us) ideas to consider and implement. A special word of thanks is also due to NSF who provided the funding for the Council on Statistics that has played a pivotal role in both the development and teaching of the material in the text.

Last, but nonetheless, recognition of our spouses Lisa and Goran that have provided both technical and emotional support throughout the writing process. More than of any others, their friendship and understanding has made it possible to undertake and finish this project.
