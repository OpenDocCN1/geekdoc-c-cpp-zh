# 7.2.2 Gaussian elimination

The results in the last section provide the means for solving any rank, upper-triangular system. More generally, this ensures that a solution can be obtained for any linear system that can be transformed to upper-triangular through a series of row operations: i.e., by left multiplication of $A$ and $c$ by appropriate matrices. This can be accomplished with iterated Gauss transformations as we will be described.

Let $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)^T$ be a vector with $x_k \neq 0$ and de Gauss the Gaussian matrix

```
$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \dots & 0 & 0 \ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \ 0 & & 1 & 0 \ 0 & & x_{k+1} & 0 \ 0 & & & x_k & 1 \ & & & -x_{nk} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
```

Then, one may check that $T_k(x)x = (x_1, \dots, x_k, 0, \dots, 0)^T$. With this mind, the system (7.1) can be transformed to upper-triangular via the Gauss process described in Algorithm 7. The algorithm begins by initializing a work $G$ as the matrix $A$ for the linear system. Similarly, a right-hand side vector $h$ is initialized to $c$. Gauss transforms are then sequentially applied to the vector and work matrix until the system is upper-triangular. At that point the solution is available by back-solving.

> * The LU decomposition of a matrix $A$ exists provided that all the principal minors of $A$ are nonzero. A more general factorization of $A$ as $LUP$ with $L$ and $U$ lower and upper-triangular and $P$ a permutation matrix always exists and reduces to the LU decomposition when the latter exists. An algorithm for computing a LUP decomposition can be found in Chapter 28 of Cormen, et al. (2001).
