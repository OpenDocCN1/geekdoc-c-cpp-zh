The middle-square algorithm does not generate numbers that are "random" in any sense. But, it does produce numbers with the characteristics of random numbers to have been made useful for Neumann's particular application.

Streams of numbers created with the middle-square technique would not meet today's standards for random number generation. The use of a deterministic algorithm to produce numbers that possessed many of the qualities of random numbers was the breakthrough that set the course for modern generation. The next step down this path was made by Lehmer (1944, 1954) who pioneered the development of the linear congruential random number generators represent the topic of this section.

A linear congruential generator requires a initial seed that will be seeded by $x_0$. A sequence of pseudo-random integers is then generated via recursion

```
$x_i = (ax_{i-1} + c) \pmod m, \ i=1, \dots,$
```

where $a, c$ and $m$ are all integers. The integer $m$ is called the modulus of the generator while $a$ and $c$ are referred to as the multiplier and increment, respectively.

For two integers $a$ and $b$ the expression $a \bmod b$ refers to the integer remainder after division of $a$ by $b$.

+   * $5 \bmod 2 = 1$; i.e., $5 = 2 \times 2 + 1$
* $6 \bmod 3 = 0$
* $5 \bmod 6 = 5$

In general, $(rm+k) \bmod m = k$ for $r$ an integer and $k=0,1,\dots,m-1$. So, for any integer $r, r \bmod m \leq m-1$.

Generators with $c \neq 0$ are sometimes called mixed-congruential generators while those with $c=0$ are referred to multiplicative-random uniform $[0, 1)$ numbers (also referred to as random deviates) from generators of either variety, we may make such a transformation that $u_i = x_i/m$ produces values in the unit interval.
