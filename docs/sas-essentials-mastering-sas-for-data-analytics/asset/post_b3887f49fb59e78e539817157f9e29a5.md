Binary logistic regression models are based on a dependent variable that can take only of two values, such as presence or absence of disease, deceased or not deceased, married or unmarried. In this setting, the independent (sometimes called explanatory or predictor) variables are used for predicting the probability of occurrence of an outcome (such as mortality). The logistic regression model is sometimes called a logit model. Logistic analysis methods are available for cases in which the dependent variable takes on more than two values, but this topic is beyond the scope of this book and we will discuss only binary logistic model. Logistic analysis is used to create an equation that can be used to predict the probability of occurrence of the outcome of interest, to assess the relative importance of independent variables, and to calculate odds ratios (OR) that measure the importance of an independent variable relative to the response. The independent variables can be either continuous or categorical.

# LOGISTIC ANALYSIS BASICS

## 16.1 LOGISTIC ANALYSIS BASICS

By describing the SAS implementation of logistic regression, we briefly discuss some of the basic ideas underlying logistic analysis. We show the actual logistic regression equations here we believe they provide insights into how logistic regression works.

### 16.1.1 The Logistic Regression Model

The basic form of the logistic equation is:

$$\rho = \frac{1}{1+e^{\beta_1X_1+\beta_2X_2+\dots+\beta_kx_k}}$$

$X_1, \dots, X_k$ are the $k$ independent variables, $\hat{\pi}$ is the probability of occurrence of the outcome of interest (which lies between 0 and 1), $\hat{\beta}_i$ is the coefficient on the independent variable $X_i$, and $\hat{\beta}_0$ is a constant term. As in linear regression, the parameters of this theoretical model are estimated from the data, resulting in the prediction equation:

$$\hat{\pi} = \frac{1}{1+e^{\hat{\beta}_0+\hat{\beta}_1X_1+\hat{\beta}_2X_2+\dots+\hat{\beta}_kx_k}}$$

where the $\hat{\beta}_j$'s are maximum-likelihood estimates of the $\beta_j$'s (calculated by SAS) and $\hat{\pi}$ is the estimated probability of occurrence of the outcome of interest. Of course, any variable with a zero coefficient in the theoretical model is not useful in predicting the probability of occurrence. SAS reports test the null hypothesis that all of the $\hat{\beta}_i$'s, $i=1, \dots, k$, are zero.

If no hypothesis is rejected, then there is no statistical evidence that the independent variables as a group are used in the prediction. If the overall test is rejected, we conclude that at least some of the variables are used in the prediction. SAS reports individual tests of the importance of each of the independent variables. That is, for each $i=1, \dots, k$, SAS reports the results of the tests.

> $H_0: \beta_i=0$: The $i$ independent variable is not predictive of the probability of occurrence.
> $H_a: \beta_i\neq 0$: The $i$ independent variable is predictive of the probability of occurrence.

### 16.1.2 Understanding Odds and Ratios

Another use of the logistic model is the calculation of OR for each independent variable. The odds measure the expected number of times an event will occur relative to the number of times it will not occur. Thus, if the odds ratio of an event is 5, this indicates that we expect five times many occurrences as nonoccurrences. An odds of 0.2 (=1/5) would indicate that we expect five times as many nonoccurrences as occurrences.

Suppose you have a dichotomous-dependent variable such as mortality that predicted whether or not a person had a penetrating wound in an accident. If you calculate the odds of dying given a penetrating wound is 0.088 and the odds of dying given a nonpenetrating wound is 0.024, the OR of dying given a penetrating wound is OR=0.088/0.024=3.66. Thus, the odds of dying given a penetrating wound is about four times greater than a nonpenetrating wound. ORs for quantitative-independent variables (AGE, SBP, WEIGHT, etc.) are interpreted differently, and this interpretation is discussed in an upcoming example in the section "Using Simple Logistic Analysis."
