Early in your process of learning to program, you will likely struggle with figuring how to express your ideas in a programming language instead of the natural language. Part of this is because programming languages are fundamentally different than natural languages in they do not allow ambiguity. In addition, they typically require you to express ideas at a lower level than you are used to natural language. Both of these are big part of the reason why everyone should learn how to program. The true benefits of programming are not in the ability to tell a computer how to do something. The real benefits come from how to break problems downs.

At a fundamental level, the computer is a stupid machine. It does not understand or analyze things. The computer does it it is both a blessing and a curse for the programmer. Ambiguity is badly when you are describing how to do things and it is particularly problematic when the instructions do not have the ability to evaluate the different possible meanings and pick the one that makes the most sense. That means programs have to be rigorous and take into account small details. On the other hand, the computer tells us to do things that humans would find incredibly tedious, and the computer will do it for as long as you ask it to. This is a big part of what makes computers so useful. They can sift through huge amounts of data and do calculations without fatigue induced errors.

In the end, you will find that converting your thoughts into a language the computer can understand is the easy part. It will take time to learn your new language and to get to the nuances of it, but the real challenge is figuring out exactly what steps are required to solve a problem. As humans, we tend to overlook many of the details in the processes we go when we are solving problems. We describe things at a high level and ignore the lower levels, assuming that they will be implicitly understood. Programming forces us to clarify these implicit steps and, in doing so, forces us to think more clearly about how we solve problems. This becomes very important as problems get bigger and the things we want to have implicitly assumed become sufficiently complex that they need to be spelled out.
