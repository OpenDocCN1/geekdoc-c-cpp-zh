# Basics of Computers, Computing, and Programming

The breadth of what programs can do is nearly unlimited.¹ Everything runs on every digital device in the world is a program. You use to type your papers and your e-mail. They likely run the fuel injection system in your car and control the lights at intersections. Programs regulate the flow of electricity from the power grid and the flow to your indoor plumbing. Programs do not just give you the apps on your phone, they are running when you call the phone to compress your speech into a digital form and send it out to a local tower where another program examines it and sends it on to destination. On the way, it passes through multiple locations and gets handled by one or more programs at each stop. At some point on the other end, another program takes the digital, compressed form and expands it back out to analog that can be a speaker to the person you are talking to can hear. Someone wrote of those programs and over time more programs are being written that serve more and more different purposes in our lives.

In the last section we mentioned that programs have multiple cores on them. The availability of multiple cores (and perhaps multiple processors) is significant for software as well. First, they get the OS to have multiple things happening at one time. But the simplest of operations performing multitasking. Multitasking is a method that allows multiple tasks to be performed during the same period and allows the OS to have multiple programs or processes running simultaneously. This can be done on a single core by giving each process a short bit of time and then switching between them. When there are multiple cores present, it allows the programs to truly run multiple processes at once, which is known as parallelism.

Each process can exploit its own parallelism by creating multiple threads. The OS is responsible for scheduling what tasks are active at any one time. This allows a single program to utilize the resources of a machine than is present on a single core. While this does not matter for some specialized applications, the use of multiple cores has become more and more commonplace and the core count on large machines as smaller devices is currently climbing at an exponential rate. As a result, the need to multithread programs becomes more vital.

This increasing number of cores has led to another interesting development in the area of servers. Servers are more powerful computers that are used by companies to store large amounts of data and lots of processing. Everything that you do on the web - if you are at a University, odds are that they have at least one full farm of servers that act as a control and data storage center for the campus.

The nature of the work that servers do is often quite different from a normal PC. A fraction of their job is just passing information around, and the workload for that can be very unevenly distributed. The combination of this and multiple cores has led to an increase in the use of virtualization. A virtual machine is a program that acts like a computer. It has a BIOS and loaded OS. The OS can schedule and run programs. This whole process happens inside of a program potentially on a different OS on a real machine. Using virtualization, you can create multiple instances of one or more operating systems running on a single machine. As long as the machine has enough cores and memory, it can support the work of all of these virtual machines. Doing this cuts down the number of real machines that have to be bought and run, reducing costs in both materials and power consumption.
