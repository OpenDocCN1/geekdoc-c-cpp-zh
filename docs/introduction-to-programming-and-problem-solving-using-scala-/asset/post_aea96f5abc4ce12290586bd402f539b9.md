- 16. Certain problems can use a bit more information than just the gcd Euclid's algorithm in exercise 15. In particular, it is often helpful to find the smallest values of $x$ and $y$ that satisfy the equation $gcd = xa + by$. This information can be found efficiently using the extended Euclid's algorithm. This is the math notation for that:

```
$eEuclid(a, b) = \begin{cases} (a, 1, 0) & b = 0 \\ eEuclid(b, a \bmod b), y, x - [a/b] * y) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
```
Convert this to Scala. Note that the $[a/b]$ operation is naturally achieved by the truncation of integer division for positive $a$ and $b$.

- 4. An interesting twist in biology over the past few decades is the ability to look at the populations of different species and how they interact with one another. Often, the way in different populations vary over time can be approximated by simple mathematical expressions. In this project you will use your basic knowledge of conditionals and functions with recursion to examine a simple case where you have two different populations that interact in a predator-prey map.

The simplest form of this problem is the rabbit and fox scenario. The idea is that each summer you count the population of rabbits and foxes in a certain region. This region is well isolated so you do not have animals coming in or leaving. In addition, the climate is extremely temperate, and there is always enough grass so environmental factors do not impact the populations. All happens each year the rabbits try to eat and have babies without getting eaten, and the foxes try to catch rabbits. We will make some formulas for what happens to the population from year to the next, and you will write a program to produce this.

Over the course of each year, the rabbit population will be impacted in the following ways. Some rabbits will be born, rabbits will die of natural causes, and some rabbits will be eaten. Some foxes will be born and die. The number of rabbits depends on the population of foxes (more foxes eat rabbits), and the number of foxes depends on the number of rabbits because foxes cannot live long without finding rabbits to eat. We can combine these things to come up with some equations that predict the number of foxes and rabbits in a given year based on the number in the previous year.

```
$R_{n+1} = R_n + A R_n - B R_n * F_n$
$F_{n+1} = C F_n + D R_n * F_n$
```
Here we assume that the natural tendency of rabbit populations is to increase with foxes around and the natural tendency of fox populations to decrease without. The four constants should have positive values. A represents the natural increase in rabbit population without predation. B is the predation rate and is multiplied by the fox population if either one is small, the predation rate is small. C is the rate at which foxes would normally die without being able to bear young (if they did not have enough food). D is the rate at which foxes will bear young when they find rabbits to eat. In reality, for numeric reasons, you should use Doubles in your program.

The input for your program is the initial rabbit population, $R_0$, the initial fox population $F_0$, and the four constants. To start off, you might try values of 100, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.05, and 0.001. The last four numbers are $A, B, C$, and $D$, respectively.
