You want to try to make your challenges balanced—not immediately easy to figure out, but not so hard that players get frustrated. For example, a player needs to dismantle a bomb and must choose which wires to disconnect in the proper order. The order of the wires could be hidden in a book that the player needs to find and read, or it could be found in a diagram to which the players need to refer when they get to that part of the game.

+   * **Exploration:** Part of a good game is having a space players explore and interact with it. Exploration is part of the world, the people, and the things that the players get to interact with as they play through the game. How much exploration you need depends on how big of a game you want to build. If you are building a small game, then you can just have a few elements that the players must explore. Larger games will have much more!

+   * **Progress:** A must let players know they are moving forward in the story and getting closer to their goals and the game's final resolution. You can provide a feeling of progress in a number of ways. Players can acquire new items, get past obstacles, gain access to new areas, and more. You want the players to feel like they are being successful in the game—otherwise, they'll get frustrated and not want to keep playing.

+   * **Choice:** Games give their players choices. Good games give their players interesting choices. As you are designing the games, the players will need to make choices to move the story forward. Try not to make the choices black and white! Instead, try to make both options look good. Maybe your players can get a hint about the right decision if they found a special item earlier in the game. Giving the players more than two choices can also help make decision points more interesting. Lastly, providing more one way to solve a problem can also provide players with an opportunity to make interesting decisions. The key is making your players want to make the right choice!
