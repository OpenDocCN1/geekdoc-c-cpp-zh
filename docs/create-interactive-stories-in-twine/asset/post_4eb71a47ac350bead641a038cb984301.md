A click macro is similar to the link macro, and it changes text in the passage by replacing or adding to it. But there are two big differences!

1. The "text a" part in a click macro is not actually shown. Instead, the macro is looking for that text in the passage, and will affect it.
2. All instances of the "text a" part will trigger the macro. And in most cases, it will affect all the instances of the text. Let’s say you make the word "dog" clickable and replaced by the word [cat] in your passage. If you have the word "dog" in your passage three times, all three of them would show as links. If the player clicked on any one of them, they would all be replaced by the word "cat" at the same time.

Click (click: "text a")[text b]
where "text a" appears in the passage, it will be clickable and trigger the macro. While all instances of "text a" will be clickable, [text b] will only appear where the macro is typed. To make the text appear everywhere, check out click-append a little further down.

Example:

- - I pet the shivering monkey. (click: "monkey")|It bites my finger.]

- - The reader will first see: 'I pet the shivering monkey.' When they click the word monkey, it will show: 'I pet the shivering monkey. It bites my finger.'

Spacing note: Like the link macro, the spacing between your text and where you put the macro matters!

Case: Your case (meaning the capital and lowercase letters) must be the same in your passage writing and "text a" section for Twine to see the two as a match and make the text clickable. For Twine, "dog" and "Dog" are two different words. On the other hand, if the word is a part of a larger word, Twine will recognize it—so be careful. That means if you were replacing dog with cat, the word "dogged" would become "catged." That's not right! Unless you have a character named Catged. But that seems unlikely.

- - Click-replace (click-replace: "text a")[text b]

- - Anywhere "text a" appears in the passage, it will be clickable. And every instance of "text a" will be replaced by [text b].

Example:

- - I hate the circus because I hate clowns. They freak me out. I know clowns seem nice, but I just don't know. They seem to be hiding something darker. (I'll never trust clowns.

- - (click-replace: "clowns")[makeup nightmares] This will make all the instance of "clowns" clickable. Clicking any of them will change the text to read: "I hate the circus because I hate makeup nightmares. They just freak me out. I know makeup nightmares seem nice, but I don't know. They seem to be hiding something darker. I'll never trust makeup nightmares.

- * Click-append (click-append: "text a")[text b]
* Anywhere "text a" appears in the passage, it will be clickable and it will have [text b] added immediately after. It doesn't matter where you put this macro in your passage, though, again, it's usually better for readability to add it at the end.
* Example:
* I hate the circus because I hate clowns. They just freak out me. I know clowns see nice, but I just don't know. They seem to be hiding something darker. (click-append: "clowns")|and feet
* This time, clicking on "clowns" will produce: "I hate the circus because I hate clowns and feet. They just freak out me. I know clowns and feet seem nice, but I just don't know. They seem to be hiding sothing darker. I'll never trust clowns and feet."

- * Click-prepend (click-prepend: "text a")[text b]
* Anywhere "text a" appears in the passage, it will be clickable and it will have [text b] added immediately before.
* Example:
* I hate the circus because I hate clowns. They just freak out me. I know clowns see nice, but I just don't know. They seem to be hiding something darker. I'll never trust clowns.
(click-prepend: "clowns")|monkey ]
* With this macro, clicking on "clowns" makes the passage read: "I hate the circus because I hate monkeymon clowns. They just freak out me. I know monkey clowns seem nice, but I just don't know.

They seem to be hiding something darker. I'll never trust monkey clowns.

Just to hammer this point home: it's best to place click macros at the bottom of your passage.
