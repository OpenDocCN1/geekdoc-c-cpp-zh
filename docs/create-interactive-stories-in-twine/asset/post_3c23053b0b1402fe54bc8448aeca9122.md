
Second, you need to have clear connections between the different locations. The person running the game needs to be able to tell how the different spaces connect. He or she will also get this information from the write-ups for the different locations, but keeping it clear on the map helps make it easier to run your game.

Your connections need to be straight, too—no corners to mess things up. Remember that the players can't see your map, and they are trying to memorize how the different rooms connect. An elbow or a corner in a connection will create connections that don't match and make it pretty hard for the players to get around.

If players go south to the next location, they would expect to have to go north to get back to where they just left. But an elbow may create a situation where the player leaves one location going south, but enters the connecting location coming from the east, confusing—so maybe it's best to avoid it.

![](img/3760f220c9ee3ac581049b311bdc3cc4_50_0.png)

The last thing you need is a compass rose. This works together with the clear location connections to let the “computer” know which directions the players can go. When connecting your locations, try to stick to directions that are easy to make out (north, south, east, and west).

![](img/3760f220c9ee3ac581049b311bdc3cc4_51_0.png)

Maybe it goes without saying, but be sure to use a pencil—so you can erase and make changes.

## Location, Location, Location

- 10–15 minutes Idea sheet, paper, pencil

Though your map probably looks similar to the story sheet you were working on when designing your CYOP game, locations on this map are different.

Locations are the different places that the players in your game can move to, explore, and make decisions. If they are in a house, the locations could be the different rooms and hallways they are exploring. If they're at the bottom of a tree and can climb to the top, those are two locations.

It's important to note that locations must not always have interesting things happening in them. But they do need to have movement or action decisions that need to be made. If players need to choose a direction or make a decision, it will be a distinct location.

For example, imagine you have a house as a part of your story, with several rooms connected to a hallway. Whenever the players need to choose a direction to go, that needs to be a location so the players can make a choice.

![](img/3760f220c9ee3ac581049b311bdc3cc4_54_0.png)

This is different than the story organizer, where you had new blocks for each decision point and their outcomes. In Parsely, your map is made up of the areas you can move to and explore. Your players may wind up making multiple decisions in a single location, depending on who and what is there.

Look at your story and fill in the rest of the locations on your game map. Think about where the players must go and what they need to do. You don't have to put the next location they need to visit right next to where they are. Spread the locations out, and have them make sense. By building the map, you are building the world that the players are going to explore. Give them the opportunity to adventure!

Don't worry if you don't fill in all of the locations now. Remember—design is a fluid thing and may change as you continue to work on your game. Just be sure to have fun!

## Narrative Sheet: Aka, Writing? Not So Much!

- 20–25 minutes Idea sheet, paper, pencil

## Activity Overview

Now that you have work done on your map, let's switch gears and look at the other part of creating a Parsely game: the narrative sheet.

On the narrative sheet, you're going to be writing up information for each location on your map. The person running your game will look between the map and the write-ups to run the game for the players. The order of locations on your narrative sheet is not important, but you should try to keep them—as best you can—in the order that the players will make their way through the game!

## Instructions

Each location should have four elements written down for the "computer":

- Title
- Descriptive text
- Exits
- Rules for the space

**Title:** The “title”—you guessed it—is the name of the location on your map. It needs to exactly match the name you gave to the space on your map! The players will be exploring your game, moving around from location to location. When they move in a direction, the person running your game will look at the map to see the name of the new location and then look on your narrative sheet to read and run that location.

**Descriptive text:** This is the narrative part of your game for each location. It is what the person running your game is going to read out loud to the players.

Your writing should be in the second-person perspective. Hold on, what? Second person? That’s right—you should always write your descriptive text so that when it gets read, sounds like “you see a master wizard” or “you hear the sound of singing.”

Be sure to take time to describe the locations and what the players can see. Taking the time to give details will really bring your world to life! For example, you could write: “You are standing in a field; there is a house in front of you.” Or you could write: “You are standing in an old, abandoned field that has been long forgotten. The stalks of grass whip in the wind. Ahead of you, a house stands in the field. The paint is chipped and peeling. The windows are all cracked and broken. No one has lived here in years"

Both descriptions let the players know what is in the location, but I think you know which one is interesting! The second description creates a mood and paints a picture that draws the players into the game.

However, you don’t want to describe how things work, or what people can do there. Instead, you just want to describe what they can see. You also don’t want to give everything away either. If there’s a table with an item on it, don’t necessarily include the item in the description. Instead, just note that table and leave it for the players to examine and discover the item!

Exits: These are the directions that the player can go; north, south, east, and west are what you'll use most. You may also use up or down, if you want to have players climbing trees or using stairs. For simplicity's sake, when creating your map, try to keep the possible directions that can go from a particular space down to a maximum of four. This makes it easier for you when designing and keeps it clear for the players when they are running the game!

If you start making "diagonal" exits, like northeast or southwest, it can get messy. Also, remember to keep paths between locations straight! Avoid including elbow corners on your map. When you are first creating your game, you may not have all of the directions on your map right away. You may still be adding locations as you continue working on your game, and that's all fine. Just be sure you can fit all your ideas in eventually!

Rules: The rules help define—for the person running the game—what the players can and can't do in the space. You don't need to think of every possible thing that can be done; just try to get down the ones that are most important for your story. The "computer" will come up with fun, interesting reactions to commands that don't make sense or aren't defined by your rules. That's part of the fun of being the computer!

Your rules don't have to be complex. You can use short, simple sentences to define them. And there's more than one way to write your rules! For example, if the players need to use a key to unlock a door, you could write any of the following rules:

The door is locked. It will open with the key from the chest.
