## Spreading Out

- 10–15 minutes
- Idea sheet, pencil

## Activity Overview

Now that you have your starting spark written down on your idea sheet, it's time to spread out and think about the world where your game is going to take place.

Remember those three categories you thought about for your starting spark? Well, you still need to think about them. They’re important parts of the story behind your game!

## Instructions

Continuing to work on the idea sheet that you created in the first activity, write down at least one idea for each of the other two categories that were not your story spark. You can write more if you like—don’t let me stop you! And don’t worry about whether they’ll all work, just get them down on paper. In the end, there’s really no chance you use every single one of them in your first project. But capturing your ideas on paper will help your story take shape and keep all of your great ideas in one place!

For example, if you decided that your story will take place at a magnified level on the back of a dog, then who might your character meet there? A family of fleas? An angry tick? And what things might happen? Getting lost? Falling off? Or worst of all-a bath?!

By spreading out, you are starting to build out the world of your game. You don't have everything you need yet, but you're building some of the key pieces that you will use or draw inspiration from!

Remember, the idea sheet is not something set in stone. It's a living, breathing piece of your story puzzle that lets you collect all your ideas in one place. You may not use them all, but having them down helps you build your story and your game.

## Writing Up a Story Sheet

With these new activities, you're going to take the rough thoughts and ideas you've been gathering on your idea sheet and begin to organize them into a story/game that readers will eventually play through. For these activities, you'll be working on a new document called a story sheet.

Like your story sheet, the story sheet is a fluid working piece that is meant to help you get closer to building your game. You'll use it to put the sequence of events in order, building the rough story arc of your game. You won't be filling in a lot of details on this sheet. Instead, you will be using it to get down the big, broad strokes of your story, sequencing them from beginning to end. Exciting stuff!

Like your story sheet, feel free to come back to this sheet as you add more ideas or make changes. The greatest stories change and evolve over time, and yours can, too!

## Bookends

- 10–15 minutes Paper, sticky notes, pencil

## Activity Overview

The first two activities got you thinking about the story and background of your game. We're designing games that take place in a story, so that's important! Everything you have down right now is just the filling and flavor that is going to go into your game. Now, you need to start making the delicious crust to put that filling in.

## Instructions

Take out your idea sheet, a new sheet of blank paper, and some small sticky notes. This new piece of paper is going to be your story sheet! It'll help you organize the player's path through your game. It will also help you visualize the connections and branches that come from the decisions the players will get to make. Like I mentioned already, your story sheet is not fixed—meaning you don't have to stick to what you first put down. Just like the idea sheet, it will also live and breathe. Don't like the way something is going? Come back to the sheet and make changes! The goal of the story sheet is to let you sketch out the path your game will take and what the players need to do get from the beginning to the end.

Orient the paper landscape, so it is longer from left to right. On the far left side, place one of your sticky notes. This will be the starting part of your game. On the far right side, place another sticky note. You guessed it—this will be the final part of your game.

Now, looking at your idea sheet, think about how and where the reader is going to start the game/story. What does this space look like? What are readers doing at the start of the game? Who are they? Why are they there? Write those ideas in the starting sticky note on the game map. Next, think about the end. How do the players win the game? What do they need to do in the end? What is the final outcome? Write that in the ending sticky note.

## Touchstones

- 10–15 minutes Sticky notes, pencil

## Activity Overview

You now have a start and an end to your game, but things need to happen between those points. We wouldn't have much of a game (or a story) otherwise! What goes between the middle and the end is called the plot.

## Instructions

For this activity, think about the main events that you want to happen in your story. Look through the ideas you put down on your story sheet and think what things need to happen in your story. In my example, the players will need to find the magic words of power to defeat the wizard. To get those, they will need to trade a piece of gold to an old elf, and they find gold in a chest buried on an island. Other things will happen in the story, but these are the main events that *must* happen. All these ideas need to go on their own sticky notes and be placed on the story sheet in order, from the beginning toward the end.

Because these are on sticky notes, you can move them around, make room for new ones, or take them away as your story takes shape. That’s the beauty of this process! Each of these events will be touchstones your players get to experience as they work through the game.

## One Step Forward

- 15–20 minutes Idea sheet, sticky notes, pencil

## Activity Overview

With your bookends and touchstones in place, now you can start building your story and game by slowly filling in the connections between the starting block, the touchstones, and the ending block. Where you start is up to you. You can begin from the starting block, moving forward in the story, or you can start from the ending block and move backward. In fact, you'll probably do a little of both!

## Instructions

For now, let's start at the beginning of the story. To do this, you need to start thinking of decision points and what could happen next in your story. What are some things the reader can do?

Decisions are any of the things the players may decide to do in that moment. The results of that decision will move the story forward and have them choose a new page to move to. The decisions can be actions, such as pressing a button or retrieving an item. They can be inactions, like not speaking, or waiting. As you get comfortable with making stories, you can get creative with what types of decisions you present to your readers!

For your first CYOP story, think of two things you could give the readers the option of doing. One would move them forward, while the other would either lead nowhere or take them to trouble. Looking back at the example starting block, I had the players wake up in a small, one-room cottage. They see some things around the cottage and they hear singing outside.

For the example, I want the singing to trick the players. So I think I will have the singing coming from a dangerous wood elf.
