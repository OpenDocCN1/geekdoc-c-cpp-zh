You may have noticed that there are two fonts being used in these examples. The words that are italicized are specifically for the “computer,” to let it know what will happen. The words in regular font can be shared with the players.
Your rules can include what the players will discover if they examine items. For example: “If the players examine the mirror, they will find a letter hidden behind the glass.” You can describe what happens if the players do something: “If the players open the door, a wolf attacks them.” You can also say what happens if they don’t have or do something: “If the players don’t have the coin, the gypsy casts a spell on them.”
Don’t be afraid to allow horrible and unsightly ends to come to your players! Remember, they can save their game and restore to where they saved. Part of the fun is knowing that—sometimes—horrible things will happen.

## Cottage

- Descriptive text: You awaken in the morning in your little cottage, nestled in the quiet village where you have lived all your life. The cottage is a simple, small, one-room house with a bed, stove, and table. You see a large stick nestled in with the corner. After a moment, you start to hear strange but beautiful singing coming from outside.
- Exits: Out
- Rules:
  - The bed is simple and a little smelly.
  - The stove contains leftovers from last night. If the players try to eat some, they will get sick all over their shoes.
  - The table is empty and bare.
  - The stick is a large stick, used for beating the dust out from the mattress. The players may take the stick.
  - The singing is almost unnatural. There is something both comforting and unsettling about it.

## Not Always a Straight Line

- 5–10 minutes Idea sheet, paper, pencil

### Activity Overview

When we were building the CYOP game, one of the things we used was a loopback to a decision point. This allowed players to make a wrong choice, but not to start all the way back at the beginning of the game again. But the players were still presented with the same choice and can only choose to make a different one, hoping to move forward this time.

In a Parsely game, you can have players run around a bit to get past obstacles or move forward in the game. They may head south to a freshly dug mound of dirt. To dig that dirt up, they need the shovel, which is only found up north.

So, unlike the CYOP game you created, where the players were always moving forward in the story, in a Parsely game, you can spread your clues, tools, and pieces around throughout the game. Now, it's all well and good to encourage exploration, but you don't want to confuse it with your story arc and the main things that you want to happen in the story.

For example, let's say you want the players to have their way past a rabid banana that is guarding the path to the city of Fruitopia. And to get past the banana, they will need to have a very hungry gorilla accompanying them. Who else is going to be able to take on and eat the rabid banana, right?! Without the gorilla, it will end horribly for the players.

This is where you, as the designer, get to have fun with your players. You don't want to find them just before they run into the banana. If you do, it becomes obvious what they will need to do and they are guaranteed to find the gorilla right before meeting the banana and will always be prepared to deal with it. Though success is fun, having to work for that success is even better!

Instead, the gorilla should be in a location a few rooms away, and may have its own challenge make it difficult to acquire. But keeping them apart creates a problem for the players to solve without an immediately obvious solution!

They may run the rabid banana first—before finding the gorilla—and die a horrible death at the hands of the yellow fruit. They can then load their game and explore other areas, looking for a way to help them get around the banana and discover the gorilla!

## Instructions

Looking at the story for your game, pick an obstacle that the players will need to get past in your game and place it in a location on your map. Next, add the thing needed to a location a few rooms away from the obstacle's location. It's as easy as that!

## Inventory and Requirements

- 20–25 minutes Idea sheet, paper, pencil

### Activity Overview

Unlike CYOP games, in a Parsely game, the players can have an inventory. As they make their way through your game, they can find and carry things around with them. This gives you an opportunity to create more interesting decisions and challenges for the players than simply choosing between a few options!

Now, players can find tools, keys, or other odd objects that they may not immediately know what to do with. You can also create obstacles that require the players to be creative in how they use what they have found! Giving your players items will give you—as you design the game—new ways of unleashing horrible events and woes on your players as they try to use things and they go terribly wrong.

## Instructions

Check out a few examples of how you can take advantage of the players being able to take and use things.

- Locks: Players can find keys, gems, or other things that they need to use on an object to unlock it. That object could be a chest, a door, a wall, or a magical portal. This could include breaking as well! Players may need an object to break open something or break down something to get inside.
- Assembly: Players may need to find several objects that they need to collect so they can put them together to make a new object that they will then use. It may be pieces of a machine, something that was broken, or snippets of code. Players may also need an additional object to then assemble or to use the assembled object on. They may also need a wrench to assemble a machine, glue to put something back together, or a computer to run the code on.
- One-time use: You can also have objects that have special one-time uses that the players need to hold onto and use at the right moment. They may need a match to get them safely through a dark area, bait to catch a small animal, or a potion to give them temporary flight. In all of these cases, the players can only use the object once! So the trick is to get the players to think carefully about when time is, or they need to go back, get another item, or start over again.

Similar to objects the players need and carry with them, you can also have obstacles that require the players have done something before they can get past. They might need to have talked with an important character, or learned a secret password before they can enter an area. They might need to have accomplished something, like rescuing the prince before they enter the castle. Passing these obstacles doesn't require that the players have something but rather that they know or have done something.

You won't put this information in the narrative parts that are read loud to the players. That wouldn't be fun at all! Instead, these are a part of the rules that will be outlined for the person running the game. Because these rules usually affect two locations—the place where the players find or learn something and the place where they use it—it is best to include it in both locations.

For example, let's say the players need to use a key they find in the study to later open the chest in the basement. When they find the key, under the rules, you can state:

The key is small and ornate and seems important. The key will open the chest found in the basement.
