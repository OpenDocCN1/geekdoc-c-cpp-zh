
# Branchy McBranchface

10–15 minutes Idea sheet, sticky notes, pencil

## Activity Overview

One of the ways in which you can use your decision points is to create a branch, or an alternate path that still continues your story forward and eventually meets back up with the main story line.

For example, maybe early on in the wizard story, there's a point where the players can choose between two paths, both of which could eventually lead them to the old pixie who gives them the gold. The players could literally be at a crossroads that forks left and right. Taking the left fork would lead to an old bridge they must cross and a troll they defeat before getting to the pixie. The right fork will get them ambushed by robbers, and they will need to pass to get to the elf.

## Instructions

Introducing forks like this makes your game more interesting and also makes your game replayable, because now if the player goes through your game and chooses a new path, a whole new part of the story opens up!

Despite the advantages, introducing a lot of paths can make it complicated to design your game. Early on, maybe just use one until you get comfortable putting the entire game together.

Take a look at your second sticky note, the one that comes right after your first decision point. Think of how you can create a branch with just a single step before you bring the story back to the main story path.

# Filling It In

10–15 minutes Idea sheet, sticky notes, pencil

## Activity Overview

By now, I hope you're comfortable adding decision steps to your game, thinking about stepping back in your story or moving forward, using a loop or two, and maybe even including a few branches. Your next activity is to work on filling in the rest of your story. Remember to title your sticky notes and include your arrows!

## Instructions

Draw from your idea sheet and think about how you can include the elements you have down. Remember, you have some really great ideas written – but don't feel like you have to include everything. Don't be afraid to add new parts as you are working. Development is always a fluid process and can change as you are working on it. That's okay! The most important thing is to have fun.

# Finding Your Words

10–15 minutes Idea sheet, paper, pencil

## Activity Overview

Congratulations: you have the framework–the bones–for your story! Now you need to turn it into the CYOP game. When you're done, you will have a fully playable game you can share with your friends.

Each of your sticky notes should have enough ideas and details to use as a foundation for writing your game. Now is the time to fill in characters and details, and to give your story life. Remember, your story is still a work in progress, so you can use some more details and make changes as you begin to!

## Instructions

If you want to write a story, you're going to have to—well—write! Get some paper. Starting with your first sticky note, begin to write your story on a new page. When you finish writing the very first story sticky note, you should be at your first decision point. Now, you'll need to present the choices to your readers at the bottom of the page that you finished writing on. So, if the first part of your story took up pages, on the bottom of second page, you will present the choices that the players have.

A good format to present the decision is: "If you make this decision, turn to page X." Going back to our example, the start of my story would look like this:

> You awake early in the morning in your cottage in a small village in the mountains. The cottage is small and simple, you never had need for things. A bed, stove, and small table adorn the room and in the corner is your walking stick.
> It must be early, because you can still hear birds singing. But you also hear something else, too: the sound of someone singing. Is he or she singing with the birds? There is something about the sound of that singing that invites you outside to come and join it.
> If you examine the walking stick, turn to page X.
> If you go outside to check out the singing, turn to page X.

You will notice that I didn't fill in my page numbers. Don't do that yet—that's one of the last steps. Now it's your turn! Write the first part of your story up until your first decision point and place that decision point on the bottom.

# And We Build a Story

- 10–15 minutes Idea sheet, paper, pencil

## Activity Overview

Now that you have the start of your story, you're ready to move forward and write the continuation. If you've been following the activities up to now—I hope you have—your players should have had two decisions to choose from. Now, you need to write up what happens as the results of those decisions. Each of those results should have a sticky note on your story map.

## Instructions

For each result on your story map, use a new page and write up what happens as a result of that decision. Don't write it on the backside of one you've already used! One of the decisions should continue to move the story forward and present new choices. The other should end the story for the players.

Continue writing, and keep in mind that for each sticky note, you will start on a brand new page, even if you have room on the sheet where you presented the decisions to the players. This is because after they make a decision they will have to turn to a page and read to find out what happens next. These are the pages that they are turning to!

Also, as you write up your sticky notes, although they will start on their own pages, they may continue across multiple pages depending on how detailed and descriptive you are, or how much action is happening. That's fine!

As you write up your story, try to keep the pages in order.

# Assembling Your Game

- 20–25 minutes Idea sheet, pencil

This is the home stretch! If you can believe it, you're almost done.

## Instructions

First, you will need to count out of the pages. If you've been working on a word processor so far, you should take advantage of the page numbering functionality of the program. Most programs also have a thumbnail view of the pages, so you can highlight the pages that are part of a section, then drag them to a new section, going back and adding the new page numbers to the decisions presented to the readers.

Now, take a look at the part of your story that happens as a result of your first decision. You need to pick a place further on in the book—maybe in the 10s or 20s. Begin numbering those pages from the page you pick. So if you picked 23, and your section is three pages long, you would number them 23, 24, and 25, and cross those numbers off your page list. Now that you have a page that this part will start on, go back to the decision point and write in the page number the reader will need to turn to. Do the same for the other decision point, picking a different place to put that section, and writing the page number.

Continue this process moving forward through your story. Try to bounce back and forth in your book, but also try to fill in the list of available pages so there are no gaps when you're all done. Don't worry too much if, when you finish, there are a couple single-page gaps. You can always fill them in with some cool illustrations!

If you are writing everything by hand, start at the beginning of your story and number your pages beginning with 1. Stop numbering when Once you have made your way through your story, it will be time for you to go back and read your way through—to make sure everything works and all the pages match between the decision and where they are in your book.
