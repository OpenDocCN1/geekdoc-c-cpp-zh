# PARSELY–NOT JUST FOR EATING

Parsely games were created by Jared A. Sorensen and draw from the old digital text adventure games from the 1980s. I'm sure that sounds like ancient history to you, but it wasn't long ago! In these digital games, players would be introduced to the start of a story and presented with a text prompt. They could write in anything they wanted. It was up to them to explore and figure out where they needed to go and what they needed to do to win the game.

This is a different experience than the CYOP games we were designing in the last set of activities. In those, the players followed your stories and simply made choices when prompted. CYOP games lean more towards a story that you interact with, while Parsely game leans more towards a story you explore.

There are other differences, as well. Parsely often require at least two people—but they can also be played by a large group of people, which is kind of cool! Parsely games have one person acting as the computer. They interpret and run the game for the rest of the group, who are the players. The players take turns giving commands to the “computer,” trying to move the game forward and win. The “computer” looks at the game layout and tells the players what happens. Any number of people can be players and make it through a Parsely game. They all have to work together, taking turns giving commands, playing as a single group, trying to help each other as they try to get to the end of the story and win!

Much of the underlying structure and approach you used with building your CYOP game can be used to make Parsely games, too. You still need to think about characters, location, and events that happen in your game. But now, there’s much less writing for you to do! Instead, your focus is to create a game that can be run for a group of people.

In fact, creating a Parsely game is very much like computer programming. But don't let that scare you! That just means you'll be putting your information together in a certain way, and when it runs, that information will create a game experience for those playing. If the players looked at a Parsely game, it would not look like much of a game! And it definitely wouldn't be very fun for them to just read through. But if someone runs the game for them, a fantastic gaming experience can be created!

This is very similar to video game coding. If you look at the code for a video game, it wouldn't look anything like a game and would not be playable or fun for the person reading. But when that code runs through a computer, it creates something that transcends all code and becomes an awesome experience!

One of the big differences when designing a Parsely game is how much less control you have over how your game plays out. Don't worry—you still have control over the big things. But that control is much looser than the CYOP story you made.

You have control of what things need to happen to progress through the game, but you no longer have full control over when elements are discovered or if the players are able to figure out what they need to do next. Like CYOP, players may stumble across horrible demises or setbacks, but this system is modeled after computer games, so players can choose to save and restore, so they don't have to go all the way back to the beginning.

In a Parsely-style game, players are simply presented with what they see and the directions they may go. It is up to them to examine and explore to find the solutions to move forward. Although they may need to go left to find the key for the locked door, they may choose to go right—you don't have control of that. So the players may go right and find something else or get stuck, then have to backtrack until they finally go left and find the key.

Even though you know, as the designer, how things need to unfold, the players do not and will often go in circles trying to figure things out. You have to be aware that you are creating a more open space that the players get to explore, and you have to plan for that. This freedom and the need to figure out what needs to happen makes Parsely games really fun and enjoyable. So let's get started with making one!

# The Format

Parsely games have a different format than the CYOP game that we designed earlier. In a Parsely game, the focus is more on building an explorable space for the players and there is a lot less actual writing. Because someone else will be running your game, the writing approach is not the same. You are writing instructions for the player to be able to run your game, with information broken between two elements: a map and a narrative sheet.
The following two activities will introduce these elements that will allow you to create a playable Parsely game!

## The MAP!

10–15 minutes Idea sheet, paper, pencil

## Activity Overview

One big part of a Parsely game is the map. The map, as you might have guessed, shows all the locations that the players can move between when exploring and playing, and how they connect to each other. Your map can be simple and stripped down, like this:

![](img/3760f220c9ee3ac581049b31bdc3cc4_48_0.png)

Or—if you want to get fancy, your map can be decorative and elaborate, with all kinds of cool details. A decorative map isn't necessary, but it makes the experience more fun for the person running the game. The decorations can be strictly thematic, or they can give visual hints to the player running the game where things are located. No matter what, it's important to remember that the players never see the map. As they listen to and explore your story, they will have to keep track of where they are and where they went in their heads.

Regardless of which map you decide to make, the map needs to have a few elements that are clear and legible for the person who will be running the game.

First, each location space on the map must have clear and readable names. These can be in the spaces themselves, or right next to them. However you you label the spaces, the person running the game must be able to glance at the location and immediately know what the name of that location is.
