
Later, when the players discover the chest in the basement, you can state in the rules:

- The chest is finely crafted, with an ornate lock. The chest will only open with the small ornate key found in the study.

In this example, you can see a few things. First, there's a clue that the key goes to the lock by the repeated use of the word ornate. This could be helpful if the players have several keys, to let them know which key to use on the lock. Second, remember to use italics to denote something for the person running the game to take note of—but not to read out loud.

Using all these tips, write up the rules for the obstacle you created in the previous activity. Also, revise the rules you wrote earlier for your first few locations and make any adjustments you might need.

## Action Words!

- 10–15 minutes, Idea sheet, paper, pencil

## Activity Overview

In interactive fiction games, players are taking actions in your games as they explore, solve problems, and get past obstacles. You want to give them opportunities and the tools and information they need to do things in your games. Applying that approach to your design is important!

## Instructions

To get started, think about the main types of actions that players might try in interactive fiction games. These are as follows:

- Look
- Take
- Drop
- Put
- Examine
- Search
- Inventory
- Open
- Close
- Lock
- Unlock
- Ask
- Tell
- Say
- Give
- Show

Thinking about how players may act in your game will help you as you think of obstacles and the things you want them to do in your game. It will also help you think of important rules for what happens in your game. Remember, you don't have to think of every possible thing players might try to do in your game! But it definitely helps to think of and make rules for the ones that are important or fun.

Brainstorm action words and create rules for the locations you have already written up in your game. Add one or two new rules for each,. It could be outrageous, funny, add more detail to the story, or cause the game to end in a way!
- Also, have fun with words. If players don’t use the right words, express confusion. For example, if the players find a box and then one of them says to you, “Open it,” you can respond and say, “I don’t see it.” Players must be specific—they see a box, and that’s what they can open. It may sound annoying, but it can make for some fun times!
- If things end horribly, start the game from the beginning. Have fun as you redo this, like you are introducing the game for the very time.

If players told you to “save,” then they can start the game over at the point they saved by telling you to “load.” They will have all the things they had at that point and you will just continue the story like they were back at that point. It's up to you to remember where they saved last, or you can ask the players to remind you where that was. What you shouldn't do, though, remind them to save and load. Let them meet a horrible end a few times. They will quickly figure out that they need to double-check themselves, and their frustration is so much fun to watch.
