symmetric matrices. We write the \(n \times n\) symmetric matrix as

\(A = \left(\begin{array}{ c c }
A^{n-1} & \begin{array}{ c }
a_{1n} \\
\vdots \\
a_{(n-1)n} \\
a_{nn} \\
\end{array} \\
\begin{array}{ c c c c }
a_{n1} & \cdots & a_{n(n-1)} & \\
\end{array} & \\
\end{array}\right).\)

By inductive hypothesis, we can reduce the matrix \(A\) to the form

\(A^* = \left(\begin{array}{ c c c c c }
a_{11}^* & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1,n-1} & a_{1n} \\
0 & a_{22}^* & \cdots & \tilde{a}_{2,n-1} & \tilde{a}_{2,n} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & a_{n-1,n-1}^* & \tilde{a}_{n-1,n} \\
a_{n1} & a_{n2} & \cdots & a_{n,n-1} & a_{nn} \\
\end{array}\right),\)

where \(a_{ii}^*, i = 1, \cdots, n-1\) are positive. From this it immediately follows that we can reduce \(A^*\) and hence \(A\) to echelon form without interchanging rows, as

\(A^{**} = \left(\begin{array}{ c c c c c }
a_{11}^* & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1,n-1} & a_{1n} \\
0 & a_{22}^* & \cdots & \tilde{a}_{2,n-1} & \tilde{a}_{2,n} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & a_{n-1,n-1}^* & \tilde{a}_{n-1,n} \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & a_{nn}^* \\
\end{array}\right).\)

Finally, since \(\det(A) > 0\), \(\det(A^{n-1}) > 0\), and \(\det(A) = \det(A^{**}) = \det(A^{n-1})a_{nn}^*\), it follows that \(a_{nn}^* = \det(A)/\det(A^{n-1}) > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

# **Example 23.2.** For the matrix \(A\) in (18.1), the echelon form is

0 & \frac{120}{11} & -\frac{48}{11} \\
0 & 0 & \frac{54}{5} \\
\end{array}\right).\)

For this matrix, it also directly follows that

\(a_{11}^* = \det(A^1) = 11\)
\(a_{22}^* = \det(A^2)/\det(A^1) = 120/11\)
\(a_{33}^* = \det(A)/\det(A^2) = 1296/120 = 54/5.\)

\end{array}\right) \left(\begin{array}{ c c c }
11 & -1 & -4 \\
0 & \frac{120}{11} & -\frac{48}{11} \\
0 & 0 & \frac{54}{5} \\
\end{array}\right).\)
