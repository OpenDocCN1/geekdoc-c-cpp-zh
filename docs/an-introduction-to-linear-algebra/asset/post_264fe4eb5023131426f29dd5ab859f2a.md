

## Theorem 4.5.

If $A\in M^{n\times n}$ is a nonsingular matrix, then

$$A^{-1}=\frac{\operatorname{adj} A}{|A|}. \tag{4.1}$$

## Proof.

From (3.1)–(3.3), we have $A(\operatorname{adj} A)=(c_{ij})$, where

$$c_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}\alpha_{jk}=\begin{cases}\det(A)&\text{if }i=j,\\0&\text{if }i\ne j.\end{cases}$$

Hence, $A(\operatorname{adj} A)=\det(A)I$, which implies (4.1).

## Example 4.2.

For the matrix

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&1\\3&2&-5\\1&3&-2\end{pmatrix},$$

we have

$$|A|=\begin{vmatrix}2&-1&1\\3&2&-5\\1&3&-2\end{vmatrix}=28\quad\text{and}\quad \operatorname{adj}A=\begin{pmatrix}11&1&3\\1&-5&13\\7&-7&7\end{pmatrix}$$

and hence from (4.1) it follows that

$$A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{11}{28}&\frac{1}{28}&\frac{3}{28}\\\frac{1}{28}&-\frac{5}{28}&\frac{13}{28}\\\frac14&-\frac14&\frac14\end{pmatrix}.$$

An $n\times n$ matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ is said to be a *band matrix* if there exist integers $r$ and $s$, $1<r,s<n$ such that $a_{ij}=0$ for all $j-i\ge r$ and $i-j\ge s$. The number $w=r+s-1$ is called the bandwidth of $A$. Matrices with $r=s=2$ so that $w=3$ are called *tridiagonal* matrices. We shall find the inverse of the tridiagonal matrix

$$A_n(x)=\begin{pmatrix}x&-1&&&&\\-1&x&-1&&&\\&-1&x&-1&&\\&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\&&&-1&x&-1\\&&&&-1&x\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.2}$$

where $x\ge 0$. For this, let $D_n(x)=D_n=|A_n(x)|$. Then, $D_n$ satisfies the following second order linear difference equation

$$D_n=xD_{n-1}-D_{n-2},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots \tag{4.3}$$

together with the initial conditions

\(D_{-1}=0,\quad D_0=1.\) (4.4)

The general solution of (4.3) can be written as

\(D_n=A\lambda_1^n+B\lambda_2^n,\) (4.5)

where \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) are the roots of the equation \(\lambda^2-x\lambda+1=0\). Using (4.4), it follows that

\(D_n=\left\{\begin{array}{ c c }
sinh(n+1)\theta/sinh\theta & \text{if}x=2cosh\theta>2 \\
n+1 & \text{if}x=2 \\
sin(n+1)\theta/sin\theta & \text{if}0\le x=2cos\theta<2.
\end{array}\right.\) (4.6)

Now let \(B_n=(b_{ij})\) be the inverse of \(A_n(x)\), i.e., \(A_n(x)B_n=I\). Then, for \(1\le j\le n\) it follows that

\(-b_{i-1,j}+xb_{ij}-b_{i+1,j}=\left\{\begin{array}{ c c }
0, & i=1,\cdots,j-1,b_{0j}=0 \\
1, & i=j \\
0, & i=j+1,\cdots,n,b_{n+1,j}=0.
\end{array}\right.\) (4.7)

Let \(x=2cosh\theta>2\). When \(1\le i\le j-1\), the general solution of (4.7) is \(b_{ij}=A\lambda_1^i+B\lambda_2^i\), where once again \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\) are the roots of the equation \(\lambda^2-x\lambda+1=0\). This solution is valid for \(0\le i\le j\), and in view of \(b_{0j}=0\) and (4.6) can be written as

\(b_{ij}=2Asinhi\theta=2AD_{i-1}sinh\theta,0\le i\le j.\) (4.8)

Similarly, when \(j+1\le i\le n\), the general solution of (4.7) is \(b_{ij}=C\lambda_1^i+D\lambda_2^i\), which is valid for \(j\le i\le n+1\). This solution in view of \(b_{n+1,j}=0\) and (4.6) appears as

\(b_{ij}=-2CD_{n-i}e^{(n+1)\theta}sinh\theta,j\le i\le n+1.\) (4.9)

Now equating (4.8) and (4.9) for \(i=j\), we obtain the equation

\(AD_{j-1}+Ce^{(n+1)\theta}D_{n-j}=0.\) (4.10)

Also, substituting (4.8) and (4.9) in (4.7) with \(i=j\), we get the equation

\(sinh\theta\left[AD_j+Ce^{(n+1)\theta}D_{n-j-1}\right]=\frac{1}{2}.\) (4.11)

Solving (4.10) and (4.11), we find

\(A=\frac{D_{n-j}}{2D_nsinh\theta},C=-\frac{D_{j-1}}{2D_ne^{(n+1)\theta}sinh\theta}.\) (4.12)

## Invertible Matrices

Now substituting (4.12) in (4.8) and (4.9), we obtain

\(b_{ij}=\frac{1}{D_n}\left\{\begin{array}{ c c }
D_{i-1}D_{n-j}, & i \leq j \\
D_{j-1}D_{n-i}, & i \geq j.
\end{array}\right.\) (4.13)

Finally, we remark that the above calculations can be modified to show that
the formula (4.13) holds for \(0 \leq x \leq 2\) also.

We summarize the above result in the following theorem.

## Theorem 4.6.

For the tridiagonal matrix \(A_n(x)\), \(x \geq 0\) given in (4.2),
the inverse matrix \(A_n^{-1}(x)=B_n=(b_{ij})\) is symmetric, i.e., \(b_{ij}=b_{ji}\) and

\(b_{ij}=\frac{D_{i-1}D_{n-j}}{D_n}>0, i \leq j.\) (4.14)

## Example 4.3.

To compute \(A_5^{-1}(3)\), first we use (4.3), (4.4), to obtain

\(D_1=3, D_2=8, D_3=21, D_4=55, D_5=144\)

and then use (4.14) to get

\(A_5^{-1}(3)=B_5=\frac{1}{144}\left(\begin{array}{ c c c c c }
55 & 21 & 8 & 3 & 1 \\
21 & 63 & 24 & 9 & 3 \\
8 & 24 & 64 & 24 & 8 \\
3 & 9 & 24 & 63 & 21 \\
1 & 3 & 8 & 21 & 55
\end{array}\right).\)

Now let \(x=-y\), \(y \geq 0\). Then, we have

\(A_n(x)=A_n(-y)=-\mathcal{A}_n(y),\) (4.15)

where

\(\mathcal{A}_n(y)=\left(\begin{array}{ c c c c c c }
y & 1 &  &  &  &  \\
1 & y & 1 &  &  &  \\
 & 1 & y & 1 &  &  \\
 &  & \cdots & \cdots &  &  \\
 &  &  & 1 & y & 1 \\
 &  &  &  & 1 & y
\end{array}\right),\) (4.16)

For the matrix (4.16) a result analogous to Theorem 4.6 is the following:

## Theorem 4.7.

For the tridiagonal matrix \(\mathcal{A}_n(y)\), \(y \geq 0\) given in (4.16),
the inverse matrix \(\mathcal{A}_n^{-1}(y)=\mathcal{B}_n=(\beta_{ij})\) is symmetric, i.e., \(\beta_{ij}=\beta_{ji}\) and

\(\beta_{ij}=\frac{(-1)^{i+j}D_{i-1}D_{n-j}}{D_n}, i \leq j.\) (4.17)

**4.1.** Which of the following matrices are singular?

\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\1&1&2\\1&3&4\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\2&4&8\\3&9&25\end{pmatrix},\quad
C=\begin{pmatrix}2&5&19\\1&-2&-4\\-3&2&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Use (4.1) to find the inverses of nonsingular matrices.

**4.2.** For what values of $x$ is the following matrix singular?

\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}
3-x&2&2\\
2&4-x&1\\
-2&-4&-1-x
\end{pmatrix}
\]

**4.3.** Let

\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}

-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12&0\\
0&0&0
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
P=\begin{pmatrix}
\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&0\\
-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12&0\\
0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Show that $P^{-1}AP$ is a diagonal matrix.

**4.4.** Show that the inverse of an upper (lower) triangular nonsingular square matrix is an upper (lower) triangular square matrix. In particular, show that the inverse of a diagonal matrix $A$ with nonzero diagonal elements $a_{ii}$, $i=1,\cdots,n$, is a diagonal matrix $A^{-1}$ with diagonal elements $1/a_{ii}$, $i=1,\cdots,n$.

**4.5.** Let the square matrices $A$, $B$ and $A+B$ be nonsingular. Show that $A^{-1}+B^{-1}$ is nonsingular, and

\[
(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}=A(A+B)^{-1}B=B(A+B)^{-1}A.
\]

**4.6.** Let $A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$. Show that if $B$ is nonsingular, then $\operatorname{tr}(B^{-1}AB)=\operatorname{tr}(A)$.

**4.7.** A real square matrix $A$ is called *orthogonal* if and only if $A^t=A^{-1}$, i.e., $AA^t=A^tA=I$. Thus, $\det(A)=\pm1$. A complex square matrix $A$ is said to be *unitary* if and only if $A^H=A^{-1}$, i.e., $A^HA^{-1}=A^{-1}A^H=I$. If $A,B\in M^{n\times n}$ are unitary matrices, show that $A^H$, $A^{-1}$, $AB$ are also unitary matrices.
