# Inner Products and Orthogonality

In this chapter we shall extend the familiar concept of inner product of two- or three-dimensional vectors to general vector spaces. Our definition of inner products leads to the generalization of the notion of perpendicular vectors, called orthogonal vectors. We shall also discuss the well-known Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization process.

An *inner product* on \((V,\mathbb{C})\) is a function that assigns to each pair of vectors \(u,v\in V\) a complex number, denoted as \((u,v)\), or simply by \(u\cdot v\), which satisfies the following axioms:

- 1. *Positive definite property:* \((u,u)>0\) if \(u\ne0\), and \((u,u)=0\) if and only if \(u=0\).
- 2. *Conjugate symmetric property:* \((u,v)=\overline{(v,u)}\).
- 3. *Linear property:* \((c_1u+c_2v,w)=c_1(u,w)+c_2(v,w)\) for all \(u,v,w\in V\) and \(c_1,c_2\in\mathbb{C}\).

The vector space \((V,\mathbb{C})\) with an *inner product* is called a *complex inner product space*. From 2. we have \((u,u)=\overline{(u,u)}\), and hence \((u,u)\) must be real, and from 2. and 3. it immediately follows that
\[
(w,c_1u+c_2v)=\overline{c_1}(w,u)+\overline{c_2}(w,v).
\]

The definition of a *real inner product space* \((V,\mathbb{R})\) remains the same as above except now for each pair \(u,v\in V\), \((u,v)\) is real, and hence in 2. complex conjugates are omitted. In \((V,\mathbb{R})\) the angle \(0\le\theta\le\pi\) between the vectors \(u,v\) is defined by the relation
\[
\cos\theta=\frac{(u,v)}{(u,u)^{1/2}(v,v)^{1/2}}. \tag{14.1}
\]

Further, the projection of \(u\) onto the vector \(v\) is denoted and defined by
\[
\operatorname{proj}(u,v)=\operatorname{proj}_v u=\frac{(u,v)}{(v,v)}v. \tag{14.2}
\]

**Example 14.1.** Let \(u=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^t\), \(v=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)^t\in\mathbb{R}^n\). The inner product in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is defined as
\[
(u,v)=u^tv=a_1b_1+\cdots+a_nb_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_i=v^tu.
\]

The inner product in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is also called *dot product* and is denoted as \(u\cdot v\). The vector space \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with the above inner product or dot product is simply called an inner product, or dot product, or *Euclidean \(n\)-space*.

Thus, for the vectors \(u=(2,3,4)^t\), \(v=(1,0,7)^t\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), we find
\[
\cos\theta=\frac{30}{\sqrt{29}\sqrt{50}}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\operatorname{proj}(u,v)=\frac{3}{5}(1,0,7)^t.
\]

Let \(A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\), then it follows that
\[
(Au,v)=(Au)^tv=u^tA^tv=u^t(A^tv)=(u,A^tv),
\]
and hence, we have the relation
\[
(Au,v)=(u,A^tv). \tag{14.3}
\]

If \(A\) is an orthogonal matrix, then from (14.3) it immediately follows that \((Au,Av)=(u,v)\).

Clearly, for \(1\times n\) vectors \(u=(a_1,\cdots,a_n)\), \(v=(b_1,\cdots,b_n)\), the above definition of inner product is
\[
(u,v)=uv^t=\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i=vu^t.
\]

**Example 14.2.** Let \(u=(a_1,\cdots,a_n)^t\), \(v=(b_1,\cdots,b_n)^t\in\mathbb{C}^n\). The standard inner (dot) product in \(\mathbb{C}^n\) is defined as
\[
(u,v)=u^t\overline{v}=a_1\overline{b_1}+\cdots+a_n\overline{b_n}
=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\overline{b_i}=v^Hu.
\]

The vector space \(\mathbb{C}^n\) with the above inner product is called a *unitary space*.

Clearly, for \(1\times n\) vectors \(u=(a_1,\cdots,a_n)\), \(v=(b_1,\cdots,b_n)\), the above definition of inner product is
\[
(u,v)=u\overline{v}^{\,t}=uv^H=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\overline{b_i}.
\]

**Example 14.3.** In the vector space \((\mathbb{C}^{m\times n},\mathbb{C})\), an inner product for each pair of \(m\times n\) matrices \(A=(a_{ij})\), \(B=(b_{ij})\) is defined as
\[
(A,B)=\operatorname{tr}(B^HA)=\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n\overline{b_{ij}}a_{ij}.
\]

**Example 14.4.** In the vector space of complex-valued continuous functions \(C[a,b]\), an inner product for each pair of functions \(f,g\) is defined as
\[
(f,g)=\int_a^b f(x)\overline{g(x)}\,dx.
\]

Let \(V\) be an inner product space. Two vectors \(u,v\in V\) are said to be orthogonal if and only if \((u,v)=0\). For example, if \(u,v\in\mathbb{R}^n\), then the vector \(u-\operatorname{proj}(u,v)\) is orthogonal to \(v\). Indeed, we have
\[
(u-\operatorname{proj}(u,v),v)
=(u,v)-\frac{(u,v)}{(v,v)}(v,v)=0.
\]

A subset \(S\) of \(V\) is said to be *orthogonal* if and only if every pair of vectors in \(S\) is orthogonal, i.e., if \(u,v\in S\), \(u\ne v\), then \((u,v)=0\). Clearly, \(0\in V\) is orthogonal to every \(u\in V\), since
\[
(0,u)=(0u,u)=0(u,u)=0.
\]
Conversely, if \(v\) is orthogonal to every \(u\in V\), then in particular \((v,v)=0\), and hence \(v=0\).

The subset \(\hat{S}\) is called *orthonormal* if \(\hat{S}\) is orthogonal and for every \(\hat{u}\in\hat{S}\), \((\hat{u},\hat{u})=1\). If \(S\) is an orthogonal set and \(u\in S\), then the set \(\hat{S}\) of vectors \(\hat{u}=u/(u,u)^{1/2}\) is orthonormal. Indeed, if \(u,v\in S\), then for
\[
\hat{u}=\frac{u}{(u,u)^{1/2}},\qquad
\hat{v}=\frac{v}{(v,v)^{1/2}}\in\hat{S},
\]
we have
\[
(\hat{u},\hat{v})
=\left(\frac{u}{(u,u)^{1/2}},\frac{v}{(v,v)^{1/2}}\right)
=\frac{1}{(u,u)^{1/2}(v,v)^{1/2}}(u,v)=0
\]
and
\[
(\hat{u},\hat{u})
=\left(\frac{u}{(u,u)^{1/2}},\frac{u}{(u,u)^{1/2}}\right)
=\frac{1}{(u,u)^{1/2}(u,u)^{1/2}}(u,u)=1.
\]

The above process of normalizing the vectors of an orthogonal set is called *orthonormalization*.

**Example 14.5.** From (14.1) it is clear that in the inner product space \(\mathbb{R}^n\), two vectors \(u=(a_1,\cdots,a_n)^t\), \(v=(b_1,\cdots,b_n)^t\) are orthogonal if and only if
\[
(u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i=0.
\]
The subset
\[
S=\{u,v,w\}
=\{(1,2,0,-1)^t,(5,2,4,9)^t,(-2,2,-3,2)^t\}
\]
of \(\mathbb{R}^4\) is orthogonal. For this, it suffices to note that \(u^tv=v^tw=w^tu=0\). This set can be orthonormalized to obtain
\[
\hat{S}
=\left\{\frac{u}{(u,u)^{1/2}},\frac{v}{(v,v)^{1/2}},\frac{w}{(w,w)^{1/2}}\right\}
\]
\[
=\left\{
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt6},\frac{2}{\sqrt6},0,\frac{-1}{\sqrt6}\right)^t,
\left(\frac{5}{\sqrt{126}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{126}},\frac{4}{\sqrt{126}},\frac{9}{\sqrt{126}}\right)^t,
\left(\frac{-2}{\sqrt{21}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{21}},\frac{-3}{\sqrt{21}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{21}}\right)^t
\right\}.
\]

The set \(\{e^1,\cdots,e^n\}\) is orthonormal.

Let \(S\) be a subset of an inner product space \(V\). The *orthogonal complement* of \(S\), denoted as \(S^\perp\) (read as “\(S\) perp”), consists of those vectors in \(V\) that are orthogonal to every vector \(v\in S\), i.e.,
\[
S^\perp=\{u\in V:(u,v)=0\text{ for every }v\in S\}.
\]
In particular, for a given vector \(v\in V\), we have
\[
v^\perp=\{u\in V:(u,v)=0\},
\]
i.e., \(v^\perp\) consists of all those vectors of \(V\) that are orthogonal to \(v\).

For a given subset \(S\) of an inner product space \(V\), it is clear that \(0\in S^\perp\).
