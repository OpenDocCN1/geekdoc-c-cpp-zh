# **Example 10.2.** In view of [Example 6.1], we have

$\frac{13}{28}(2,3,1)^t+\frac{47}{28}(-1,2,3)^t+\frac{21}{28}(1,-5,-2)^t=(0,1,4)^t$

and hence $(13/28,47/28,21/28)^t$ are the coordinates of the vector $(0,1,4)^t\in R^3$ relative to the basis $\{(2,3,1)^t,(-1,2,3)^t,(1,-5,-2)^t\}$.

Now let $S=\{u^1,\cdots,u^n\}$ and $T=\{v^1,\cdots,v^n\}$ be two ordered bases of $n$-dimensional vector space $(V,F)$. Then, there are unique scalars $a_{ij}$ such that

$v^j=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}u^i,\quad j=1,\cdots,n.$  (10.1)

Let $(x_1,\cdots,x_n)^t$ be the coordinates of a given vector $u\in V$ with respect to the ordered basis $T$. Then, we have

$u=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_jv^j,$

and hence from (10.1) it follows that

$\begin{aligned}u&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_j\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}u^i\right)\\&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{ij}x_j)u^i\\&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}x_j\right)u^i.\end{aligned}$

Now since the coordinates $(y_1,\cdots,y_n)^t$ of $u$ with respect to the ordered basis $S$ are uniquely determined, we find

$y_i=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}x_j,\quad i=1,\cdots,n,$  (10.2)

which is exactly the same as (5.1) ((5.2) in matrix notation) with $m=n$. Since $S$ and $T$ are linearly independent sets, $y=(y_1,\cdots,y_n)^t=0$ if and only if $x=(x_1,\cdots,x_n)^t=0$. Thus from Corollary 5.1 it follows that the matrix $A$ in (10.2) is invertible, and

$x=A^{-1}y.$  (10.3)

In terms of coordinate isomorphisms, relations (10.2) and (10.3) for any $u\in V$ can be written as

$y_S(u)=Ax_T(u)\quad\text{and}\quad x_T(u)=A^{-1}y_S(u).$  (10.4)
