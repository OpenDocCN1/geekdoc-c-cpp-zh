## INTERESTING PROPERTIES OF THE AIR.

### NEW OBSERVATIONS ON THE LIQUEFACTION OF GASES

### CONDENSATION OF CERTAIN GASES AT TEMPERATURES FAR ABOVE THEIR CRITICAL TEMPERATURES.

The critical temperature of a gas is the temperature above which it cannot be liquefied by pressure alone, no matter how great the pressure may be. At temperatures below this critical point the gas may be condensed, and if the pressure is sufficiently high it becomes a liquid. The gases whose properties are here considered are those which remain in the gaseous state under ordinary conditions, and hence are often called the non-boiling gases.

The following table gives the temperatures at which the more important of these gases cease to boil when the pressure is reduced to a very low point. It will be observed that these temperatures are in general far below the ordinary boiling points of the substances, and are often much higher than the critical temperatures of the corresponding gases.

**TABLE I.**

**TEMPERATURES OF THE NON-BOILING GASES.**

| Gas | Temperature |
| --- | --- |
| Carbon dioxide | 31° C. |
| Oxygen | -118.8° C. |
| Nitrogen | -146.9° C. |
| Hydrogen | -240.3° C. |
| Helium | -267.9° C. |

These figures show how far the so-called permanent gases are from condensation at ordinary temperatures. The recent observations described in the opening heading show, however, that under suitable conditions certain gases may be condensed at temperatures far above their critical temperatures. This remarkable result is of great theoretical interest, and it promises to throw much light on our knowledge of the physical properties of matter in the gaseous state.

Further experiments on the condensation of gases in vacuo have been undertaken, and it is believed that they will not only explain the facts already observed, but will also lead to the discovery of many new properties of the air and of the other gases which compose the atmosphere.
