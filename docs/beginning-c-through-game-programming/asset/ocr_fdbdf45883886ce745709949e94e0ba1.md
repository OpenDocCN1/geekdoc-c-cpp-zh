# I Don't Want to Kill You

ARTHUR BRYANT, a young manager at GreenLeaf Industries, has a problem: his normally friendly, chatty boss Reginald has become withdrawn and hostile. After a tense morning meeting, Arthur discovers that Reginald is planning to fire him. When he overhears two police officers at the coffee shop mentioning the name "Reginald" and an investigation into a series of thefts at the company, Arthur realizes that his boss is the target of a criminal investigation. As Arthur watches Reginald across the room, the two share a moment of mutual understanding, and Arthur knows he must decide whether to expose his boss or protect the man he has come to respect.

THAT EVENING, Arthur finds himself alone in his apartment, replaying the conversation he overheard. Was Reginald really a thief? It didn't seem possible. The man had built GreenLeaf from the ground up, had poured his life into the company. Yet the evidence was there, sitting in the back of Arthur's mind like a stone he couldn't swallow.

Arthur's phone buzzes. It's a text from Reginald: "You're right. I've been unfair to you. Can we meet for coffee?" Arthur stares at the message, his finger hovering over the reply button. His gut tells him to trust Reginald, but his head reminds him of the officers' words.

He types: "Sure. The usual place, 7 p.m."

At the coffee shop, Reginald looks older than Arthur remembers. His shoulders seem heavier under his jacket. They order, and for a long moment neither speaks.

"I know about the investigation," Arthur says quietly. "I heard two officers talking."

Reginald's fork clatters against his plate. "Then you know. I was hoping to tell you myself before it got out. The company is in trouble, Arthur. Not just financial trouble - the kind that follows you home at night." He pauses, rubbing his temples. "I didn't steal, not in the way they think. I made mistakes, bad ones, but I never meant to hurt anyone."

Arthur watches his boss's hands, the way they tremble around the cup of coffee. He thinks about the man who once stayed late to help him prepare a presentation, who sent a fruit basket when Arthur's mother was in the hospital. This is the same man, he tells himself. The same hands that wrote him a birthday card last month.
