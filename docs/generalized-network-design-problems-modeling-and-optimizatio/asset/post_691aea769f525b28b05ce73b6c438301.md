### 6.5 Notes

The GFCNDP consists of finding the cheapest backbone mesh network that interconnects one hub from a given number of clusters. This problem is an important subproblem when constructing hierarchical telecommunication networks and was introduced by Thomadsen and Stidsen [200].

In this chapter the following results have been presented:

- two novel formulations of the GFCNDP based on integer programming: a directed multicommodity flow model from which we obtained a closely related formulation called the bidirectional flow formulation described by Thomadsen and Stidsen [200] and the local-global model;
- two approaches for solving the GFCND problem: a branch-cut-and-price algorithm, proposed by Thomadsen and Stidsen [200], and a solution procedure, based on the local-global formulation of the problem developed by Pop and Pop Sitar [169].

The material presented in this chapter is based on the results published by Pop and Pop Sitar [169], and Thomadsen and Stidsen [200].

Further work may be directed towards the design of exact methods and metaheuristic algorithms for solving the GFCND problem.

## The Generalized Minimum Edge-Biconnected Network Problem (GMEBCNP)

As we have seen in Chapter 2, when we aim at designing communication networks, a minimum spanning tree is usually the cheapest network that will allow a given set of sites to communicate. However, such a network is not robust against failures, since it might not survive the break of even a single link or site. For many communication structures, an important issue, besides the minimization of connection costs, is reliability. The network should be robust against failures in connections or against switching nodes, in the sense that any two nodes do not lose connection in case of simultaneous failures up to a certain maximum number. To accomplish this, redundant communication routes must exist for any pair of nodes.

In order to deal with this issue, the terms vertex-connectivity and edge-connectivity have been introduced in graph theory, to describe this kind of robustness.

The classical edge-biconnectivity augmentation (E2AUG) problem consists of identifying the cheapest set of augmented edges, so that a single edge deletion does not disconnect the graph. The problem was introduced and investigated by Eswaran and Tarjan [35]. They also showed that the problem is NP-hard by a reduction from the Hamiltonian cycle problem.

In this section, we are concerned with the generalized version of the edge-biconnectivity augmentation (E2AUG) problem called the generalized minimum edge-biconnected network problem (GMEBCNP). Given an undirected graph, whose nodes are partitioned into a number of clusters, the GMEBCNP is to find a minimum cost subgraph containing exactly one node from each cluster and being connected in an edge-biconnected way.

The GMEBCNP was introduced by Huygens [84] and arises in the design of backbones in large communication networks.

A similar problem is the generalized minimum vertex-biconnected network problem (GMVBCNP), where the goal is to find a minimum cost subgraph containing exactly one node from each cluster and satisfying the vertex-biconnectivity constraint. For more information on this problem, we refer to [136].

### 7.1 Definition and complexity of the GMEBCNP

Given an n-node weighted undirected graph G = (V, E) whose edges are associated with non-negative costs and V1, ... , Vm a partition of V into m clusters, the generalized minimum edge-biconnected network problem (GMEBCNP) seeks to find a minimum-cost subgraph S, connecting a subset of nodes, which includes exactly one node from each cluster \(V_i, i \in \{1, \dots, m\}\) and which contains no bridges. The bridges of a connected graph are the graph edges whose removal disconnects the graph. More generally, a bridge (also known as a cut-edge or cut arc or an isthmus) is an edge whose deletion increases the number of connected components, see [72] for further details. We will call such a subgraph a generalized edge-biconnected subgraph.

The GMEBCNP is an \(\mathcal{NP}\)-hard problem, since in the particular case where \(|V_i| = 1, \forall i = 1, \dots, m\), the problem of finding a minimum-cost biconnected network, spanning all the nodes of a graph is an \(\mathcal{NP}\)-hard problem. See Garey and Johnson [45].

The generalized minimum edge-biconnected network problem is closely related to some other generalized network design problems such as the generalized traveling salesman problem (GTSP). Because every solution of the GTSP is obviously edge-biconnected and therefore is also a solution of the GMEBCNP, its solution value can be seen as an upper bound of the GMEBCNP.

### 7.2 A mixed integer programming model of the GMEBCNP

In this section, we present the mixed integer programming formulation described by Hu et al. [82]. The model is a multicommodity flow mixed integer programming formulation based on the local-global approach which we originally suggested for the GMSTP (see Chapter 2, Section 2.3.5). The following decision binary variables are considered

```
x_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if edge } e = (i, j) \in E \text{ is included in the selected subgraph} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \quad z_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the node } i \in V \text{ is included in the selected subgraph} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \quad y_{lr} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if cluster } V_l \text{ is connected to cluster } V_r \text{ in the global graph} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \quad f_{lr}^k = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if a flow } f \text{ of commodity } k \text{ exists from cluster } V_l \text{ to cluster } V_r \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
```

where \( l, r \in \{1, \dots, m\} \) and \( k \in \{2, \dots, m\} \).

The formulation consists of two parts. The first part, called the multi-commodity flow part, operates on the global graph and is based on sending from the root cluster \( V_1 \) two units of flow \( f \) to every other cluster, using edge-disjoint routes. The flows for different clusters are distinguished by their commodity \( k \). The second part, called the local-global part, described in Section 2.5.3 relates the local variables \( x_{ij} \) and \( z_i \) to the global variables \( y_{lr} \), describing the connections between clusters.

```
\begin{aligned}
\min \sum_{e \in E} c_e x_e \\
s.t. \quad \sum_{l=1}^m f_{lr}^k - \sum_{r=1}^m f_{rp}^k = \begin{cases} -2 & \text{if } r=1 \\ 2 & \text{if } r=k, \quad \forall r \in \{1, \dots, m\}, \\ 0 & \text{else} \end{cases} \quad \forall k \in \{2, \dots, m\}, \quad (7.1) \\
f_{lr}^k + f_{rl}^k \le 1 \quad \forall l, r \in \{1, \dots, m\}, \forall k \in \{2, \dots, m\} \quad (7.2) \\
\sum_{i \in V_k} z_i = 1, \quad \forall k \in K = \{1, \dots, m\}, \quad (7.3) \\
x(V_l, V_r) = y_{lr}, \quad \forall l, r \in K = \{1, \dots, m\}, l \neq r \quad (7.4) \\
x_{ij} \le z_i, \quad \forall i \in V_l, \forall j \in V \setminus V_l, \forall l \in \{1, \dots, m\}, \quad (7.5) \\
y_{lr} \ge f_{lr}^k, \quad \forall l, r \in \{1, \dots, m\} l \neq r \quad \forall k \in \{2, \dots, m\}, \quad (7.6) \\
f_{lr}^k \ge 0, \quad \forall l, r \in \{1, \dots, m\}, \forall k \in \{2, \dots, m\}, \quad (7.7) \\
y_{lr} \in \{0, 1\}, \quad \forall l, r \in \{1, \dots, m\}, \quad (7.8) \\
x_e, z_i \in \{0, 1\}, \quad \forall e = (i, j) \in E, \forall i \in V, \quad (7.9)
\end{aligned}
```

where \( x(V_l, V_r) = \sum_{i \in V_l, j \in V_r} x_{ij} \).
