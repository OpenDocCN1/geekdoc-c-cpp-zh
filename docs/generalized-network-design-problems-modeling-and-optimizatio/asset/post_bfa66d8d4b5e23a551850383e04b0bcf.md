
\(x(i, V_r) \le z_i, \quad \forall r \in K, \forall i \in V \setminus V_r,\)
\(y \in P_{TSP},\)
\(y_{lr} \in \{0, 1\}, \quad 1 \le l, r \le m.\)

This new formulation of the GTSP was obtained by incorporating the constraints characterizing \(P_{TSP}\), with \(y \in \{0, 1\}\), into \(P_{local}(y)\).

## 3.5 Solving the Generalized Traveling Salesman Problem

The researchers paid a lot of attention to solving the GTSP. As we have seen in the previous section, several transformations of the GTSP into TSP have been proposed. Two approximation algorithms for the GTSP have been developed. Slavik [187] developed a \(3\rho/2\)-approximation algorithm for the GTSP, where \(\rho\) is the number of nodes in the largest cluster, that is, \(\rho = \max_{i=1,\dots,m}(|V_i|)\) and with the additional assumption that the distance matrix satisfies the triangle inequality. As \(\rho\) may be quite large, the worst-case bound may be relatively weak. Garg and Konjevod [46] described an approximation algorithm for the group Steiner problem, which provided an \(O(\log^2(n) \log(\log(n)) \log(m))\) approximation algorithm for GTSP.

An efficient exact algorithm has been proposed by Fischetti et al. [43], based on a branch-and-bound algorithm that solved the problem to optimality for instances with up to 89 clusters and 442 nodes, etc. This method is the state-of-the-art exact algorithm for solving the GTSP. Noon and Bean [133] described a Lagrangian relaxation approach, in order to compute a lower bound on the total cost of an optimal solution of the GTSP.

The difficulty of obtaining optimum solutions for the GTSP has led to the development of several heuristic and metaheuristic algorithms. Noon [132] described several heuristics for the GTSP, the most promising of which is an adaptation of the well-known nearest-neighbor heuristic for the TSP. Fischetti et al. [43] propose similar adaptations of the farthest-insertion, nearest-insertion, and cheapest-insertion heuristics. Renaud and Boctor [178] developed the most sophisticated heuristic published to date, which they call GI³ (Generalized Initialization, Insertion, and Improvement). It consists of three phases and it is a generalization of the I³ heuristic proposed by Renaud et al. [179]. Recently, Cacchiari et al. [14] proposed a multistart heuristic, which starts iteratively with a randomly chosen set of vertices and applies a decomposition approach, combined with improvement procedures. Karapetyan and Gutin [95] investigated specific neighborhoods for GTSP and algorithms that can explore these neighborhoods quickly.

Several metaheuristic approaches have been proposed for solving the GTSP, including a genetic algorithm that uses a random-key encoding, which assures that the constructed solutions by crossover and mutation operators are feasible, and which was introduced by Snyder and Daskin [188]. Pinto et al. [139] developed a metaheuristic, based on an ant colony system, called the reinforcing ant colony system (RACS) algorithm, because it uses new pheromone rules and pheromone evaporation techniques. A genetic algorithm with several new features, including isolated initial populations and a new reproduction mechanism, based upon the TSP ordered crossover operator, was described by Silberholz and Golden [184]. An ensemble of differential evolutionary algorithms, with parallel populations, was developed by Tasgetiren et al. [197]. Particle Swarm Optimization based algorithms have been developed by Shi et al. [185] and by Tasgetiren et al. [196]. Gutin et al. [65, 66]. Bontoux et al. [13] proposed memetic algorithms, which combine the power of genetic algorithms with local search procedures.

Recently, two hybrid algorithms have been developed by Pop et al. [161, 166], in which a genetic algorithm, respectively a consultant-guided search technique are combined with a local-global approach, in order to efficiently solve the GTSP. The memetic algorithm obtained by Gutin and Karapetyan [65] by combining a genetic algorithm with a powerful local search procedure can be considered the most competitive algorithm published to date.

In this section we will describe two algorithmic methods for solving the generalized traveling salesman problem:

- a reinforcing ant colony system
- a hybrid heuristic technique obtained by combining the consultant-guided search technique with a local-global approach to the GTSP.

### 3.5.1 Reinforcing ant colony system for solving the GTSP

In this section, we describe a new algorithm called the reinforcing ant colony system (RACS), based on the Ant Colony System (ACS) for solving the Generalized Traveling Salesman Problem.

The new algorithm enforces construction of a valid solution, used in (ACS), with an update pheromone rule as in [140] and pheromone evaporation as in Stützle and Hoos, [194].

Let \( V_k(v) \) denote the node \( v \) from the cluster \( V_k \). The RACS algorithm for the GTSP works as follows:

- Initially, the ants are placed in the nodes of the graph, choosing random *clusters* and randomly selecting a node from the chosen cluster.
- At iteration \( t+1 \), each ant moves to a new node in an unvisited *cluster*, and the parameters controlling the algorithm are updated.

Each edge is labeled by a trail intensity. Let \( \tau_{ij}(t) \) represent the trail intensity of edge \( (i, j) \) at time \( t \). When an ant decides which node it moves to next, it does so with a probability based on the distance to that node (i.e. cost of the edge) and the amount of trail intensity on the connecting edge.

The inverse of the distance from a node to the next node, is known as the *visibility*, \( \eta_{ij} = \frac{c_{ij}}{d_{ij}} \).

- During each unit of time, evaporation takes place, to stop the intensity trails from increasing unbounded. The rate evaporation is denoted by \( \rho \), and its value is between 0 and 1.

In order to stop ants from visiting the same *cluster* in the same tour a tabu list is maintained. This prevents ants from visiting *clusters* they have previously visited. The ant tabu list is cleared after each completed tour.

- To favor selection of an edge with high pheromone value, \( \tau \), and high visibility \( \eta \), a probability function \( p^k_{iu} \) is considered. Denoting by \( J^k_i \) the neighbors of node \( i \) unvisited by ant \( k \) and letting \( u \in J^k_i, u = V_k(v) \) being the node \( v \) from the unvisited cluster \( V_k \), this probability function is defined as follows:

```
\( p^k_{iu}(t) = \frac{[\tau_{iu}(t)][\eta_{iu}(t)]^\beta}{\sum_{o \in J^k_i} [\tau_{io}(t)][\eta_{io}(t)]^\beta}.\) (3.17)
```

where \( \beta \) is a parameter used for tuning the relative importance of the edge cost in selecting the next node.

If \( q > q_0 \), \( p^k_{iu} \) is the probability of choosing \( j=u \), where \( u = V_k(v) \) is the next node (the current node is \( i \)).

If \( q \le q_0 \) the next node \( j \) is chosen as follows:

```
\( j = \arg\max_{u \in J^k_i} \{ [\tau_{iu}(t)][\eta_{iu}(t)]^\beta \}\) (3.18)
```

where \( q \) is a random variable uniformly distributed in the interval \( [0, 1] \), and \( q_0 \) is a parameter similar to the temperature in simulated annealing, with \( 0 \le q_0 \le 1 \).

The parameter \( q_0 \) is playing an important role, having the chance to improve the algorithm. In the RACS algorithm, the value of the parameter \( q_0 \) is changed at each iteration. For example, for an initial value of \( q_0 \) closer to 1, the value of \( q_0 \) is reduced by a small value \( c_0 \), for example \( c_0 = 0.1 \). If \( q_0 \le c_0 \) then \( q_0 \) is modified.

- After each transition the trail intensity is updated, using the correction rule from [140]:

```
\(\tau_{ij}(t+1) = (1-\rho)\tau_{ij}(t) + \rho \frac{1}{n \cdot L^+}.\) (3.19)
```

where \( L^+ \) is the cost of the best tour.

- In ant colony systems only the ant that generates the best tour is allowed to globally update the pheromone. The global update rule is applied to the edges belonging to the *best tour*. The correction rule is

```
\(\tau_{ij}(t+1) = (1-\rho)\tau_{ij}(t) + \rho \Delta \tau_{ij}(t).\) (3.20)
```

where \( \Delta \tau_{ij}(t) \) is the inverse cost of the best tour.

- In order to avoid stagnation, we used the pheromone evaporation technique introduced by Stützle and Hoos, [194]. When the pheromone trail is above an upper bound \( \tau_{max} \), the pheromone trail is re-initialized.

The pheromone evaporation is used after the global pheromone update rule.

The RACS algorithm computes a sub-optimal solution for a given time \( time_{max} \) and can be stated as follows:

Algorithm 3.5 (Reinforcing Ant Colony System algorithm for the GTSP).

```
For every edge \((i, j)\) do \(\tau_{ij}(0) = \tau_0\)
for \(k = 1\) to \(m\) do
  place ant k on a randomly chosen node
  from a randomly chosen cluster
  let \(T^+\) be the shortest tour found and \(L^+\) its length
  while \((t < time_{max})\) do
    for \(k = 1\) to \(m\) do
      build tour \(T^k(t)\) by applying n-1 times
      choose the next node \(j\) from an unvisited cluster,
      \(j = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \arg\max_{u \notin J_k^t} [\tau_{iu}(t)] \cdot [\eta_{iu}]^\beta & \text{if } q \le q_0 \\ J & \text{if } q > q_0 \end{array} \right.\)
```
