\(x(i, V_r) \le z_i, \quad \forall r \in K, \forall i \in V \setminus V_r,\)
\(y \in P_{TSP},\)
\(y_{lr} \in \{0, 1\}, \quad 1 \le l, r \le m.\)

This new formulation of the GTSP was obtained by incorporating the constraints characterizing \(P_{TSP}\), with \(y \in \{0, 1\}\), into \(P_{local}(y)\).

# ## 3.5 Solving the Generalized Traveling Salesman Problem

The researchers paid a lot of attention to solving the GTSP. As we have seen in the previous section, several transformations of the GTSP into TSP have been proposed. Two approximation algorithms for the GTSP have been developed. Slavik [187] developed a \(3\rho/2\)-approximation algorithm for the GTSP, where \(\rho\) is the number of nodes in the largest cluster, that is, \(\rho = \max_{i=1,\dots,m}(|V_i|)\) and with the additional assumption that the distance matrix satisfies the triangle inequality. As \(\rho\) may be quite large, the worst-case bound may be relatively weak. Garg and Konjevod [46] described an approximation algorithm for the group Steiner problem, which provided an \(O(\log^2(n) \log(\log(n)) \log(m))\) approximation algorithm for GTSP.

An efficient exact algorithm has been proposed by Fischetti et al. [43], based on a branch-and-bound algorithm that solved the problem to optimality for instances with up to 89 clusters and 442 nodes, etc. This method is the state-of-the-art exact algorithm for solving the GTSP. Noon and Bean [133] described a Lagrangian relaxation approach, in order to compute a lower bound on the total cost of an optimal solution of the GTSP.
