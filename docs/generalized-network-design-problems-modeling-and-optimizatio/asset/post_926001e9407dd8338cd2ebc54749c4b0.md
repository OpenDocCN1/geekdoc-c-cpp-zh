A feasible solution to the 2-GTSP may be seen as a cycle-free subgraph with $2m - 1$ edges, connecting all clusters, such that exactly two nodes from each cluster are selected.

Therefore, the 2-GTSP can be formulated as the following 0-1 integer programming problem:

```
min \sum_{e \in E} c_e x_e.
s.t. z(V_k) = 2, \quad \forall k \in \{1, ..., m\}, (4.9)
x(\delta(i)) = 2z_i, \quad \forall i \in V \setminus V_1, (4.10)
x(E) = 2m - 1, (4.11)
x(V_k, V_k) = 1, \quad \forall k \in \{2, ..., m\}, (4.12)
x(E(S)) \le 2r - 1, \quad \forall S = \cup_{j=1}^r V_j, 2 \le r \le m - 1, (4.13)
x_e \in \{0, 1\}, \quad \forall e \in E,
z_i \in \{0, 1\}, \quad \forall i \in V.
```

In the above formulation, constraint (4.9) guarantees that we select exactly two nodes from every cluster. The constraints (4.10) require the number of edges incident with a node $i$ to be either 2 (if node $i$ is visited) or 0 (otherwise). Constraint (4.11) guarantees that the selected subgraph has $2m - 1$ edges, constraints (4.12) guarantee that we select one edge from each cluster (except the starting cluster). Finally, the constraints (4.13) eliminate all cycles connecting at most $m - 1$ clusters.

Replacing the subtour elimination constraints (4.13) with connectivity constraints, we obtain the so-called generalized cut-set formulation:

\(\min \sum_{e \in E} c_e x_e.\)
\(s.t. (1), (3) - (5) \text{ and }\)
\(x(\delta(S)) \ge 2(z_i + z_j - 1), \forall S \subset V,\)
\(\text{with } 2 \le |S| \le m - 1\)
\(\text{and } \forall i \in S, j \in V \setminus S,\)
\(x_e, z_i \in \{0, 1\}, \forall e \in E, i \in V.\)
(4.14)

where, as usual for \(S \subseteq V\), we denoted the cut-set by \(\delta(S)\).

In the above formulation, the constraints (4.14) are the connectivity constraints, which state that each cut separating two visited nodes (\(i\) and \(j\)) must be crossed at least twice.

In addition to the constraints that appear in the previous formulations, we consider the following constraints specific to the railway traveling salesman problem:

\(t_d z_d - t_a z_a \ge t_k, \forall a, d \in V_k, 2 \le k \le m. \quad (4.15)\)

The above constraints state that the difference between the departure and arrival times must be at least a specified period \(t_k\) (depending on the city). This means that the traveling salesman has to stay in each city for some time, in order to finish business. If the difference is too small, the salesman may fail to achieve this. If, on the other hand, the difference is too large, the waiting time at the station will be inconvenient.

The disadvantage of the described integer programming formulations are their exponential number of constraints (we have to choose subsets of \(V\), constraints (4.13) and (4.14)). These constraints can be omitted and can then be generated, as needed, by a *separation algorithm*: one can start without constraints (4.13), solve the corresponding relaxation, and then generate subtour inequalities that are violated by the current solution. The separation algorithm for subtour constraints is based on network flow techniques. For further details see [43].

## Solution procedure and computational results

We used the following *cutting plane algorithm*, in order to solve the 2-GTSP:

- 1. Let the integer programming (IP) formulation consist of the constraints (4.9)–(4.12), (4.15) and the integrality constraints.
- 2. Solve the IP and assume that the optimal solution consists of \(r\) subtours: \(S_1, \dots, S_r\).
- 3. If \(r = 1\), then STOP. The solution to the 2-GTSP is optimal. Otherwise, add the corresponding constraints that eliminate the cycles \(S_1, \dots, S_r\) to the IP formulation and go to Step 2.

The algorithm was implemented in C and for each instance we have created the corresponding integer program, which we solved with CPLEX 6.5.

Test data for our algorithm are real networks from the Dutch railroad company Nederlandse Spoorwegen. The first three data sets contain the Intercity train connections among the larger cities in the Netherlands, stopping only at the main train stations, and thus are considered faster than the normal trains. These trains operate at least every half hour. The second real-world data set contains the schedules of the Intercity trains and regional trains. The regional trains connect the cities in only one region, including some main stations and stop at each intermediate station between two main ones.

## 4.3.3 Solving the RTSP via a transformation into the classical TSP

As we have already seen, the RTSP is related to the GTSP. Due to the fact that there are several efficient algorithms for solving the GTSP, such as genetic algorithms [184, 188], branch-and-cut [43], the algorithms described in Section 3.5, or approaches that exploit the transformation of the GTSP into the TSP [8, 26], it seems a reasonable idea to transform the RTSP into TSP. Such an approach was described by Hu and Raidl [78].

### Transformation of the RTSP to the asymmetric TSP

Applying ideas similar to those from Behzad et al. [8], who presented an efficient transformation of the GTSP into the classical TSP, Hu et al. proposed a scheme to transform the reduced RTSP into an asymmetric TSP, defined on a graph \(G' = (V', E')\). The transformation works as follows: for each station \(\sigma \in B\) in the original reduced graph, the following procedure is applied to obtain a corresponding new station \(\sigma' \in V'\).

Let \(a'_1, ..., a'_k \in \sigma'\) be exact copies of arrival-nodes \(a_1, ..., a_k \in \sigma\), ordered according to their times. We connect them to a cycle with zero cost edges \((a'_1, a'_2), ..., (a'_{k-1}, a'_k), (a'_k, a'_1)\). The same procedure is applied to the departure-nodes \(d'_1, ..., d'_l\), which get connected with zero cost edges \((d'_1, d'_2), ..., (d'_{l-1}, d'_l), (d'_l, d'_1)\). Let \(pred(v)\) denote the cyclic predecessor node \(v\), according to these cycles. Every arrival-node \(a'_i, i=1,...,k\) is connected with every departure-node \(d'_j, j=1,...,l\). For the initial station, \(c(a'_i, d'_j)\) is set to \(t_{d'_j} - t_{d'_i}\), which does not depend on \(t(a'_i)\).

The reason for choosing such a value is that the salesman has to end his tour at the initial station, but not at the initial time. So it only makes a difference when he starts the journey, thus

\(c(a'_i, d'_j) = t_{d'_j} - t_{d'_i},\)

where \(t(d'_i)\) is the earliest possible departure time from the initial station. For all other stations in \(B\), the costs are set to

\(c(a'_i, d'_j) = [1440 + t_{d'_j} - t_{pred(a'_i)} - f_B(\sigma)] \mod 1440 + f_B(\sigma).\)

Note that, since not all original stay-edges are present, their costs are included in these new edges. For example, in Figure 4.5, going from \(a_1\) to \(d_4\) means taking the busy-edge \((a_1, d_3)\) and the stay-edge \((d_3, d_4)\). The costs are \(t_{d_4} - t_{a_1}\), which equal the costs of the new edge \((a'_2, d'_4)\).

Finally, the outgoing edges from \(d'_j, j=1,...,l\) were also adapted. For each original travel-edge \((d_j, v)\) an edge \((pred(d'_j), v)\) was introduced with cost

\(c(pred(d'_j), v) = c(d_j, v) + M.\)

where \(M\) must be a sufficiently large number, e.g. \(\sum_{(u,v) \in E} c(u, v)\), so that no more than one travel-edge will be included in the optimal solution.

This transformation will be illustrated in Figure 4.5 and additionally shows an example of how a route is adapted to the new graph. We may notice that the resulting graph is directed and, therefore, the TSP is asymmetric.
