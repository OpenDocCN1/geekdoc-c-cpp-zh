To achieve a more accurate numeric value (and thus, more accurate reconstructed signal at the other end), the frequencies more common to voice tightly packed with numeric values, whereas the 'fringe frequencies' on the high and low end of the spectrum are more spaced apart.

The sampling device breaks the 8 bits in each byte into two components: positive/negative indicator and the numeric representation. As shown in Figure 1-13, the first bit indicates positive or negative, and the remaining seven bits represent the actual numeric value.

The first bit in Figure 1-13 is a 1, you read the number as positive. The remaining seven bits represent the number 52. This is the digital value used one voice sample. Now, remember, the Nyquist theorem dictates that you need to take 8,000 of samples every second. Doing the math, 8,000 samples a times the 8 bits in each sample, and a-law codec gets you 64,000 bits per second. It's no coincidence that uncompressed audio (including the G.711 audio codec) consumes 64 kbps. Once the sampling device assigns numeric values and a-law all analog signals, a router can place them in a packet and send them across a network.

The last and optional step in the digitization process is to compression measures. Advanced codecs, such as G.729, allow you to compress the number of samples and use less bandwidth. This is possible because human voice 8,000 times a second produces many samples that are similar or identical. For example, say the "cow" out loud to yourself (provided you are in a relatively private area). That takes a second to say, right? If you say it slower it does. Now, listen to the sounds you are making. There's the distinguished "k" sound that starts the word, then you have the "hhhhhh" sound in the middle, followed by the "wa" sound at the break into the end. If you repeat for 8,000 individual samples, chances are most of them would sound the.
