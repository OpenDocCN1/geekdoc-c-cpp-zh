Unfortunately, chopping the amount of bandwidth comes with a price. Quality is usually impacted by the compression process. Early on in the voice digitization years, the powers that are created a measurement system known as a Mean Opinion Score (MOS) too to rate the quality of the various voice codecs. The test used to rate the quality of voice is simple: A listener listens to a caller say the sentence, "Nowadays, a chicken leg is a rare dish," and rates the clarity of this sentence on a scale of 1–5. Table 1-2 leads into a much-needed discussion about audio coder/decoders (codecs). You can use a few audio codecs on your network, geared for different purposes and environments. For example, some codecs are geared specifically for military environments where audio is sent through satellite link and bandwidth is at a premium. These codecs sacrifice audio quality to achieve very streamlined transmissions. Other codecs are designed to meet the need for quality.

If you stay in the Cisco realm for you, you will hear two codecs continually repeated: G.711 and G.729. This is because Cisco designed all its phones with the ability to code in either of these two formats. G.711 is the "common ground" between all VoIP devices. For example, if a Cisco phone is attempting to communicate with an Avaya IP phone, they may support compressed codecs, but can at least agree on G.711 when communicating.

> Note: G.729 comes in two different variants: G.729a (Annex A) and G.729b (Annex B). G.729a sacrifices audio quality to achieve a more processor-efficient coding process. G.729b introduces support for Voice Activity Detection (VAD), which makes voice transmissions much more efficient. You learn more about these variants in the following section.
