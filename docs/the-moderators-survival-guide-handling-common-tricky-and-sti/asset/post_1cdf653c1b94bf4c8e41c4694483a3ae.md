# LEARNING TO ENJOY THE SILENCE

Most moderators are uncomfortable with silence during a study, especially new moderators! You may feel especially uncomfortable if you're using a think-aloud protocol, since it may seem the participant forgot your instruction to share what he is looking for or expects to see.

However, silence can be a valuable tool. Sometimes allowing a participant to quietly process and then answer the question or resume thinking aloud is more valuable than probing immediately for his thoughts whenever he becomes silent. For example, people have difficulty thinking aloud while reading thick blocks of content; hold off on your questions while the participant is obviously reading. Similarly, answering the participant's question right away can lead the participant to answer it on his own. However, if he shifts toward you, or repeats the question while raising his voice, it is time to say something.

It takes practice to become comfortable with this approach, but it pays off when the participant doesn't feel rushed or harassed by your reminders to talk about what he is thinking.

To find out if you're creating or contributing to a problem:

- Check your body language:
  - Are you too close to or too far from the participant?
  - Are you maintaining eye contact, or are you making too much eye contact?
- Have you been yawning or glancing at your watch?
- Are you sitting upright, or are you slouched down in your chair?
- Have you been frowning or grimacing instead of maintaining a friendly, neutral expression?

2. Replay what you've said during the session:

- Have you neglected to reassure the participant that you're listening to him? Have you been chiming in with the occasional “mm-hm” to encourage him to continue talking?
- Have you been talking too much, and not letting the participant talk?
- If running a usability study with a think-aloud protocol, did you give the participant enough time to respond before reminding him to think aloud? (See the sidebar “Learning to Enjoy the Silence” for more on this.)
- Are you using closed-ended or leading questions?
- Have you been laughing with the participant?

3. Evaluate your tone:

- Did you sound judgmental about the participant?
- Was your language disingenuous?

4. Evaluate your note-taking:

- Are you taking notes while the participant performs tasks?
- Are you taking notes of something unexpected?

5. Check the physical setup of the room:

- Is the temperature too hot or too cold?
- Is the monitor too far back for the participant?
