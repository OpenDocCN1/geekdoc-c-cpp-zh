# THE MODERATOR'S SURVIVAL GUIDE

# 12.4 Participant tells you something personal

The participant tells you something personal about himself or his situation. Maybe he apologizes for seeming distracted and lets you know he's going through a very bitter divorce. Or, he starts telling you about his spouse's recent health problems and seems on the verge of tears. The information that the participant shares may be that needs to be kept confidential, such as his social security number and personal financial details.

+   Method(s): Any
Frequency: Occasional
Pattern(s) to apply: Redirect the participant; Reassurance the participant; Take a break

# What to do

We've experienced times when a participant shares personal information. Sometimes the participant is sharing because he knows he's emotional and gives you a heads up in case comes up later in the session. "Sorry I'm not focused. My mother died last week." Other times it comes unexpectedly as an answer to one of your standard questions. Or, you may be starting the session with the expectation of hearing a participant's personal stories because a sensitive matter is the focus of your research.

+   If the participant is unnecessarily giving away personal information as financial information because he thinks it's necessary for the study (e., while interacting with a financial application), ask him not to share any personal data. Go back to the notes and make sure that any of information is deleted or masked.

+   If you're performing research in a sensitive subject area, you should be expecting to hear a participant's personal stories. Remember to remain empathetic as you answer his questions, and be sensitive if you probe on his answers or ask follow-up questions. Thank him sincerely for his feedback.

+   If the feedback provided by the participant affects you deeply on a personal level (e., because you've had similar experiences), remember to keep the focus on the participant instead of sharing your story. You want the participant to feel safe and respected, and intercepting your takes the focus away from the importance of his feedback.

+   If the participant starts telling you a story that seems to be affecting him emotionally, let him finish the story, and then redirect him to the task at hand. For example, if you asked a participant to tell you how long he opened a checking account and he responds with a story about how he and his late wife chose together, listen to him respectfully until he finishes the story. Thank him for his answer, and if he's okay continuing to the next section or if he'd like to take a short break.
