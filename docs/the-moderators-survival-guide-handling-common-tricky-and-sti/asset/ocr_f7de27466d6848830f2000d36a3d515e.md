# THE MODERATOR'S SURVIVAL GUIDE

# 12.8 Participant has an unexpected disability or service animal (cont.)

# What to say

+   - If you see a service animal with you. Is there anything that you need me to do, or not do, for your animal during our session?
- You mentioned that you have some issues with your vision. Tell me the issues you have, and about the setup you have at home to accommodate these issues.
- I noticed you seem to be squinting at the monitor. Is there anything I can adjust to make it easier for you? Feel free to move your chair to the monitor as well if that helps.
- I want to be accommodating and respectful of your vision. Is there anything in particular that I prefer help with or not help with while you're here?

# What not to do or say

+   - Don't make adjustments to your setup without checking with the participant first. You will just be making assumptions about what will work for them.
- Likewise, do not make assumptions about what the participant will need in terms of physical assistance. You don't want to come across as patronizing or make things more difficult. Honestly ask him what he prefers and how you can help with.
- Don't pet or try to play with a service animal. They're on duty!

# How to avoid

+   - Most participants with any kind of limitation will mention it during the screening process, so you'll usually have a heads up from the recruiter. However, if the participant doesn't mention it ahead of time and meets your recruit criteria, there's a good chance he is still part of your target audience, so anything you do to accommodate him will yield interesting results—that is, this may not be a situation you want to avoid.
- If there are specific reasons why you can't have someone with a physical limitation (e.g., eye-tracking study), include the criteria in your screener. For example, for eye-tracking studies, we include in our screener a question whether the respondent uses bifocals, trifocals, or hard contact lenses—if he does, he is ineligible to participate. We recommend reviewing your screener by an institutional review board (IRB) to ensure that your wording is nondiscriminatory.
- To learn more recommend reviewing the “Interacting with Diverse Populations” chapter in Dumas and Loring (2008).
