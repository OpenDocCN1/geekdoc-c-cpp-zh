# SURVIVAL STORY: "TOO DUMB’ TO YIELD MEANINGFUL RESULTS"
CHARLOTTE SCHWENDEMAN

At the conclusion of a session during the first usability study I ever facilitated, a developer who watched the session from an observation room approached me in a slightly confrontational manner. He put his hands on his hips, stomped his foot, and asked, “Where did you get these stupid users?” I didn’t expect to hear participants called “stupid” again during my career, but also, I did.

My team was in the process of creating and iteratively usability testing prototypes for a web-based solution for a new concept. The company itself was new and its existence depended on the success of the study. I was working for a small consulting firm at the time and my client consisted of the group of senior marketing executives who originated the idea for the concept.

I sold the concept on the value of observing the sessions and we all flew to the city where my team would facilitate the first round of usability studies. Since our client had never seen a usability study before, I sat with them in the observation room, explaining what was happening and why, while another member of my team facilitated the first session. After 20 minutes into the session the client began making noises that the participant was not “normal.” After a few minutes the client became angry and vocal, stating that the participant was “too dumb” to yielding, valid results. The client wanted me to end the session prematurely and move on to the next participant because they didn’t think they were “getting enough” from it. I attempted to calm the client and explain in many ways as I could that the participant was a good match and what they were seeing was typical. The client remained persistent and, in the end, got its way.

As I write about it now, long after the fact, it doesn’t seem any more unusual than other odds that occur during usability studies; however, at the time, the client was so angry about it that it created a very volatile situation. It changed the relationship between the client and my team going forward. They feared their concept would fail and my feared marketing team would insist on cherry-picking participants for the remainder of the project, thereby rendering our usability studies useless. If I had been a “do-over,” I think I would have paid more time and attention explaining to the client that participants who fit the profile can run a fairly wide gamut. The biggest lesson I learned was the time to before a test to explain in detail to stakeholders what they will see and why, and the type of results they can expect.
