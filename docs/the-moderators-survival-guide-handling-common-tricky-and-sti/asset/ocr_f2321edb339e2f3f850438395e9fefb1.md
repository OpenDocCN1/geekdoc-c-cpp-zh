# THE MODERATOR'S SURVIVAL GUIDE

Let’s talk about these qualities in more detail:

+   Empathy helps you recognize the participant is feeling. Remember that no matter what you're dealing with outside of a session -argumentative stakeholders,amental technology, travel problems, etc.—the participant is a person and not just a way for you to get feedback. Put yourself in her shoes and think of how you'd want to be treated if the positions were reversed. Be genuinely interested to identify when she is upset or frustrated and respond in a sincere, compassionate way to help her feel comfortable again.

+   Flexibility is necessary because these situations occur—a participant becomes sick or angry, an observer swears, or in technology fails—you can't railroad your way through the study plan. Sticking superbly to your original plan may just exacerbate the situation, leading to uncontrollable participants and biased results.

New moderators tend to have the most difficulty with the principle of flexibility. See the sidebar "Tips for New Moderators."

+   Creativity or improvisation is crucial for unexpected situations arise. In fact, we know a few improv actors who are great moderators. A simple formula called “Yes, and ...” underlies improv. The principle behind “Yes, and...” is to acknowledge and accept what you have to work with and build upon it rather than fight it. This concept is well-suited for user research activities. If a tricky situation arises, embrace it (flexibility, or the “yes”) and think quickly about how to handle it (creativity, or the...). Creativity is not an easily learned thing, and for some it’s more natural than others. You can set up a framework conducive to creativity by embracing your mindset.

When used carefully, subdued humor can take the edge off an awkward situation. Carefully is the key word. Use humor when appropriate and there is no way for the participant to feel that you're laughing at her.

+   A sense of humor is important in unpredictable situations. When everything feels like it’s falling apart during a session, it’s better for you to laugh than to cry! We don't mean you have to be obnoxious or make inappropriate judgments to the participant—of course you need to stay professional. Rather, we mean you. Try not to get upset or nervous if something doesn't go as planned. Take a breath and remind yourself that you have the tools to handle this. In the worst case, you’ll have a great story to share with your colleagues (after removing any confidential information, of course).

+   An aura of authority means, as the moderator, you must establish both to the participant and the observers as one in control of the session. Remember that you're ultimately responsible for what happens here! Your body language and tone of voice are vital in establishing this authority. You must also be comfortable keeping the session on track. For example, if the participant is taking longer than you expected to show you during a complex inquiry and you need additional feedback before the session ends, you must politely firmly move on. Remember to balance your authority with empathy that the participant feels comfortable and confident while sharing her feedback.

While you have control over the session, the participant should feel that she is leading the conversation (i.e., she “speaking”). See Dumas and Loring (2008) for more on this.

While you put the participant in the spotlight during user research, don't forget that you're ideally in control. Harness your inner ringmaster, plus or minus the hat and coats.
