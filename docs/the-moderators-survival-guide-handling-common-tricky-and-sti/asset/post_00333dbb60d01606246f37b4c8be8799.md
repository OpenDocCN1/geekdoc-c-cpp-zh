## 3.1 Take responsibility

Take responsibility means recognizing that, as the moderator, you're in charge of the session, and then using power wisely.

### When to use

When something happens during a session that a participant may not know how to react to, or when a misunderstanding is encountered, you should step up and take responsibility.

- To keep the participant from feeling badly about something she did.
- If the participant seems to have misunderstood something you asked her about, or to.
- If the participant is approaching emotional or physical discomfort or physical danger.
- If there is an emergency where you have to assist the participant to ensure her safety.

### What to do

This pattern means that you take responsibility for anything that, from the participant’s perspective, is unexpected during the session. For example, if you need to skip tasks or end a session due to something about the participant’s behavior (e.g., she brings a baby to the session or misrepresents a question), you can blame yourself for not having the setup to support her situation or for not choosing the best words. Putting the responsibility on you keeps the participant from feeling badly herself.

This pattern also means that if something in the session seems to be wrong, you should take, or absorb, responsibility for it. If the participant is complaining, don’t agree or disagree with her—just absorb and accept her feedback. If something goes wrong, don’t blame players, developers, recruiters, or the participant—even if something is objectively their fault. As far the participant is concerned, the buck stops with you.

Take responsibility is a pattern that can be used in almost any kind of session. It is a good principle to keep in mind as unexpected situations arise.

You also need to take responsibility for the participant when it comes to her physical comfort and safety, especially in life-threatening situations such as fires or earthquakes. Especially if she is at your facility, it’s your job to watch over, and direct her until she is safely on her way home.

Keep in mind that you may also need to use this pattern with observers, if your observers are in the same room as you and the participant. If the observers are distracting or causing problems, you must take responsibility and resolve the situation to ensure the comfort of the participant.

## 3.2 Clarify the task/question

By clarifying what you mean by a task/question, or asking the participant to reiterate understanding of what you asked her to do, you’ll reduce misunderstandings and improve the odds of getting the kind of feedback you need.

### When to use

Use this pattern when you want to make sure that the participant is clear about what you asked. This pattern may be appropriate:

- If the participant obviously did not understand the task/question.
- If you think the participant may not have understood the task/question.
- If the participant seems to be avoiding a task/question.

### What to do

First, verify that the participant misunderstood the task/question by asking her to tell you, in her own words, what she is doing or describing. For example, if you asked a participant during an interview to tell you about the last time she purchased a computer and she is talking about a recent restaurant she visited, you can say, "So tell me why you're describing your restaurant experience." You might find out that she didn't hear your question correctly, or she visited this restaurant before she went to a store to purchase her computer.

If the participant misunderstood what you were asking, reword or rephrase the task/question to ensure she understands what she is being asked to do. In a usability study, you can ask her to re-read the task out, and ask if she has any questions about the task. You may also need to provide some guidance on the type of feedback you're looking for (e.g., functionality rather than visual design, thinking alike, etc.) or point the participant toward an area of the product that she hasn't found on her own.

This pattern is especially useful in situations where the participant is behaving or answering questions in an unexpected way. Several of these situations are described in Chapters 6 and 10.

While this pattern is fairly straightforward, it can be used in a wide range of situations to help keep the participant on the desired path. Be careful of its misuse or overuse; for example, if you're prompting the participant to re-read a task every time she gives off the optimal or successful path for a task, she may catch on it and could start to influence her behavior.

## 3.3 Redirect the participant

Sometimes the participant pursues a path that will be less useful to you, or the energy that she sends out doesn't mesh with what you need from her. Redirection to help align the participant with your goals.

### When to use

Use this pattern when you need to take the participant's focus and emotions and redirect them in a direction more appropriate for your study. This pattern may be appropriate:

- If the participant seems fixated on something other than you need to receive feedback on.
- If the participant seems to be going down a path that will not be useful on your study goals, and you want to bring her attention back to your original intent.
- If you need to give the participant an assist so she is able to continue a task during a usability study.
- If the participant is obviously disoriented or unhappy with the organization/product, and you can tell that her complaints will get in the way of your feedback.
- If the participant is overly enthusiastic with the organization/product and loses focus.

You will see redirection referenced in many situations throughout the book, but it is especially key in situations where participants are uncertain. These situations are listed in Chapter 8.

### What to do

This pattern redirects the participant toward an outcome more appropriate to the research you're performing. The most common application of this pattern is turning a question back around the participant. For example, if she asks, “Where would I go to that?” you respond, “Where would you expect to go?” If the participant asks a question that is more difficult to test around—“Am I doing right?—this pattern lets you provide a vague answer that redirects her to a topic more relevant to the study.

You may also need to provide a usability study participant with an assist if she struggles with a task that you need to get feedback on, or if she is unable to complete future tasks that depends on. In these situations, you're redirecting the participant from the path she is currently taking to the one that you need to use so you can accomplish your study goals.

An assist can mean anything from a gentle prompt to pointing out what the participant hasn't noticed on her own. For the different types of assists, see section 6.2 and the sidebar "The Diversionate Assist."
