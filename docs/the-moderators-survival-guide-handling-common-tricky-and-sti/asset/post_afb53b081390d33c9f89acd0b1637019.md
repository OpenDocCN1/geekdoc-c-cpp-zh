# SURVIVAL STORY: "HE REFUSES TO LEAVE"
CLIFF ANDERSON

I was at my desk when I got a call from the lab (another usability engineering was running a study that week. Was from our lab assistant, who seemed more than a little excited—“Cliff, there is a man here, and he refuses to leave, and he is threatening us, and we don't know what to do." After calling him, I learned that he had expected to get his $100 compensation in cash, was basking because we were offering Visa gift cards—something that was new for us and for our regular recruiting agency.

I told her "I'll be right down," then hit the stairs to get to the lab. When I got there, I found my assistant, the usability engineer was running our test, and the user. Things seemed pretty under control, but I noticed that the user seemed a little perturbed.

I went right up to him, introduced myself, shook his hand, and asked him what was the matter. He went into his story, and I did my best job at active and empathetic listening, using all the tricks and tropes that I had learned over the years working with users and doing research.

I could tell he was calming down, but I still hadn't come up with a way to address his problem. So, I put my hand on my chin, knotted my brows, and it came to me! "Why don't you give him that gift card, and we can go to the ATM in the lobby downstairs, and I can withdraw your $100"

He agreed, we withdrew the $100, shook hands, and parted amicably. I went back upstairs shaking my head, giving myself credit for keeping cool under pressure (in general, one of the best skills a usability engineer can have) and also making sure that our screener (and recruiting agency) made sure to mention this new form of compensation ahead of time.

Some Guidance Required:
Participants in Need of Shepherding

Sometimes you need to give participants a little nudge. This nudge may take the form of an assist in a usability study or reassurance that they won't get in trouble or hurt anyone's feelings if they provide honest feedback. Approach the situation thoughtfully and gently to avoid embarrassing the participant while encouraging him to provide the feedback you need.

For example, if you're interviewing a call center representative about his calls and sense that he is worried about getting in trouble for not following them, consider saying something like, “I know that you are trained to do things, I'd like to understand more about when you have to do something that is different from your training. Please know that any feedback you share will be kept confidential.
- Talk to your observers after the session and remind them to respect the participant's feedback and anonymity, even (and especially) if his feedback was controversial.

# 6.1 Participant reluctant to say anything negative (cont.)

# What to say

To the participant:

- "I know that this is a product you’re required to use in your job, but tell me candidly..."
- "Please know that your honesty is appreciated. Nothing you say will hurt anyone’s feelings. You’re here to help us make the product better for yourself and future users."

To observers:

- "I get sense that this participant was worried about offending someone with his feedback. So I just to remind all of you, for him and our other participants, please respect the participant’s confidentiality and feedback. Don’t approach the participant to discuss his participation or talk to one about another since he is an employee here."

# What not to do or say

- Avoid making the participants feel like you don’t believe them. If you suspect a participant is not being entirely honest, don’t say, “It’s okay, you can tell the truth,” or “Could you tell me how you really feel?”

# How to avoid

- Set expectations with the participant that his data will be kept confidential, and that his superiors or team members are not observing the session and will not have access to the raw identifiable data from the study.

# 6.2 Participant does something you don't understand

The participant starts sharing feedback of a highly specialized nature in a domain that you don't fully understand. Perhaps you're a consultant who is new to the content or the participant is an expert in a field that you don't know about. Either, you aren't able to follow everything that he is saying or can't determine what pieces of his feedback are important or relevant. Similarly, in contextual inquiry, you may observe the participant doing something you're unable to understand.
