
## 13.6 Participant makes a strangely specific request (cont.)

### What not to do or say

- Avoid giving the impression that you’re at her beck and call. Be kind and a good host and listen politely, but remain in control and present yourself professionally.

### How to avoid

- When the participant is recruited, make sure she knows she’ll be a participant for a user research session with specific goals (rather than a visit or tour as a guest), and what amenities (if any) will be provided.
- If you have a waiting area at your facility, provide reading materials and have some common beverages on hand (e.g., water, coffee, tea).

## 13.7 Participant makes request during a site visit

> You're conducting a study at a participant's house and asks you politely if you'd like a beverage, or if she can make you some food. Maybe the request is more personal (e.g., asking you to take off your shoes), or it's just plain unexpected (e.g., asking you to take out garbage).

Method(s): Any in-person method
Frequency: Occasional
Pattern(s) to apply: Take responsibility; Disengage from the participant

### What to do

When you visit a participant's home, keep in mind that you're a guest and need to be polite and accommodating. Establishing an amicable yet professional relationship will set the tone for a fruitful feedback session. However, it's a slippery slope from being polite to getting too involved or friendly with the participant. Doing so can bias the results of your study or get you into a sticky or dangerous situation.

- If the participant asks you for a beverage, feel free to accept as long as it's nonalcoholic, doesn't create a burden on your host, and doesn't take too much away from the session. Be gracious and thank her for the offer, and remember that the participant is meeting social norms for having a visitor.
- Whether you accept a beverage from the participant or bring your own bottle of water, be careful where you place it. Some people are picky about beverages on their computer desks, or on tables without coasters. Ask the participant where she'd prefer to place your beverage.
- If the participant asks you you want some kind of food, try to avoid having her out of the way unless she's really excited or about serving you and it seems offensive to turn her down. Thank her for the offer. However, be aware that if a participant is, cooking you a steak dinner, it sets a precedent for a more friendly relationship, and it may become harder to stay neutral and in control during the session.
- If the participant has particular customs or rules like taking off shoes, be considerate and follow her rules. As a rule of thumb, it's always a good idea to go to a home visit with socks and shoes rather than without socks. Make sure your socks are ready for public viewing with no holes or inappropriate illustrations.
- If the participant has a really specific request, be accommodating but try to avoid denying tasks for her. For example, out of respect you may take a few minutes of silence for her religious ritual such as praying, but you don't have to take out her, answer the phone, or fix her dishwasher in return for her feedback!

## 13.7 Participant makes request during a site visit (cont.)

- Also try to resist any activities that don't relate to the topic you're researching. For example, if the participant tries to show you old home videos, tell them that you'd like to get started with the session. If necessary, take responsibility and say that you packed a lot into your study plan or use some pretext having to leave early to help her stay focused.
- If the participant seems to be too friendly or to connect with you, use the pattern of disengaging from her slightly. Adjust your demeanor and style to be a bit less approachable and more to Business, and remind her of your purpose for the session.

### What to say

To politely refuse a request:

- “Oh, no, I've just eaten but thanks so much for the offer.”
- “Thank you for offering, but I have a bottle of water right here. If it's okay, I'd like to go ahead and get started.”

If asked to take part in some awkward activity:

- “That's alright, I actually have a bit of prep work to do here so if it's okay, I'll just be here working quietly.”
- “Actually, I'd rather just get started if that's okay. I have a lot to get through and leave a few minutes early.”

### What not to do or say

- Don't do anything that may be perceived as disrespecting the participant's space, like touching her things, opening her fridge, or sitting on her bed (even in a studio apartment), unless you have explicit permission to do so.
- Don't agree to anything that may put you in danger, such as going into bedroom or basement alone with the participant unless location is relevant to the research you're doing. Basically, don't do anything you innocent victims do in horror movies—use your common sense.

### How to avoid

- You never know what you'll be asked or offered a home visit. But one way to get a sense for the participant's personality is to get her call personally and chat for a few minutes with her.

### Survival Story

# SURVIVAL STORY: "SHE TIPPED ME BIG!"
Michael ROBBINS

It started as a routine usability study. Observing behind one-way mirror my director, my boss, a couple of operators, and the client. The participant and I were seated side-by-side in front of a PC in a cramped, bare-walled room. She was a cheerful woman in fifties and I was a thirty-old guy.

Well into the hour-long session before it dawned on me that she might be flirting. She was getting progressively more complimentary and effusive. From behind the mirror I felt amused grins getting wider. Everything was fine until she ended. She started with waves of praise about how well I had done, how professional I was, etc. I thanked her for her kind words as I handed payment to her—an envelope of cash. Before I knew what happened, she stuffed $20 into my hand and showered me with a tsunami of praise. She tipped me ... big!

My first thought was "Oh no! What do I do now?" Then a mental flash of our unseen transfixing on this rapidly unfolding, live drama. I quickly interrupted her and handled the cash back, asking that she keeps it. She refused. I put the cash down on the table in front of her and rationalized that she earned it and belonged to her. She wasn't wanting it. I insisted. Back and forth it went. Changing tactics, I stuffed the $20 deep into her open purse, got close to her, and explained in an earnest and disarming whisper that, just as an architect is, I wouldn't feel right as a professional accepting her money and I wanted her to keep it ... really. And, for some extra oops, I also assured I was doing alright with money ... really. It worked! She left perhaps even more cheerfully than she arrived. Pewhew! I appreciated the knowing smile my director was wearing as she stepped out of the observation room and the good-natured ribbing from my colleagues.

### Survival Story (cont.)

Upon reflection, answering her good-natured flirtation with continued friendliness had the best chance of keeping the study running smoothly. Any “correction,” however gentle, risked my participant’s willing chilliness.

As far as the tip, other than clairvoyance about which approach would work the first time, I'm not sure I would have changed much here either, except perhaps stuffing the money in her purse. That might have gone horribly awry but, as the paraphr adage goes, it's not what you do that is important, but how you do it.
