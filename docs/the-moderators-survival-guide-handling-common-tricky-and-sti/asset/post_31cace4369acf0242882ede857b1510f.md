# 1.1 "Are they laughing at me?"

I used to work at an institution of higher learning. We did a lot of usability testing, up to 120 studies a year. One particular day we had a client who wanted to test a website, but wanted their entire department staff to observe the testing. Our observation room sat 12 and it was mostly filled. Being late in the day, the observers were getting sloppy-after watching users identify the same usability problems over and over again. It was our last session of the day when we encountered a slightly eccentric participant than normal.

As she was telling the tasks, she was quiteirable in some of her feedback, and you could hear, through the adjoining wall to the observation room, muffled commentary becoming louder. I proceeded to instant message my colleague in the room to have the observers keep down, but by the time she read note, it was too late. My participant's eccentric nature had come out via an absolutely hysterical moment that I, as the moderator, had to literally bite my tongue to not laugh. While I controlled my reaction, a volcanic eruption of laughter could be heard through the wall. As my participant heard, her face changed immediately, followed by “Are they laughing at me?"

I no longer wanted to laugh. I was paralyzed by “What do I do?” I lied. I told her, they're not laughing at her, but rather that everyone who has in room today has had a similar experience with this task, so we'll have to redesign the website. It was the best I could do at the time, but I'm not sure she believed me. During the next several tasks, there were no sounds from the observation room; they were silent as she became. After the end, she walked out and apologized on behalf of the observers.

> Michael Dutton

# 1.2 Power and responsibility

Michael's story emphasizes how important a role you have as a moderator of a research session. Your position on the session's frontline means when tricky, sticky, and unexpected situations arise, you're the only one who has decide what to do and take action.

When you encounter the awkward or uncomfortable situation with a friend or else you know, you can inform report with your knowledge about that person. You rarely have any additional knowledge to help you when the same situation happens during a research session. And, of course, you have additional complicado to consider.

# THE MODERATOR'S SURVIVAL GUIDE

![](img/a21e37d9c044778903fdc14dde5d4ca5_20_0.png)

**Figure 1.1** As the moderator, you're balancing a lot of competing needs during the session.

During a session, your reactions as a moderator must balance the following competing needs (Figure 1.1):

- The participant's emotional and physical states. Is she nervous or uncomfortable? Is she looking like she is unwell or experiencing physical discomfort?
- Your emotional and physical states. Are you uncomfortable with anything happening in the session? Are you reacting to an allergen in the environment? Is there an external circumstance occurring that might threaten your physical safety or of the participant's?
- The study's research goals. Are you measuring an experience, looking for issues that need to be fixed, or trying to understand the participant's process?
- General session management needs. Are your remote session technology and equipment working correctly? Are your observers behaving well, especially if they're in the same room as you?
- Protection of your organization. Are you ensuring the participant leaves with a positive experience so she doesn't say things about your organization on social media? Are you doing your best to get feedback from participants that will help improve the experience for future users of your organization's products or services?

Throughout a session, this balance may shift you, as the moderator, have to adjust accordingly. The way you handle a situation at the beginning of a session can be very different than how you handle the same situation later on, even with the same participant.
