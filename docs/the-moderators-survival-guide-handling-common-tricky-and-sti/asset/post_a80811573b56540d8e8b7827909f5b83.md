Finding the right balance of these needs can be difficult and stressful, especially if you’re new to moderating. There are times when you’ll feel pressure to help the participant—it can be painful to watch their struggle—but you must resist that and maintain neutrality so that the data you’re collecting are unaffected. ## 1.3 The session ringmaster

Empathy. Flexibility. Creativity. Sense of humor. Aura of authority. To successfully handle tricky and sticky situations while moderating, you must embody and balance all these qualities. You may recognize these skills as being similar to that of a circus ringmaster. The ringmaster actively listens to monitors everything that is going on around her, directs the audience’s attention, and controls the overall pacing and flow of the show.

# THE MODERATOR'S SURVIVAL GUIDE

Let’s talk about these qualities in more detail:

- Empathy helps you recognize the participant is feeling. Remember that no matter what you're dealing with outside of a session—argumentative stakeholders,amental technology, travel problems, etc.—the participant is a person and not just a way for you to get feedback. Put yourself in her shoes and think of how you'd want to be treated if the positions were reversed. Be genuinely interested to identify when she is upset or frustrated and respond in a sincere, compassionate way to help her feel comfortable again.

- Flexibility is necessary because these situations occur—a participant becomes sick or angry, an observer swears, or in technology fails—you can't railroad your way through the study plan. Sticking superbly to your original plan may just exacerbate the situation, leading to uncontrollable participants and biased results.

New moderators tend to have the most difficulty with the principle of flexibility. See the sidebar "Tips for New Moderators."

- Creativity or improvisation is crucial for unexpected situations arise. In fact, we know a few improv actors who are great moderators. A simple formula called “Yes, and ...” underlies improv. The principle behind “Yes, and...” is to acknowledge and accept what you have to work with and build upon it rather than fight it. This concept is well-suited for user research activities. If a tricky situation arises, embrace it (flexibility, or the “yes”) and think quickly about how to handle it (creativity, or the...). Creativity is not an easily learned thing, and for some it’s more natural than others. You can set up a framework conducive to creativity by embracing your mindset. When used carefully, subdued humor can take the edge off an awkward situation. Carefully is the key word. Use humor when appropriate and there is no way for the participant to feel that you're laughing at her.

- A sense of humor is important in unpredictable situations. When everything feels like it’s falling apart during a session, it’s better for you to laugh than to cry! We don't mean you have to be obnoxious or make inappropriate judgments to the participant—of course you need to stay professional. Rather, we mean you. Try not to get upset or nervous if something doesn't go as planned. Take a breath and remind yourself that you have the tools to handle this. In the worst case, you’ll have a great story to share with your colleagues (after removing any confidential information, of course).

- An aura of authority means, as the moderator, you must establish both to the participant and the observers as one in control of the session. Remember that you're ultimately responsible for what happens here! Your body language and tone of voice are vital in establishing this authority. You must also be comfortable keeping the session on track. For example, if the participant is taking longer than you expected to show you during a complex inquiry and you need additional feedback before the session ends, you must politely firmly move on. Remember to balance your authority with empathy that the participant feels comfortable and confident while sharing her feedback.

## # "YES, AND ..."

The "Yes, and ..." principle is central to improvisation. Let’s imagine that you start a scene with “Happy birthday. I made you *cake,* and my response is, “That is not a cake. That is a dinosaur and my birthday was last month.” This response makes it harder to respond with something that builds the scene along. You say, “Happy birthday. I made you *cake.* Now my response is, “Thank you so much! Mmm, the cake frosting is my favorite part.” Once again, the goal is to build upon the scene, however, instead of controlling it. Rather than saying, “No, it’s not bacon frosting! It’s buttercream,” you might say, “Yeah. It goes well with the wasabi filling. This is saying—”Yes, and!” You took weird and made it weirder—which makes an exciting and (potentially) scene.

We’re not saying if the participant starts a bizarre conversation, how would follow it if it goes (your goals). But if unexpected happens, roll with it. If a prototype breathes, troubleshoot-on-the-fly or find a way to get the participant’s feedback. If an observer barges in, address and resume the session. If the participant talks about cake frosting, keep a poker face and bring the scene back on track (again, unless bacon frosting is in scope!). Flexibility and creativity to smoothly move the “scene” along.

## 1.4 The science and art spectrum

You’re conducting a usability study. The participant is attempting a task that asks her to book a flight on a travel website. gets to a page where she is considering the fares and pays, “I don’t usually just book a flight. I typically explore the flight-plus-hotel combination deals and purchase one of them,”

Do you ask the participant to continue the original task of picking a flight? If you did, you’d be able to compare all participants’ data fairly. Or do you go off-script and follow the participant into whatever they would typically explore? You’d get some valuable information about other areas of the website that you might not get from other participants.

You’ve just stumbled into one user’s great debates: Is moderating a science or an art?
