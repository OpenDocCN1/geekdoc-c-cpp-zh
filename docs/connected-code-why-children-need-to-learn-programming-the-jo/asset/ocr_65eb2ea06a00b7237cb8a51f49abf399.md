In *Connected Code: Why Children Need to Learn Programming*, Yasmin B.\nKafai and Quinn Burke document the revival of Papert’s dream. In the past\nfew years, coding has suddenly become fashionable again, with numerous\nwebsites and organizations encouraging and supporting people to learn to\ncode. My Lifelong Kindergarten research group at the MIT Media Lab has\nactively contributed to this trend, developing the Scratch programming\nlanguage and online community to enable young people to program and\nshare interactive stories and games, and collaborating with the Lego Com-\npany on the development of programmable robotics kits.

Kafai and Burke help put this coding revival in context. Although many\npeople have focused on the job and career opportunities associated with\ncomputer programming (and those opportunities are indeed considerable),\nKafai and Burke recognize that the appeal of coding goes beyond financial\nand economic motives. They situate the coding revival as part of the larger\nmaker movement. I like to think of the coding revival as the coder move-\nment. While the maker movement focuses on new ways for people to build,\ncreate, personalize, and customize physical things, the coder movement\ndoes the same for digital things. In both cases, the movements are catalyzed\nby both technological and cultural trends, as new technologies make it eas-\nier and cheaper to make (and code) things, and a cultural shift toward do-\nit-yourself activities broadens the range of people who want to be involved.

Perhaps the most important contribution of *Connected Code* is its intro-\nduction of computational participation as a guiding framework. In recent\nyears, many researchers have rallied around computational thinking as a\nframework and motivation for computer science education, arguing that\ncomputational concepts can serve as a foundation for understanding all\ntypes of processes and systems in the world. I recognize the power and\nvalue of computational ideas but worry that computational thinking is\noften interpreted too narrowly, focusing on how individual people learn\nand use computational concepts for solving problems. Kafai and Burke’s\nconception of computational participation serves as a broader, more inclu-\nsive framework for computer science education, expanding beyond indi-\nvidual problem solving to include personal expression, creative design, and\nsocial engagement.
