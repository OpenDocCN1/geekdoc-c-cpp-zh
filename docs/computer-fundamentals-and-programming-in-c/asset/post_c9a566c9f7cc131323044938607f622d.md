
and its use is not encouraged, but it does illustrate how the elements are actually stored.

```
for(i = 0; i < 12; i++)
    a[0][i] = i;
```

This trick is possible because C does not do run-time range checking of array subscripts. The following code is also valid for the two-dimensional array a[4][3] of integer type

```
for(i = 0; i < 12; ++i)
    scanf("%d", &a[0][i]);
```

Readers may have noticed that in C the rightmost subscript of a two-dimensional array varies faster than the leftmost (in fact, there are no multidimensional arrays in C, but arrays of arrays). This fact suggests that the array is stored in a ‘row major addressing’ format. So the array equation for element ‘a[m][n]’ of type T is as follows:

```
Address of (a[i][j]) = address of a[0][0] + (i * n + j)
```

The array equation is important. In C, it is hidden from the programmer; the compiler automatically computes the necessary code whenever an array reference is made. The obvious advantage is that the number of rows is not required in the array equation, the address of an element does not have to be computed. That is why, when a two-dimensional array is passed to a function, it is not always necessary to specify the first dimension of the corresponding formal parameter in the function header of the function definition. But for higher-dimensional arrays, the equation gets more and more complicated.

Brian Kernighan and Dennis Ritchie tried to create a unified treatment of arrays and pointers, one that would expose rather than hide the array equation in the compiler’s code. It has been already discussed that a[i] = *(a + i). Therefore, following the same concept, a two-dimensional array can be expressed as follows:

```
a[i][j] = *(a[i] + j) = *(*(a + i) + j) = *(*(a + i) + j)
```

The array equation discussed above is a consequence of the aforesaid notations in the case of a two-dimensional array. The following program illustrates the facts just discussed.

### EXAMPLE

```
27. #include <stdio.h>
int main()
{
    int a[2][3]={10,20,30,40,50,60};
    int i,j;
    for(i=0;i<2;++i)
    {
        printf("\n");
        for(j=0;j<3;++j)
            printf("%d\t",*(*(a+i)+j));
    }
    return 0;
}
```

### Output

```
10 20 30
40 50 60
```

The same output will result if the statement

```
printf("%d\t",*(*(a+i)+j));
```

is replaced by the following equivalent statements,

```
printf("%d\t",*(a[i]+j));
printf("%d\t",*(*(a+i)+j));
printf("%d\t",*(&a[0][0]+i*3+j));
```

Thus, to evaluate either expression, a total of five values must be known.

- The address of the first element of the array, which is returned by the expression `a`, i.e., the name of the array
- The size of the type of the elements of the array, in this case `sizeof(int)`
- The second dimension of the array
- The specific index value for the first dimension, 2 in this case
- The specific index value for the second dimension, 3 in this case

### 13.14.1 Passing Two-dimensional Array to a Function

The following are several alternative ways in C to handle an array passed to a function. They differ in the formal parameter. For illustration, the following C statements are considered.

```
#define MAX_ROWS 10
#define MAX_COLS 10
int A[MAX_ROWS][MAX_COLS];
```

When data is accessed in our matrix using the notation

```
A[i][j]
```

The location for this data is computed using

```
&A[0][0] + MAX_COLS * i + j
```

Some interesting information about a two-dimensional array A[10][10] is as follows.

- `&A[0][0]` is the base address.
- `A[0]` is the base address.
- `A` is the base address.
- `&A[0]` is the base address.

But these are not interchangeable. For instance,

| 表达式 | 指向 |
| --- | --- |
| `&A[0][0] + 1` | points to `A[0][1]`. |
| `A[0] + 1` | points to `A[0][1]`. |
| `A + 1` | points to `A[1][0]` (`A + 1` is the same as `&A[1]` and points to row 1.) |
| `&A[0] + 1` | points to `A[1][0]`. |

## HISTORY OF THE CITY OF BROOKLYN.

### PART I.

### CHAPTER I. THE FORMATION OF BROOKLYN.

The earliest inhabitants of the western end of Long Island, of whom we have any authentic record, were the Canarsee Indians, a tribe of the great Mohegan family, whose territory extended from the shore of the bay, at the mouth of the Hudson, to the highlands of Neversink, on the coast of New Jersey. The principal village of the Canarsees was situated on the hills, in the vicinity of the present ferry at the foot of Fulton street, and was called by the Dutch *Breukelen*, whence the name of Brooklyn. The Indians were, however, a mere handful of savages, who have left no trace of their existence, except in the names of the places which they inhabited, and in the few relics which have been dug up from time to time, in the course of the improvements of the city.

The first European who visited the shores of New York Bay was Henry Hudson, an Englishman in the service of the Dutch East India Company. He entered the bay on the 3d of September, 1609, and spent several days in exploring the coast, and in trading with the natives. He did not, however, penetrate beyond the Narrows, and consequently did not see the site of Brooklyn. The first white man who set foot upon the soil of Kings County was probably Adriaen Block, a Dutch navigator, who, in 1614, sailed up the East river, and anchored in the bay, near the mouth of the Wallabout. He gave the name of *Waal-bogt* to the bay, which is still retained in the Dutch form of Wallabout.

The first settlement of Brooklyn, however, was not made until 1636, when a party of Dutch emigrants, under the direction of the West India Company, landed at the Wallabout, and erected a few huts, which they called *Breukelen*, after a village in Holland, from which the principal of them had emigrated. The settlement was, for some years, a mere agricultural community, and the colonists were subjected to many hardships, in the shape of Indian depredations, and the exactions of the Dutch government. The country was, however, fertile, and the climate mild, and the settlers gradually extended their clearings, and built more comfortable dwellings.

### BROOKLYN INDIANS.

The Canarsee Indians, who occupied the territory now embraced in the city of Brooklyn, were a branch of the great Lenni-Lenape or Delaware family, and spoke a dialect of the Algonquin language. They were, at the time of the first settlement, a harmless and inoffensive race, and were easily induced to part with their lands for a few trinkets, or a small quantity of rum. They were, however, greatly reduced in number by the wars which they carried on against the Dutch, and by the ravages of the small-pox, which was introduced among them by the colonists. Their principal village was at the present Gowanus, and their chief was named *Sewaken*. The last of the Canarsees is said to have died about the year 1730.

### CHAPTER II. THE DUTCH VILLAGE OF BREUCKELEN.

The little Dutch village of Breuckelen, at the time of its first settlement, consisted of a few farm-houses, scattered along the shore of the East river, and upon the hills which rose gently from the water. The dwellings were constructed of wood, with peaked roofs, and were generally surrounded by large gardens, containing apple, pear, and peach trees. The inhabitants were almost all engaged in agricultural pursuits, and were in the habit of selling the produce of their farms to the inhabitants of New Amsterdam, on the opposite side of the river.

The first church in Brooklyn was erected in 1666, on the present site of the borough hall, and served as a place of worship for the whole community, which was at that time entirely Dutch. The principal citizens of the village were, however, not only the most wealthy, but also the most intelligent, and many of them had received a good education in the mother country. The village was, for many years, governed by a board of trustees, who were appointed by the governor of the province, but who, after the English conquest, were elected by the people.
