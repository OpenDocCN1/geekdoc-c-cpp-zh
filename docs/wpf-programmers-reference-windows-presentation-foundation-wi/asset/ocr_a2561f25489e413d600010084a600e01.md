# Printing

Although some programs never need to produce “hard” output, printing is an important part of many applications. Printing lets you produce a permanent physical record of your work. It lets you make to give to your boss, pamphlets and newsletters to give to your neighbors, and doodles to stick on your refrigerator.

# AMAZING FACT

> According to HP Communities' Print 2.0 Blog, people printed around 45 trillion pages in 2005. Of those, roughly 9 percent, or 4.05 trillion, were printed with digital printers. (I don’t know how many were printed with WPF.)

WPF provides some remarkably powerful printing capabilities. One of the most impressive of these is the ability to produce transformed output with little or no loss of resolution. Because WPF's retained-mode graphics uses objects to represent graphical output, those objects can generate output even after they are transformed. That means a print can display a small piece of output zoomed to a large scale but still at a high resolution.

Figure 17-1 shows two prints of the same window at different scales. One on the left shows the window at its normal scale. One on the right shows the window greatly enlarged.

It's hard to tell in Figure 17-1, but both prints display smooth curves and lines even though the one on the right is enlarged. Further, perhaps to the point where the word New in the title area filled the entire page, it would show smooth lines and curves with none of the grainy or blocky appearance that you would see if you enlarged a bitmap.

This chapter explains printing in WPF. It explains how you print objects that inherit from the Visual class as the window shown in Figure 17-1. It also tells how you can print output generated by code and complex documents.
