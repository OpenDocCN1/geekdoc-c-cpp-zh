+   - Resizes, moves, and changes the corner radius of the rectangles that act as buttons.

+   - Changes the Fill and Stroke brushes for the rectangles pretending to be buttons.

+   - Changes the text displayed in the Labels.

+   - Moves and resizes the Image.

+   - Changes the background and foreground colors.

In addition to displaying different appearances, animated skins let the user watch as one interface morphs into another. The effect is extremely cool.

# THE PRICE OF COOLNESS

You might argue that coolness isn't really the focus in many applications, and you would be completely correct, but programmers who write skins aren't usually focused on getting by the least possible work. It's hard to argue that most serving anything other than an aesthetic purpose, so long as you're spending extra effort providing skins, it's not completely fair to say that the extra coolness of animated skins isn't worth the effort. By the same token, you could argue that you shouldn't even use WPF and stick with Windows programming, which is generally easy.

That said, however, that animating skins are a lot of time. Tweaking the animations to give everything right the position, size, and appearance takes time. The AnimatedSkins example program uses two storyboards (one for each skin) but 100 property animations to get everything right. And the differences between these two skins aren't as great as I've seen, you could spend a huge amount of time getting everything right.

One interesting side of this technique is that one animation doesn't need to finish before a new animation can start. For example, suppose you click on the blue circle in Figure 16-9 to switch to the blue. After the controls start moving to their new positions, you can click on the green circle shown in Figure 16-10. At that point, the controls immediately moving back to their positions for the green skin without going all the way to their blue positions.
