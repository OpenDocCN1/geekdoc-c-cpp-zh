The computers were to be connected with communications lines operating at speeds on the order of 50,000 bits per second, a stunning rate at a time when most people (of the few having networking access) were connecting to telephone lines at a rate of 110 bits per second. Academic research was about to take a giant leap forward. ARPA proceeded to implement what quickly became known as the ARPANET, the precursor to today's trillions-bits-per-second speeds on the horizon!

Things worked out differently from the original plan. Although the ARPANET enabled researchers to network their computers, its main benefit proved to be the capacity for quality easy communication via what came to be electronic mail (email). This is true on today's Internet, with instant messaging, file transfer and social media such as Facebook and Twitter enabling billions of people worldwide to communicate quickly and easily.

The protocol (set of rules) for communicating over the ARPANET became as the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP). TCP ensured that messages, consisting of sequentially numbered packets, were properly routed from sender to receiver, arrived intact and were assembled in the correct order.

# The Internet: A Network of Networks

In parallel with the evolution of the Internet, organizations worldwide were implementing their networks for both intraorganization (that is, within an organization) and interorganization (that is, between organizations). A huge variety of networking hardware and software appeared. One challenge was to enable these networks to communicate with each other. ARPA achieved this by developing the Internet Protocol (IP), which created a "network of networks," the current architecture of the Internet. The combined set of protocols is now called TCP/IP.

Businesses rapidly realized that using the Internet, could improve their offers and new better services to their clients. Companies started spending large amounts of money to develop and enhance their Internet presence. This generated fierce competition among communications carriers and hardware and software suppliers to meet the increased infrastructure demand. As a result, bandwidth—the information-carrying capacity of communications lines—on the Internet has increased tremendously, while hardware costs have plummeted.

# The Wide Web: Making the Internet User-Friendly

The Wide Web (simply called "the web") is a collection of hardware and software associated with the Internet that allows users to locate and view multimedia-based documents (documents with combinations of text, graphics, animations, audio and videos) over any subject. In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee of CERN (the Organization for Nuclear Research) began developing HyperText Markup Language (HTML)—the technology for sharing information via "hyperlinked" text documents. He also wrote communication protocols such as HyperText Transfer Protocol (HTTP) to form the backbone of his new hypertext information system, which he referred to as the Wide Web.
