## Making Objects from Classes

Just as someone has to build a car from his engineering drawings before you can actually drive a car, you must build an object from a class before a program can perform the tasks that the class's methods define. The process of doing this is called instantiation. An object is then referred to as an instance of its class.

## Reuse

Just as a car's engineering drawings can be reused many times to build many cars, you can reuse existing classes when building new classes and programs save time and effort. Reuse also helps when building more reliable and effective systems, because existing classes and components often have gone through extensive testing (to locate problems), debugging (to correct those problems) and performance tuning. Just the notion of interchangeable parts was crucial to the Industrial Revolution, reusable classes are crucial to the software revolution that's been spurred by technology.

## Messages and Method Calls

When you drive a car, pressing its pedal sends a message to the car to perform a task—that is, to go fast. Similarly, you send messages to an object. Each message is implemented as a method call that tells a method of the object to perform its task. For example, a program might call a particular bank-account's deposit method to increase the account's balance.

## Attributes and Instance Variables

A car, besides having capabilities to accomplish tasks, also has attributes, such as its color, its number of doors, the amount of gas in its tank, its speed and its record of miles driven (i.e., its odometer reading). Like its capabilities, the attributes are represented as part of its design in its engineering drawings (which, for example, include an odometer and a fuel gauge). As you drive a car, these attributes are carried along the car. Every car maintains its own attributes. For example, each car knows how much gas is in its gas tank, but not much is in the tanks of other cars.

An object, similarly, has attributes as part of its class. For example, a bank account has a balance attribute that represents the amount of money in the account. Each bank account knows the balance in the account it represents, but not the balances of the other accounts in the bank. Attributes are specified by the class's instance variables.

## Properties, get Accessors and set Accessors

Attributes are not necessarily accessible. The car manufacturer does not want drivers to take apart the car's engine to observe the amount of gas in its tank. Instead, the driver can check the fuel gauge on the dashboard. The bank does not want its customers to walk to the vault to check the amount of money in an account; check personalized online bank accounts. Similarly, you do not need to access an object instance variables in order to use them. You should use the properties for reading the values of variables, and set accessors for storing values into them.

## Encapsulation

Classes encapsulate (i.e., wrap) attributes and methods into objects created from those classes—an object's attributes and methods are intimately related. Objects communicate with one another, but they are not normally allowed to know how other objects are implemented—implementation details are hidden within the objects themselves. Information hiding, as we will see, is crucial to good software engineering.

## Inheritance

A new class of objects can be created quickly and conveniently by inheritance—the new class absorbs the characteristics of an existing class, customizing them and adding unique characteristics of its own. In our car analogy, an object of class "automobile," but more specifically, the "convertible"—the roof can be raised or lowered.

## 1.7 Internet and World Wide Web

In the late 1960s, ARPA—the Advanced Research Projects Agency of the United States Department of Defense—rolled out plans for networking the main computer systems of approximately a dozen ARPA-funded universities and research institutions. The computers were to be connected with communications lines operating at speeds on the order of 50,000 bits per second, a stunning rate at a time when most people (of the few having networking access) were connecting to telephone lines at a rate of 110 bits per second. Academic research was about to take a giant leap forward. ARPA proceeded to implement what quickly became known as the ARPANET, the precursor to today's trillions-bits-per-second speeds on the horizon!

Things worked out differently from the original plan. Although the ARPANET enabled researchers to network their computers, its main benefit proved to be the capacity for quality easy communication via what came to be electronic mail (email). This is true on today's Internet, with instant messaging, file transfer and social media such as Facebook and Twitter enabling billions of people worldwide to communicate quickly and easily.

The protocol (set of rules) for communicating over the ARPANET became the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP). TCP ensured that messages, consisting of sequentially numbered packets, were properly routed from sender to receiver, arrived intact and were assembled in the correct order.

## The Internet: A Network of Networks

In parallel with the evolution of the Internet, organizations worldwide were implementing their networks for both intraorganization (that is, within an organization) and interorganization (that is, between organizations). A huge variety of networking hardware and software appeared. One challenge was to enable these networks to communicate with each other. ARPA achieved this by developing the Internet Protocol (IP), which created a "network of networks," the current architecture of the Internet. The combined set of protocols is now called TCP/IP.

Businesses rapidly realized that using the Internet, could improve their offers and new better services to their clients. Companies started spending large amounts of money to develop and enhance their Internet presence. This generated fierce competition among communications carriers and hardware and software suppliers to meet the increased infrastructure demand. As a result, bandwidth—the information-carrying capacity of communications lines—on the Internet has increased tremendously, while hardware costs have plummeted.

## The Wide Web: Making the Internet User-Friendly

The Wide Web (simply called "the web") is a collection of hardware and software associated with the Internet that allows users to locate and view multimedia-based documents (documents with combinations of text, graphics, animations, audio and videos) over any subject. In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee of CERN (the Organization for Nuclear Research) began developing HyperText Markup Language (HTML)—the technology for sharing information via "hyperlinked" text documents. He also wrote communication protocols such as HyperText Transfer Protocol (HTTP) to form the backbone of his new hypertext information system, which he referred to as the Wide Web.
