# Making Objects from Classes

Just as someone has to build a car from his engineering drawings before you can actually drive a car, you must build an object from a class before a program can perform the tasks that the class's methods define. The process of doing this is called instantiation. An object is then referred to as instance of its class.

# Reuse

Just as a car's engineering drawings can be reused many times to build many cars, you can reuse existing classes when building new classes and programs save time and effort. Reuse also helps when building more reliable and effective systems, because existing classes and components often gone through extensive testing (to locate problems), debugging (to correct those problems) and performance tuning. just the notion of interchangeable parts was crucial to the Industrial Revolution, reusable classes are crucial to the software revolution that's been spurred by technology.

# Messages and Method Calls

When you drive a car, pressing its pedal sends a message to the car to perform a task—that is, to go fast. Similarly, you send messages to an object. Each message is implemented as a method call that tells a method of the object to perform its task. For example, a program might call a particular bank-account's deposit method to increase the account's balance.

# Attributes and Instance Variables

A car, besides having capabilities to accomplish tasks, also has attributes, such as its color, its number of doors, the amount of gas in its tank, its speed and its record of miles driven (i.e., its odometer reading). Like its capabilities, the attributes are represented as part of its design in its engineering drawings (which, for example, include an odometer and a fuel gauge). As you drive a car, these attributes are carried along the car. Every car maintains its own attributes. For example, each car knows how much gas is in its gas tank, but not much is in the tanks of other cars.

An object, similarly, has attributes as part of its class. For example, a bank account has a balance attribute that represents the amount of money in the account. Each bank account knows the balance in the account it represents, but not the balances of the other accounts in the bank. Attributes are specified by the class's instance variables.

# Properties, get Accessors and set Accessors

Attributes are not necessarily accessible. The car manufacturer does not want drivers to take apart the car's engine to observe the amount of gas in its tank. Instead, the driver can check the fuel gauge on the dashboard. The bank does not want its customers to walk to the vault to check the amount of money in an account. check personalized online bank accounts. Similarly, you do not need to access an object instance variables in order to use them. You should use the properties for reading the values of variables, and set accessors for storing values into them.
