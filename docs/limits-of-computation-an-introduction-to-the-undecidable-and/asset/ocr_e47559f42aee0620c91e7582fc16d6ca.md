This means that if two problems are tractable, then one can combine
easy problems in all standard ways and the result is tractable.
But why are NP-hard problems not easy? Why call them intractable?
This brings us to the boundaries of computer science knowledge.

There is no known polynomial algorithm for any NP-hard problem.
However, it has never been shown—andwill (almost certainly) never be
shown that NP-hard problems do not have polynomial solutions.
The incomplete theorem of Kurt Goddel shows that any theory will
have some statements that are true but never be proved, and (most)
computer scientists think that the nonexistence of polynomial solutions
for NP-hard problems is in this category.

In other words, it is believed that no one will find a polynomial time algo-ith for any NP-hard problem. It is also believed that no one will prove
at polynomial time algorithm does not exist. The person who can do
one of these—find a polynomial algorithm for some NP-hard prob-
em or show that one cannot exist—will be immediately famous, at least in
t world of theoretical computer science. See the Web site referenced in
Exercise 11 at Claymath.org for a temptation to take on this problem.

If either of these is achieved, the whole theory of complexity will essen-
tially collapse, and the phrases NP-hard and NP-complete never again be
mentioned in any computer science class, except perhaps in passing refer-
 to the ignorance of the ancients.
