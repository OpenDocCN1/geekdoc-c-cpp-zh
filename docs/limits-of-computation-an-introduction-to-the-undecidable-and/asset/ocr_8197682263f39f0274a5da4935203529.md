Ambiguity in grammars is important to language design and compiler writing. For example, no ambiguous grammar is suitable for either LL or LR parsing (although there are sometimes ways to get around the problem and use a grammar, even though it is ambiguous). Thus, knowing whether or a grammar is ambiguous is a question of interest.

# Theorem 7.20

It is undecidable whether a Context Free Grammar (CFG) G is ambiguous.

# Proof

By reduction from PCP, that is, PCP ≤ Grammar_G_ambiguous. Given an instance of PCP with tiles

| a_1 | a_n |
| b_1 | ... | b_n |

the task is to construct a grammar G that is ambiguous if and only if the instance of PCP has a solution.

The construction, by Floyd (1962), builds two grammars with start symbols A and B, and combines them into one with start S and Rules → A|B.
A grammar uses the a_i strings from the top of the tiles, plus "index variables" r_1, ..., r_n.
The rules are: A → a_1Ar_1 | a_2Ar_2 | ... | a_nAr_n | a_1r_1 | a_2r_2 | ... … a_nr_n.
This grammar is not ambiguous, and generates strings of the form (some string of a_i) (some string of r_j) with matching lengths and reversed indices.

