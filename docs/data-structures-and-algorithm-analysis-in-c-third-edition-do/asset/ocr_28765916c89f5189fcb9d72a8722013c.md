# 14.2 Recurrence Relations

Recurrence relations are often used to model the cost of recursive functions. For example, the standard Mergesort (Section 7.4) takes a list of size $n$, splits it in half, performs Mergesort on each half, and finally merges the two sublists in $n$ steps. The cost for this can be modeled

```
$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n.$
```

In other words, the cost of the algorithm on size $n$ is two times the cost for input of size $n/2$ (due to the two recursive calls to Mergesort) plus (the time to merge the sublists)

There are many approaches to solve recurrence relations, and we briefly describe three here. The first is an estimation technique: Guess the upper and lower bounds for the recurrence, use induction to prove the bounds, and tighten as required. The second approach is expand the recurrence to convert it to a summation and then use summation techniques. The third approach is to take advantage of already proven theorems when the recurrence is of a suitable form. In particular, divide and conquer algorithms such as Mergesort yield recurrences of a form that fits a pattern for which we have a ready solution.

# 14.2.1 Estimating Upper and Lower Bounds

The first approach to solving recurrences is to guess the answer and attempt to prove it. If a correct upper or lower bound is given, an easy induction proof will verify this. If the proof is successful, then try to tighten the bound. If the proof fails, then loosen the bound and again. Once the upper and lower bounds match, you are finished. This is a useful technique when you are only looking for asymptotic complexities. When seeking a precise closed-form solution (i.e., you seek the constants for the expression), this method will probably be too much work.
