# Sec. 15.1 Introduction to Lower Proofs

Now we know precisely what we are aiming for when designing an algorithm: We want to find an algorithm who's upper bound matches the lower bound of the problem. Putting together all that we know far about algorithms, we can organize our thinking into the following “algorithm for designing algorithms."\2

+   - If the upper and lower bounds match,
 stop,
 else if the bounds are close or the problem isn't important,
 stop,
 else if the problem definition focuses on the wrong thing,
 then restate it,
 else if the algorithm is too slow,
 then find a faster algorithm,
 else lower bound is too weak,
 then generate a stronger bound.

We can repeat this process until we are exhausted. This brings us smack one of the toughest tasks in analysis. Lower bounds are notoriously difficult to construct. The problem is coming with arguments that truly cover all of the things that any algorithm possibly could. The most common fallacy is to argue from the point of view of what some good algorithm actually does, and that any algorithm must do the same. This is simply not true, and any lower bound proof that refers to specific behavior that must take place should be viewed with suspicion.

Let us consider the Towers of Hanoi problem.. Recall from Section 2.5 as our basic algorithm is to move $n-1$ disks (recursively) to the middle pole, move the disk to the third pole, and then move $n-1$ disks (again recursively) from the middle to the third pole. This algorithm generates the recurrence $T(n) = 2T(n-1) + 1 = 2^n - 1$. So, the upper bound for our algorithm is $2^n - 1$. But is this the best for the problem?

For our first try at a lower bound proof, the "trivial" lower bound is that we must move every disk at least once, for a minimum cost of n. Slightly better is to observe that get the biggest disk to the third pole, we must move every other disk at least twice (once to get it off the bottom, and once to get them to the third pole). This yields a cost of $2n-1$, which is still not a good match for our algorithm. Is the problem in the algorithm or in the lower bound?
