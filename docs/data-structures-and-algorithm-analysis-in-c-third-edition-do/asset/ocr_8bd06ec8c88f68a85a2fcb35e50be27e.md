One application that makes use of some ADT might use particular member functions of ADT more than a second application, or the two applications might have different time requirements for the various operations. These differences in the requirements of applications are the reason why a given ADT might be supported by more than one implementation.

**Example 1.5** Two popular implementations for large disk-based database applications are hashing (Section 9.4) and the B+-tree (Section 10.5). Both support efficient insertion and deletion of records, and both support exact-match queries. However, hashing is more efficient than the B+-tree for exact-match queries. On the other hand, the B+-tree can perform exact-range queries efficiently, while hashing is hopelessly inefficient for range queries. Thus, if the database application searches to exact-match queries, hashing is preferred. On the other hand, if an application requires support for range queries, the B+-tree is preferred. Despite this performance issues, both implementations solve versions of the problem: updating and searching a large collection of records.

The concept of an ADT can help us focus on key issues in non-computing applications.

**Example 1.6** When operating a car, the primary activities are steering, accelerating, and braking. Nearly all passenger cars, you steer by turning the wheel, accelerate by pushing the gas pedal, and brake by pushing the brake pedal. This design for car can be viewed as an ADT with operations (“accelerate,” and “brake.” Two cars might implement these operations in radically different ways, with different types of engine, or front versus rear-wheel drive. Yet, most drivers can operate different cars because the ADT presents a uniform method of operation that does not require the driver to understand the specifics of any particular engine or design. These differences are deliberately hidden.

The concept of an ADT is one instance of an important principle that must be understood by any successful computer scientist: managing complexity through abstraction. A central theme of computer science is complexity and techniques for handling it. Humans deal with complexity by assigning a label to an assembly of objects or concepts and then manipulating the label in place of the assembly. Cognitive psychologists call such a label a *metaphor*. A particular label might be related to other pieces of information or other labels. This collection can turn in be given a label, forming a hierarchy of concepts and labels. This hierarchy of labels allows us to focus on important issues while ignoring unnecessary details.
