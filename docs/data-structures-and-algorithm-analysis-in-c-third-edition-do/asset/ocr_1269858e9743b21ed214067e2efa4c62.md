**Figure 15.7** Merge insert sort for ten elements. First five of elements are compared. The five winners are then sorted. This leaves the elements labeled 1-4 to be sorted into the chain by the remaining six elements.

Call the optimal cost for $n$ elements $S(n)$. We know that $S(n+1) \le S(n) + [log(n+1)]$ because we sort elements and use binary insert for the last one. For all $m$, $S(n+m) \le S(n) + M(m,n)$ where $M(m,n)$ is the best time to merge two sorted lists. For $n=47$, it turns out that we can do better by splitting the list into pieces of size 5 and 42, and then merging. merge sort is not quite optimal. But it is extremely good, and nearly optimal for smaller numbers of elements.

# 15.8 Further Reading

Much of the material in this book is also covered in many other textbooks on structures and algorithms. The biggest exception is that many other textbooks cover lower bounds in any significant detail, as is done in this chapter. Those that focus on the same example problems (search and selection) because it tells a tight and compelling story regarding related topics, while showing off the major techniques for lower bounds. Two examples of such textbooks are “Computer Algorithms” by Baase and Van Gelder [BG00], and “Connected to What?” by Gregory.E. Rawls [Raw92]. “Fundamentals of Algorithms” by Brass and Bratley [BB96] also cover lower bounds.
