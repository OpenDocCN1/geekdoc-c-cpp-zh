For example, that the algorithm starts at P in Figure 6-6 and, trying to find a path Y, it visits W, S, and T. There is no reason for the algorithm to consider the flight from T to W because W is already in the stack. Any city you could fly to by flying from W to S, from S to T, and back to W—such as Y—you do not allow the algorithm to visit W a second time, it will backtrack from S and T to W and then go directly to Y. Notice that backtracking to W is very different from visiting W for a second time.

+   * If you have visited city C, it is no longer in the stack—because you backtracked from it and popped it from the stack—you do not want to visit C again. This situation is subtle; consider two cases that depend on why you backtracked from the city.

If you backtracked from C because there were no flights out of it, you certainly do not ever want to try going through C again. For example, if starting at P in Figure 6-6, the algorithm goes to R and then to X, will backtrack from X to R. At this point, although X is no longer in the stack, you certainly do not want to visit again, because you know there are no flights out of X.

Now suppose that you backtracked from C because all cities adjacent to it had been visited. This situation implies that you have already tried all possible flights from C and failed to find a way to get to the destination city. There is no reason to go to C again. For example, suppose starting from P in Figure 6-6, the algorithm executes the following sequence: Visit R, visit X, backtrack to R (because there are no flights out of X), backtrack to P (because there are no unvisited cities adjacent to R), visit W, visit Y. At this point, the stack contains P-W-Y on top, as Figure 6-b shows. You need to choose a flight out of Y. You do not want to fly from Y to R because you have visited R and tried all possible flights out of R.

In both cases, visiting a city second time does not gain you anything, and in fact, it may cause you to go around in circles.
