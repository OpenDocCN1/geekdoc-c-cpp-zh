# 13.4 Application: Simulation

Simulation—a major application area for computers—is a technique for modeling the behavior of both natural and human systems. Generally, the goal of a simulation is to generate statistics that summarize the performance of an existing system or to predict the performance of a proposed system. In this section we will consider a simple example that illustrates important type of simulation.

## Simulation models the behavior of systems

A problem to solve. Ms. Simpson, president of the First City Bank of Springfield, has her customers complain about how long they have to wait for service at the branch located in a downtown grocery store. Because she is losing customers to another bank, she is considering whether to hire a second teller for that branch.

Before Ms. Simpson hires another teller, she would like an approximation of the average time a customer has to wait for service from that branch's only teller. Ms. Simpson heard you were great at solving problems and came to you for help. How can you obtain this information for Ms. Simpson?

Considerations. You could stand with a stopwatch in the lobby all day, but that task is not particularly exciting. Besides, you should use an approach that also allows Ms. Simpson to predict how much improvement she could expect if the bank hired a given number of additional tellers. She certainly does not want to hire the tellers on a trial basis and to monitor the bank's performance before making her final decision.

You conclude that the best way to obtain the information needed is to use a computer model to simulate the behavior of the bank. The first step in simulating a system such as a bank is to construct a mathematical model that captures the relevant information about the system. For example, how many tellers does the bank employ? How often do customers arrive? How long do the customers transactions take?

If the model accurately describes the real-world system, a simulation can derive accurate predictions about the system's performance. For example, a simulation can predict the average time a customer has to wait before service. A simulation can also evaluate proposed changes to the real-world system, such as predicting the effect of hiring more tellers at the bank. A large increase in the time predicted for the average wait of a customer might justify the cost of hiring additional tellers.

After discussing the problem with Ms. Simpson, you decide that you want the simulation to:

- The average time a customer waits for service from the current teller
- The decrease in customer time with each new teller

Simulation time and events. Central to a simulation is the concept of simulated time. Envision a stopwatch that measures elapsed during a simulation. For example, suppose that the model of the bank specifies only one teller. At time 0, which is the start of the banking day, the simulated system would be in its initial state with no customers. As the simulation runs, the stopwatch ticks units of time—minutes—and events occur. At time 20, the first customer arrives. Because there is no teller, the customer goes directly to the teller and begins her transaction, which will take about 6 minutes to complete. At time 22, the second customer arrives. Because the first customer has not completed her transaction, the second customer must wait. The first customer completes her transaction and the second customer can begin. Figure 13-6 illustrates these four times in the simulation.

To gather the information you need, run this simulation for a specified period of time. During the course of the run, you need to keep track of certain statistics, as the average time a customer has to wait for service. Notice that in the small example of Figure 13-6, the first customer had to wait 0 minutes to begin a transaction and the second customer had to wait 4 minutes to begin a transaction—an average of 2 minutes.

One point not addressed in the previous discussion is how to determine when events occur. For example, why did we say the first customer arrived at time 20 and the second at time 22? After studying real-world systems like our bank, learned events such as the arrival of people using techniques from probability theory. This statistical information is incorporated into the mathematical model of the system and is used to generate events in a way that reflects the real world. The simulation uses these events and is called an **event-driven simulation**. Note that the goal is to reflect the long-term behavior of the system rather than to predict occurrences of specific events.

Although the techniques for generating events to reflect the real world are interesting and important, they require a good deal of sophistication. Therefore, we assume that we already have a list of events available for our use. In particular, for the bank, we assume that a file contains the time of each customer's arrival—an **arrival event**—and the duration of the transaction once the customer reaches the teller.
