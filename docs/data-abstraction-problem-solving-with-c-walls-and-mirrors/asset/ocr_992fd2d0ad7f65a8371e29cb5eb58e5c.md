Place queens one column at a time

Suppose you provide some organization for the guessing by placing one queen in column, with the first square of column 1. Figure 5-7a shows this column and its range of attack. When you consider column 2, you eliminate your first square because row 1 contains a queen, you eliminate your second square because of a diagonal attack, and you finally place a queen in the third square of column 2, as Figure 5-7b illustrates. The blue dots in the figure indicate squares that are rejected because of an attack. The blue dots also indicate the additional squares that your new queen can attack.

If you reach an impasse, backtrack to the previous column

We continue to place queens in this manner until we get to column 6, as Figure 5-7 shows. Although the five queens placed cannot attack each other, they can attack any square in column 6, and therefore, you cannot place a queen in column 6. You must back up to column 5 and move your queen to the next possible square in column 5, which is in the last row, as Figure 5-7i indicates. When you consider column 6 once again, there are still no choices for a queen in that column. Because you have exhausted the possibilities in column 5, you must back up to column 4. As Figure 5-7g shows, the next possible square in column 4 is in row 7. You then consider column 5 again and place a queen in row 2 (Figure 5-7h).

How can use recursion in the process that was just described? Consider an algorithm that places a queen in a column, given that you have placed queens correctly in the preceding columns.
