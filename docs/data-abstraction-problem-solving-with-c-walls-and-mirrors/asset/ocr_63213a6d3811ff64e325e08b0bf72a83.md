Together, a selection sort of $n$ items requires

$n \times (n-1)/2 + 3 \times (a-1) = n^2/2 + 5 \times n/2$

major operations. By applying the properties of the growth-rate functions in Chapter 10, you can ignore lower-order terms to get $O(n^2)$ and then ignore the multiplier $1/2$ to get $O(n^2)$. Thus, the selection sort is $O(n^2)$.

Although a selection sort does not depend on the initial arrangement of the data, which is an advantage of this algorithm, it is only appropriate for small $n$ because $O(n^2)$ grows rapidly. The algorithm requires $O(n^2)$ comparisons, it requires $O(n)$ data moves. A selection sort could be a good choice over when moves are costly but comparisons are not. Such might be the case if each item is long the sort key is short.

Selection sort is $O(n^2)$

> Note: If you are sorting a large array, an $O(n^2)$ algorithm is too inefficient.

# 11.1.2 The Bubble Sort

The next sorting algorithm is one that you have already seen. The bubble sort compares adjacent items and exchanges them if they are out of order. This usually requires several passes over the data. During the first pass, you compare the first items in the next. If they are out of order, you exchange them. Then you compare the items in the next pair—that is, in positions 2 and 3 of the array. If they are out of order, you exchange them. Then you proceed in the same manner, comparing and exchanging items two at a time, until you reach the end of the array.

Figure 12a illustrates the first pass of a bubble sort of five integers. You compare the items in the first pair—29 and 10—and exchange them because they are out of order. Next you consider the second pair—29 and 14—and exchange these items because they are out of order. The items in the third pair—29 and 37—are in order, so you do not exchange them. Finally, you exchange the items in the last pair—37 and 13—and 13.

When you order successive passes of items, the largest item bubbles to the top (end) of the array
