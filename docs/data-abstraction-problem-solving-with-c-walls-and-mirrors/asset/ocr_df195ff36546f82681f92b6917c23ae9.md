# 10.1 What Is a Good Solution?

In this textbook a computer program is the final solutions, so we should consider what constitutes a good program. Presumably, you write a program to perform some task. While performing that task, the program incurs a real and tangible cost. This cost includes factors as the computing time and memory that the program requires, the difficulties encountered by those who use the program, and the consequences of a program that do not behave correctly.

However, the costs just mentioned do not give the whole picture. They pertain only to the life of the solution after it has been deployed. In assessing whether a solution is good, you must consider the effort to develop the solution as any changes—bug fixes or extensions—to the program that are made after the program has been deployed. Each of these costs counts too. The total cost of a solution must take into account the value of time the program you analyzed, designed, debugged and tested. A solution's cost must also include the cost of maintaining, modifying, and expanding it.

Thus, when calculating the overall cost of a solution, you must include a diverse set of factors. If you adopt a multidimensional view of cost, it is reasonable to evaluate a solution against the following criteria:

+   - A solution is good if the cost incurs over all phases of its life is minimal.

Suppose two algorithms perform the same task, such as searching. The faster one is not necessarily better. Several components contribute to the cost of a computer program, including the cost of human time—the time of the people who develop, maintain, and use the program—and the cost of program execution—that is, its efficiency—namely, its efficiency—measured by the amount of computing time and memory that the program requires to execute.

Today's programs are larger and more complex than ever. Typically, many people are involved in their development. Good structure and documentation are of the utmost importance. In addition, the costs associated with malfunctions are high. People should not have to entrust their livelihoods—or lives—to a program that only its authors can understand and maintain. Thus, society needs well-structured programs and techniques for formally verifying their correctness.

At the same time, do not get the impression that a solution's efficiency is the prime determinant of whether a solution is effective. However, a solution's efficiency is only one of many aspects that you must consider. If two solutions have the same efficiency, other factors should dominate the comparison. However, even when the efficiencies of solutions are similar, that can be a major concern.

You should be concerned about efficiency when you are developing the algorithm. The choice of a solution's components—the objects and the design of the interactions between them—rather than the code you write, which has the most significant impact on efficiency. This book advocates a problem-solving philosophy that views the cost of a solution as multidimensional. This philosophy is reasonable in today's world, and it will likely be reasonable in the years to come.

> The relative importance of the various components of a solution's cost has changed since the early days of computing. In the beginning, the cost of computer time relative to human time was extremely high. In this type of environment, one clearly overshadowed all others: computer resources. If two programs performed the same task, the one that required less time and memory was better.

Computing costs have dropped dramatically since the early days of computers. Today, the value of the designers' and programmers' time is a much more significant factor than computing time in the cost of a solution. These developments have made obsolete the notion that the fastest-executing solution is always the best.
