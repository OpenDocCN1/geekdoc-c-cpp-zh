The first step in simulating a system such as a bank is to construct a mathematical model that captures the relevant information about the system. For example, how many tellers does the bank employ? How often do customers arrive? How long do the customers transactions take?

If the model accurately describes the real-world system, a simulation can derive accurate predictions about the system's performance. For example, a simulation can predict the average time a customer has to wait before service. A simulation can also evaluate proposed changes to the real-world system, such as predicting the effect of hiring more tellers at the bank. A large increase in the time predicted for the average wait of a customer might justify the cost of hiring additional tellers.

Simulation time and events. Central to a simulation is the concept of simulated time. Envision a stopwatch that measures elapsed during a simulation. For example, suppose that the model of the bank specifies only one teller. At time 0, which is the start of the banking day, the simulated system would be in its initial state with no customers. As the simulation runs, the stopwatch ticks units of time—minutes—and events occur. At time 20, the first customer arrives. Because there is no teller, the customer goes directly to the teller and begins her transaction, which will take about 6 minutes to complete. At time 22, the second customer arrives. Because the first customer has not completed her transaction, the second customer must wait. The first customer completes her transaction and the second customer can begin. One point not addressed in the previous discussion is how to determine when events occur. For example, why did we say the first customer arrived at time 20 and the second at time 22? After studying real-world systems like our bank, learned events such as the arrival of people using techniques from probability theory. This statistical information is incorporated into the mathematical model of the system and is used to generate events in a way that reflects the real world. The simulation uses these events and is called an **event-driven simulation**. Note that the goal is to reflect the long-term behavior of the system rather than to predict occurrences of specific events. This goal is sufficient for the needs of our simulation.

Although the techniques for generating events to reflect the real world are interesting and important, they require a good deal of mathematical sophistication. Therefore, we assume that we already have a list of events available for our use. In particular, for the bank, we assume that a file contains the time of each customer's arrival—an **arrival event**—and the duration of the customer's transaction once the customer reaches the teller.
