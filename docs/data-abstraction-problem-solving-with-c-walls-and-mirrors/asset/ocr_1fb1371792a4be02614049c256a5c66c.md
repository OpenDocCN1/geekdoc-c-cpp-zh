# 11.2.3 The Radix Sort

The radix sort is included here because it is quite different from the other sorts we've described, as it does not compare the array's entries.
Imagine that you are sorting a hand of cards. You pick the cards one at a time and arrange them by rank into 13 possible groups of: 2, 3...10, J, Q, K, A. Combine these groups and place the cards on the table so that the 2s are on top and the aces are on the bottom. Now pick up the cards one at a time and sort them by into four groups in this order: clubs, diamonds, hearts, and spades. When taken together, the groups results in a sorted hand of cards.
A radix sort uses this idea of forming groups and then combining them to sort a collection of data. The sort treats each item as a character string. As a first example of a radix sort, consider this collection of three-letter strings:

ABC, YWZ, BWZ, AAC, RLT, JBX, RDT, KLT, AEO, TLJ

The sort begins by organizing the data according to the rightmost (least significant) letters. Although none of the strings ends in A or B, two place these strings into a group. Continuing through the alphabet, you perform the following:
(ABC, AAC) (TLJ) (AEO) (RLT, RDT, KLT, JBX) (XYZ, BWZ)
The strings in each group end with the same letter, and the groups are ordered by letter. In addition, the strings within each group retain their relative order from the original list of strings.
Now combine the groups into one as follows. Take the items in the first group in their present order, follow them with the items in the second group in their present, and so on. The following group results:
ABC, AAC, TLI, AEO, RLT, RDT, KLT, JBX, XYZ, BWZ
