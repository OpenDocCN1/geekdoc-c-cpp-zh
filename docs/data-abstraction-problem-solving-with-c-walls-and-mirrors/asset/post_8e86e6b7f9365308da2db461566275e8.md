## 13.5 Position-Oriented and Value-Oriented ADTs

Of the abstract data types that we have seen so far, the stack, the list, and the queue have operations defined in terms of the positions of data items. We call them position-oriented ADTs. Stacks and queues greatly restrict the positions that they can affect; only their ends can be accessed. The list removes this restriction.

The ADT sorted is an example of a value-oriented ADT. When a new item is added to a sorted list, the sorted list determines the item's placement according to its value. Unlike a list, a sorted list cannot choose where to place items, since it orders its entries by their priorities, which are values that are part of each entry.

Although stacks and queues order their entries, and you do not specify whether a stack or a queue should place its items, the entries' positions are not determined by their values. Thus, we do not classify stacks and queues as value-oriented.

> Note: The ADTs stack, queue, sorted list, and priority queue order their entries. The stack, queue, and list are position oriented; the sorted list and priority queue are value oriented. The ADT bag does not order its entries; it is neither position oriented nor oriented.

Stacks are really similar to queues. This similarity becomes apparent if you pair off their operations, as follows:

- *isEmpty and enqueue*: isEmpty see whether any items exists in the ADT.
- *push and enqueue*: insert a new item into one end (the top and back, respectively) of the ADT.
- *pop and dequeue*: The pop operation removes the most recent item, which is at the top of the stack, and dequeue removes the first item, which is at the front of the queue.
- *peek and peekFront*: The peek operation retrieves the most recent item, which is at the top of the stack, and peekFront retrieves the first item at the front of the queue.

The ADT list allows you to insert, remove, and inspect any item at any position of the list. Thus, it is the most flexible of the three position-oriented ADTs. You can view the list operations as general versions of the stack and queue operations as follows:

- *length*: If you ignore the restriction that the stack and queue versions of isEmpty see only when an item is present, you obtain an operation that can count the number of items that are present.
- *Insert*: If you ignore the restriction that pop and enqueue insert new items into only one position, you obtain an operation that can insert a new item into any position of the list.
- *remove*: If you ignore the restriction that pop and dequeue remove items from only one position, you obtain an operation that can remove an item from any position of the list.
- *get*: If you ignore the restriction that peek and peekFront can retrieve items from only one position, you obtain an operation that can retrieve the item from any position of the list.

# SUMMARY

1. The definition of the queue operations gives the ADT queue first-in, first-out (FIFO) behavior.
2. Models of real-world systems often use queues. The event-driven simulation in this chapter used a queue to model line of customers in a bank.
3. Central to a simulation is the notion of simulated time. In a time-driven simulation, simulated time is advanced by a single time, whereas in an event-driven simulation, simulated time is advanced by the time of the next event. The event-driven simulation uses a queue to maintain events that have not occurred.
4. A priority queue allows to retrieve or remove the item with the highest priority.
5. ADTs are classified as either position oriented or value oriented. A position-oriented ADT organizes items by their positions, whereas a value-oriented ADT organizes by their values.
