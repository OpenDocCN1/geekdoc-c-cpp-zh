An everyday list provides a way for us to organize our thoughts, plans, or tasks. Each list has a first entry, a last entry, and often entries in between. The entries have the position within the list, and therefore they are ordered. In computer science, an ADT list provides a way to organize data. Like everyday list, the entries in an ADT list have a position and are ordered. This order is not determined by the list itself, but rather by its client.
This chapter specifies the ADT list and gives some examples of how to use it.

## 8.1 Specifying the ADT List

Consider a list that you may encounter, such as a list of chores, a list of important dates, a list of addresses, or the grocery list pictured in Figure 8-1. Where do you put new items? Assuming that you have a neat-column list, you could add items to the end of the list. You could just as well add items to the beginning of the list or that your list is sorted alphabetically. Regardless, the items appear in a sequence. The list has first item and last item. For the first and last items, each item has a unique predecessor and a unique successor. The first item—the head of the list—does not have a predecessor, and the last item—the tail or end of the list—does not have a successor.

Although the items in the list in Figure 8-1 have a sequential order, they are not necessarily sorted. They appear in the order in which they occurred to you on the shelves, or more likely they appear in the order in which you wrote the list.

Lists contain items of the same type. You can have a list of grocery items or a list of phone numbers. You might count the items on the list, add an item to the list, remove an item from the list, or retrieve an item. The items on a list, together with operations you can perform on them, form an ADT: the ADT list.

![](img/d0cb86b2f18b2db569968ad7c743510e_280_0.png)

> **Note:** The ADT list is simply a container of items whose order you indicate and whose position you refer by.

You reference list items by their position

You must specify the behavior of the list's operations without thinking about how you could implement them. For example, you might decide where to add a new item and which to retrieve or remove. The various possible decisions lead to several kinds of lists. You might decide to add, remove, and retrieve items only at the end of the list, only at the front, or at both the front and the end. The specifications of these lists, which manipulate items at one or both ends, are left as an exercise, they are not really adequate for an actual grocery list. Instead we will discuss a more general list, one that allows you to access items anywhere on the list. That is, you might look the item at position $i$, remove the item at position $i$, or insert an item at position $i$ on the list. Such operations are part of the ADT list. Other operations that check whether a list is empty or return the length of the list are also useful.
