# CHAPTER 2 Recursion: The Mirrors

The goal of this chapter is to ensure you have a basic understanding of recursion, which is one of the most powerful techniques available to the computer scientist. This chapter assumes that you have little or no previous introduction to recursion. If, however, you have already studied recursion, you can review this chapter as necessary.

By presenting relatively simple problems, the chapter demonstrates the techniques that lead to recursive solutions. These problems are diverse and include examples of counting, searching, and organization. In addition to presenting recursion from a conceptual viewpoint, this chapter discusses techniques that will help you understand the mechanics of recursion. These techniques are particularly useful for tracing and debugging recursive functions.

Some recursive functions are more elegant and concise than the best of nonrecursive counterparts. For example, the classic Towers of Hanoi problems may be quite difficult, yet it an extremely simple recursive solution. On the other hand, some recursive solutions are terribly inefficient, as you will see, and should not be used.

Chapter 5 continues the formal discussion of recursion by examining more difficult problems. Recursion will play a major role in many of the solutions that appear throughout the remainder of this book.

# 2.1 Recursive Solutions

Recursion is an extremely powerful problem-solving technique. Problems that at first appear to be quite difficult often have simple recursive solutions. Other problem-solving techniques, recursion breaks a problem into smaller problems. What is striking about recursion is these smaller problems are of the same type as the original problem—mirror images, so to speak.

Did you hold a mirror in front of another mirror so the two mirrors face each other? You will see many images of yourself, each behind and slightly smaller than the other. Recursion is like mirror images. That, a recursive solution solves a problem by solving a smaller instance of the same problem! It then solves this new problem by solving an smaller instance of the same problem. Eventually, the problem will be so small that its solution will lead to the solution of the original problem.

For example, suppose you could solve problem $P_i$ if you had the solution $P_j$, which is a smaller instance of $P_i$. Suppose that you could solve problem $P_j$, if you had the solution to problem $P_k$, which is a smaller instance of $P_j$. If it was small enough to reach, you would be able to solve $P_k$. You could then use the solution to $P_k$ to solve the original problem $P_i$.

Recursion can seem magic, especially at first, but as you will see, it is a real and important problem-solving approach that is alternative to iteration. An iteration solution involves loops. You should know that not all recursive solutions are better than iterative ones. In fact, some recursive solutions are impractical because they are so inefficient. However, recursion can provide elegant solutions to problems of great complexity.

As an illustration of the elements in a recursive solution, consider the problem of looking up a word in a dictionary. Suppose you wanted to look up "vacademem". Imagine starting at the beginning of the dictionary and looking at every word until you find "vacademem". That is what sequential search does, and for obvious reasons, you want a faster way to perform search.
