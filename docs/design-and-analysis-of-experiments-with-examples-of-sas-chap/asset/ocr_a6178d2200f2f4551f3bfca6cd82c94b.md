where \(y_{ijk}\) is the kth measurement (\(k = 1, ..., r\)) made by the \(j\)th operator (\(j = 1, ..., B\)) on the \(i\)th part (\(i = 1, ..., A\)), \(a_i\) is the part effect, \(b_j\) is the operator or inspector effect, and \(ab_{ij}\) is the interaction effect.

The difference between this and model (3.2) in Chapter 3 is the fact that the effects \(a_i\), \(b_j\) and \(ab_{ij}\) are now assumed to be independent, normally distributed random variables with zero means and variances \(\sigma_a^2\), \(\sigma_b^2\), and \(\sigma_{ab}^2\). Since the variance of a sum of independent random variables is the sum of the variances, the total variance in the response \(Var(y) = \sigma_y^2 = \sigma_a^2 + \sigma_b^2 + \sigma_{ab}^2 + \sigma^2\). \(\sigma_a^2\) represents the portion of the total variance due to actual differences in part features, \(\sigma_b^2\) is the portion of the variance caused by differences among operators, \(\sigma_{ab}^2\) is the portion of the variance caused by the interaction of operator and part, and \(\sigma^2\) is the portion of the variance caused by replicate measurements or gage repeatability. The sum of \(\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_{ab}^2\) is the gage reproducibility. The repeatability plus reproducibility \(\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_{ab}^2 + \sigma^2\) is a measure of the variance attributable to measurement error.

# 5.5.1 Estimating Variance Components

For the case with an equal number of replicates per subclass, like the example in Table 5.5, it is convenient to use either the method of moments or REML estimators of the variance components. For example the SAS commands to read the gage R&R data and compute the estimates are shown below:

options ls=64;
data gagerr;
input part oper y;
datalines;
1 1 .71
1 1 .69
1 2 .68
1 2 .70
proc varcomp data=gagerr method=type1;
class part oper;
model y=part oper*oper;
run;
proc varcomp data=gagerr method=reml;
class part oper;
model y=part oper*oper;
run;
