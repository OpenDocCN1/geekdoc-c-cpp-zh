Cufflinks extends the model of Jiang and Wang (43) for the isoform expression estimation problem to paired-end reads, incorporating a length distribution. Since it specifies the probability of obtaining a fragment by a linear function of transcript abundances, the corresponding log-likelihood function is concave and its unique maximum can be determined by a hill-climbing method. Cufflinks assigned ML estimates close to zero by an *expectation maximization* (EM) algorithm are accounted for in Cufflinks with an additional regularization step. A *maximum posteriori* estimates are determined by sampling from the posterior distribution using the multivariate Gaussian with mean equal to the MLE as proposal distribution.

iReck is based on a similar likelihood function introduced in (44) for the estimation of gene expression through RNA-seq mapping accuracy. An EM algorithm tackles the interdependence of assignment of reads to isoforms and their abundance estimation by an iterative approach. Based on the current estimates of the model parameters, the transcript abundances, (expected) assignments of fragments is computed (E-step), which in turn allow to re-evaluate the estimates of transcript abundances (M-step). In contrast to Cufflinks, iReck model does not only comprise a selected (minimal) set of isoforms but also all candidate transcripts. To prevent overfitting, iReck augments the objective by a regularizing penalty term (see Section 11.3.2,2), which requires an addition of the M-step to a numerical optimization scheme and demands restarts over the loss of concavity.

## 11.3.4 Completeness

In the most general sense, completeness refers to the property that all observations, or reads, can be explained by at least one predicted transcript. In particular, if the set or even number of isoforms is determined in the absence of a measure of *accuracy*, two-stage optimization approaches such as Cufflinks, CLASS, and CLIQ counteract the problem of *minimality* objective by a notion of *completeness*: The isoform reconstruction problem is that instance of a *covering* problem, requiring every fragment (Cufflinks) or constraint (CLASS) to be explained by one of the reconstructed transcripts.

Since CLIQ in a first stage does not attempt to minimize a loss function minimizes the number of isoforms needed to bound the estimation error, it introduces a covering constraint that requires the estimated number of reads to be at least the minimum number of reads observed on any exon junction. TRIP accounts for a relatively coarse measure of accuracy by requiring every junction to appear in at least one predicted transcript.

In contrast, the set cover instance solved by IsoInfer merges the solutions obtained for substances of the original isoform reconstruction problem. To cope with the combinatorial complexity of the problem, IsoInfer decomposes the instance into substances and solves them independently. The solution to the set cover instance combines the solutions to the substances in such a way that a small subset of the most frequent transcripts explain (cover) all the reads and all provided transcription start and polyadenylation sites.

When isoforms and their expression levels are obtained through minimizing a loss function that measures the deviation of the estimated number of reads from the observed data (see Section 11.3 for an example), incompleteness (underestimation) is penalized in a global attempt to balance deviations in both directions, by using positive or negative values of $\epsilon$ in (11.2) simultaneously across all observations $b_j$. Despite minimizing (11.3), however, Isolasso sets additional lower bounds for completeness by introducing constraints that require the estimated number of reads on each observed segment to be above a certain threshold.

## 11.3.5 Extensions

In the previous sections, we described the general principles underlying current methods for the transcript assembly problem. In practice, many features play an important role in the applicability of a software tool in a specific context. Clearly, the extent to which a method depends on an existing annotation, from being completely independent of any annotation, as Cufflinks, to incorporating the known transcript start sites and polyadenylation sites, as is the case for iReck, determines the suitability of a method for the problem at hand. Methods also consider gravely in the way they employ read pairing information. Some methods ignore this information completely (TRAPH) or use it in the abundance estimation (Cufflinks), while others apply read mapping signatures for filtering or for additional support (MITIE, CLASS, Script), and other links it to an empirically estimated length distribution (SLIDE).

As an additional design consideration, few in particular MITIE and CLIQ, are to jointly analyze multiple RNA-seq data sets. Lastly, models additionally incorporate solutions to modeling biological phenomena or data artifacts, including unspliced pre-mRNA and intron retention events (32), PCR amplification biases (32) or a GC content bias (33), and reads to multiple locations on the genome.

## 11.4 CONCLUSIONS

When a high-quality reference genome is available, genome-based methods that first map reads to the genome and then assemble alignments into exon-intron transcript structures are generally superior to de novo approaches, although the latter can be very accurate (13, 14). Genome-based methods allow for better resolution of repeat and paralogous sequences, as well as overlapping gene models, and offer higher sensitivity, particularly in capturing low coverage transcripts. As a drawback, they depend critically on the accuracy of read mapping, a challenging task that needs to take into account errors, polymorphisms, and splicing, and that may be adversely impacted by the quality of the genome. Conversely, de novo approaches are favored when there is no genome sequence available or when the genome is fragmented or of low quality. Specific scenarios such as the presence of trans-spliced genes, in polymorphic regions not included in the reference genome, gene fusion or rearrangement events characteristic of cancer samples are also well addressed with de novo techniques. As a drawback, de novo assembly is computationally more challenging and significantly more sensitive to sequencing errors. It is also more prone to overclustering of paralogs and producing chimeric constructs. Given the mix of strategies employed by genome and transcriptome analysis projects, both approaches are likely to be needed and will be widely used in the foreseeable future. Transcript reconstruction methods and their mathematical foundations need to continually adapt to provide more accurate solutions and to adapt to the characteristics and biases of the evolving sequencing technologies.
