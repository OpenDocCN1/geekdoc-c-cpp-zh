Once a SNP is identified, the process of determining the alleles in a sample for a given SNP is called SNP genotyping. SNPs have been thoroughly studied in different organisms because they are well distributed across the genome and automated methods have been developed to perform cost-effective and high-quality SNP genotyping across several samples (7). These properties make SNPs amenable for evolution and population genetic studies (2) to perform mapping of traits through QTL analysis or genome-wide association studies (GWAS) (4) and to perform marker-assisted selection (8). However, because SNPs only produce minor effects in the DNA Structure, and (with the exception of SNPs in regulatory or coding regions), they are not causative for phenotypic variation and their association with traits is normal due to high linkage disequilibrium (LD) with the causative variants (4). Hence, complete understanding of genomic variation requires the identification of other types of variants with larger structural footprints. These types of variants include insertions and deletions (usually summarized as indels), copy number variants (CNVs), inversions, and translocations (9). Indels can be defined as DNA sequences present only in a subset of samples. The determination of insertion or deletion status depends on choosing a particular sequence as reference if the sequence is inserted or deleted from the reference. Indels are also well distributed across the genome, but they are less frequent and not as to genotype as SNPs. Large indels can span whole genes and hence can be causative for significant phenotypic variation.
