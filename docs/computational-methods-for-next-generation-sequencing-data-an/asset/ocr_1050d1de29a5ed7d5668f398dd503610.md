# 7.3.4 The "Twilight Zone": Definitio

We conclude that both internal segment size-based and hybrid approaches can discover deletions at sufficient power only in the range of 100 bp (approximately six to seven times the standard deviation of the internal segment size distribution) and larger. Split-read readers, on the other hand, are able to discover deletions only of size up to 30 bp (approximately two times the standard deviation). We consequently suggest 2 to 6–7 times the standard deviation in terms of base pairs for the “twilight zone” of NGS deletions.

# 7.4 DISCOVERING “TWILIGHT ZONE” DELETIONS:
NEW SOLUTIONS

Since 2012, new solutions have been presented for discovering deletions of length 30–100 bp, at both sufficient power and precision (9, 10). The earliest approach that immediately addressed the discovery of mid-sized insertions and deletions was MoDil (15). While successful by principle, MoDil is too slow in practice: a single genome, sequenced at the standard coverage of 30x, needs more than 3 days on a large cluster, which is no option in a population-scale project. In contrast, CLEVER (9) needs only 6–8 hours on a single CPU. CLEVER significantly outperforms MoDil in terms of both recall and precision in discovery. We therefore focus on CLEVER and its relatives in the following. We will also mention PINEL (14), as the most favorable contemporary split-read aligner, which can make considerable contributions in the twilight zone.

# 7.4.1 CLEVER

The key insight of CLEVER (9) is that exchanging steps 1 and 2 in the workflow outlined in Section 7.3.1 leads to success when aiming at the discovery of insertions and deletions smaller than 100 bp. CLEVER reads alignments before discarding nondiscordant reads. The point is that, although concordant (=) reads make no enough statistical signal for a deletion on their own, groups of concordant reads, all of which transmit a rather weak signal for a deletion, can be “bundle up” to form a strong signal for a deletion. CLEVER aims at the discovery of such group signals.

While this sounds easy in principle, it is not in practice.
