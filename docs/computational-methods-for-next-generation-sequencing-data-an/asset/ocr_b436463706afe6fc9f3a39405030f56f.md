# MICROBIOME ANALYSIS: STATE OF THE ART AND FUTURE TRENDS

# 18.5 CORRELATIONS AND ASSOCIATION RULES

It is important to know which OTUs tend to occur together and do not. More precisely, it is interesting to identify instances when relative abundance levels for one OTU tend to coincide with high abundance levels for a second OTU. Alternatively, it is also interesting to identify when high relative abundance levels for one OTU coincide with low abundance levels for a second OTU. In addition, there could be other factors that might be considered along with OTU abundance levels. For biomedical studies, demographics of the subject from which samples were taken, or other known environmental conditions (such as acidity or aerobicity of the niche) can be included.

Finding these types of correlations may be the first step in deducing interactions between OTUs in a community. If two OTUs are positively correlated, this could suggest that they are competing for the same resources or niche in the community and could even indicate some kind of dependency between them. For example, the waste products of one be modifying the pH levels in the environment, making it more agreeable to a second OTU. Likewise, if the change in pH is toxic to another OTU, we would observe a negative correlation between them. However, there are many alternative interpretations for correlations in the abundance of OTUs. Consider a situation where three OTUs are competing for the same limited resource. If one OTU is especially efficient at utilizing this resource, its numbers would likely increase while the abundance of its competitors would decline. There should be a negative correlation between this exceptionally competitive OTU and its rivals. The abundance of the rivals, however, would tend to change concordantly, assuming they are equally fit. The presence and success of the efficient OTU would cause the number of the two less efficient OTUs to decline, resulting in a positive correlation despite the fact that they are competitors for the same limited resource. Although it is not straightforward to understand the reason for the existence of a correlation, establishing the connections can help build a picture of what is going on in a community. We will explore various ways of visualizing a network of correlations shortly.

Of greater interest than just pairs of OTUs is to determine sets of OTUs that tend to co-occur, and whether the presence of one OTU is predictive of the presence of another a subset of the subjects. For this, we can turn to statistical data mining tools for generating association rules, such as the Apriori algorithm for basket data (52). This works by identifying specific sets (combinations) of bacterial taxa or other items that are frequently observed together in a large number of subjects, and generating a rule for each item observed with the other items in the set. The validity of the rule can be confirmed for the given data by computing quantities such as support, confidence, and lift (53). Apriori can be used to essentially cluster OTUs by how often they occur and suggest which OTUs we would be seeing based on guilt-by-association. Although association rules are of interest, Apriori can also be used as a classifier to predict disease states. If certain item sets are frequently found in a group of diseased individuals but are absent from otherwise healthy individuals, the item sets can act as markers for the disease.
