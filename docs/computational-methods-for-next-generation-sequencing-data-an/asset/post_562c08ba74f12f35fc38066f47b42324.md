## 11.2.1 Preprocessing of Reads

Because even small errors can significantly impact the quality of the assembled contigs, read sequences are evaluated prior to assembly and filtered to remove low-quality sequences and likely artifacts. Vector and adapter sequences and also polyadenylation stretches at the ends of the reads, which could cluster sequences from unrelated genes, can be detected and removed using a tool such as cutadapt (16). Sequencing errors often impact the last few positions in a read, creating sequence differences that prevent assembly; they can be removed by clipping the last few bases of reads. Furthermore, *de novo* assemblers use the topology of the De Bruijn graph to detect and correct sequencing errors occur randomly. Lastly, error correction methods have been devised and are highly successful in correcting DNA reads prefix to assembly (17), improving the quality of the assembled sequence. These methods detect identifying sequence words that have a smaller than expected number of occurrences in the data set. However, few of these methods have been adapted to RNA-seq data, an area of active development.

## 11.2.2 The De Bruijn Graph for RNA-seq Assembly

### 11.2.2.1 The De Bruijn Graph in Sequence Assembly

The De Bruijn graph was introduced to bioinformatics by the work of Iury and Waterman (18) and Pevner et al. (19), who applied it to the problem of assembling genes. This data structure is based on short sequence segments of a fixed length $k$ (typically $k \ge 19$), called $k$-mers. Given a sequence or set of input sequences, the corresponding De Bruijn graph has a node for each distinct $k$-mer that occurs in the input, and an edge between two nodes if the two $k$-mers are adjacent in an input sequence (Figure 11.2). Consequently, for each edge, the $(k - 1)$ length suffix of the originating node ($k$) coincides with the $(k - 1)$ prefix of the target node. Usually, the nodes are labeled with the $k$-mer occurrence count.

## 11.2.2 Adaptation to Transcriptome Assembly

While transcriptome assembly using Bruijn graphs is a natural extension of genome assembly, there are significant differences between the two problems. First, instead of the graph encoding just one sequence, several isoforms share the same path in the graph while the alternatively spliced regions. Second, genes and their isoforms have varying expression, and different sets of parameters may be optimal for each assembly.

In Bruijn graphs, the k-mer size significantly affects the balance between sensitivity and accuracy. Lower k values increase sensitivity by allowing low expression isoforms to be asserted, but can introduce artifacts. Larger k values are more accurate in reconstructing transcripts with high read depths, but may miss expression isoforms. Starting from the assumption that a single k value is unlikely to render a universally effective solution, several programs were developed using multiple k values in parallel to produce independent assemblies. The millions of contigs are then merged across assemblies and filtered to remove redundancies and spurious constructs.
