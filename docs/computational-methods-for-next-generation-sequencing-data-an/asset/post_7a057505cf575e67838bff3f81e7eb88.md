## 14.2 RNA-Seq Protocol

RNA-Seq is an increasingly popular approach to transcriptome profiling that uses the capabilities of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies and provides better measurement of levels of transcripts and their isoforms. RNA-Seq experiments are reproducibility. This is a central problem in bioinformatics. It is not easy to benchmark the RNA-seq process (9), and the fact that there are fundamentally different ways of analyzing the data (assembly, feature counting, etc.) make it more difficult. Nevertheless, RNA-Seq offers huge advantages over microarrays since there is no limit on the numbers of genes surveyed, no to select which genes to target, and no requirements for probes or primers, and is it the tool of metagenomics studies. Also, RNA-seq has the ability to quantify a large range of expression levels, which leads to transcriptomics and metranscriptomics.

Rapid advances in NGS have enabled sequencing of total DNA and RNA extracted from complex microbial communities, ushering the new fields of metagenomics and metranscriptomics. Depending on surrounding conditions, for example, food availability, stress, or physical parameters, the gene expression of organisms can vary widely. The aim of genomics is to capture the gene activity. Transcriptomics helps perform gene profiling to unveil functional genes. It can tell which metabolic pathways are active under the respective conditions and how the organisms interact with the environment. Hence, it can be applied for monitoring and for the identification of key genes. Transcriptomics also play a role in clinical diagnosis and in screening for drug targets or for genes, enzymes, and metabolites relevant for biotechnology (14–16).

Although transcriptomics deals with the gene expression of single species, metagenomics covers the gene activity profile of the whole microbial community. Metranscriptomics studies the function and structure of complex microbial communities as it adapts to environments such as sea and seawater. Unfortunately, as in all “meta” approaches, only a small percentage of the vast number of ecologically important genes has been correctly annotated (17).

Here, we use RNA-Seq protocol and transcriptome quantification to estimate gene expression and differential gene expression analysis.

RNA-Seq, or deep sequencing of RNAs, is a cost-efficient high-coverage technology for transcriptome analysis. There are various tools and algorithms for RNA-Seq data analysis developed to different computational challenges, among them are transcriptome quantification and reconstruction. We focus on the problem of transcriptome quantification, that is, on the estimation of the expression level of each transcript.

Recent review of computational methods for transcriptome quantification from RNA-Seq data reports several problems with the current state of transcriptome quantification, among them a significant variation in distributions of expression levels throughout transcriptome reconstruction and quantification tools (18). Transcriptome quantification from RNA-Seq data highly depends on read depth. Due to the sparse read support at some loci, many tools fail to report all/most of the exons or exon-intron junctions.

## 14.2 OVERVIEW OF THE STATE-OF-THE-ART METHODS

### 14.2.1 Quantificato Methods

From optimization point of view, the variety of approaches to transcriptome quantification is very wide. The most popular approach is likelihood through different variants of expectation maximization (EM) (20–22), integer linear program (LP)-based methods (23), (24), min-cost flow (25), and regression (26).

RNA-Seq by Expectation Maximization (RSEM) is an expectation maximization (EM) algorithm that works on the isoform level. The initial version of RSEM only handled single-ended reads; however, the latest version (21) has been extended to support paired-end reads, variable-length reads, and incorporates fragment length distribution and quality scores in its modeling. In addition to the maximum likelihood estimates of isoform expressions, RSEM also calculates 95% confidence intervals and posterior mean estimates. RSEM is the best algorithm presented so far, so we compare our SimReg to RSEM in Evaluation section.

The main limitation of statistically sound EM is that it does not include uniformity of transcript coverage, that is, not clear how to make a solution with more uniform coverage of transcripts be preferred to the one coverage is volatile. LP and integer LP-based methods overcome this limitation but cannot handle many isoforms simultaneously.

More recently, the authors of (22) proposed a quasi-multinomial model with a single parameter to capture positional, sequencing, and mapping biases. Tomescu et al. (27) proposed a method based on network flows for a multiassembly problem from transcript identification and quantification with RNA-Seq. This approach is good at keeping overall uniformity coverage but is not suitable for likelihood maximization.

Regression-based approaches are the most related to the proposed method. The most representative of these are IsoLasso approach (26). IsoLasso mathematically models gene partitions into segments (a segment is a consecutive exon while a subexon is a nonsplic region).

IsoLasso also assumes reads are uniformly sampled from transcripts. The Poisson distribution (28) then then used to approximate the binomial distribution for the number of reads falling into each segment subexon. The following quadratic program (26) is well known as LASSO approach:

```
minimize: \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_i - \sum_{j=1}^{N} a_{ji}x_j^2
subject to: $x_j \ge 0, 1 \le j \le N, \sum_{j=1}^{N} x_j \lambda \cdot t = 1 |T|$
```

and two more "completeness" constraints (namely that each segment or junction with maps is covered by at least one isoform; and the sum of expression levels of all isoforms that contain this segment must be strictly positive)) were added to this program in IsoLasso. Many oversimplification is an assumption that each segment receives from transcripts the number of reads proportional to its length. For example, it is not clear how to handle very short subexons and take into account position of subexon. Fragment length distribution also discriminate one subexon from another. Especially difficult to accurately estimate portions of pair-end reads emitted from each subexon in fact such reads are frequently emitted by multiple subexon collectively. Furthermore, mapping the reads into transcripts is frequently ambiguous, which is consciously ignored in Reference 26.

In this chapter, we propose to apply a more accurate싼 of read emission. Our novel algorithm falls into the category of regression-based methods: namely, SimReg is a Monte Carlo based method.

In general, one of the main goals of differential expression (DE) analysis is to identify the differentially expressed genes between two or more conditions. Such genes are selected by combination of expression level threshold and expression score cutoff, which is usually based on p-values generated by statistical modeling. The expression level of RNA unit is measured by the number of sequenced fragments that map to the transcript, which is expected to correlate with its abundance level. The outcome of DE analysis is influenced by the primary analysis (mapping, mapping parameters, counting) is counted (30). In addition, the overall library preparation protocol and quality is also an important factor of bias (31–33). As described in the following chapters, DE analysis methods differ in how to deal with these pre-analysis phases. Furthermore, RNA Seq experiments tend to be underpowered (too few replicates) and we need methods to perform DE under these circumstances.
