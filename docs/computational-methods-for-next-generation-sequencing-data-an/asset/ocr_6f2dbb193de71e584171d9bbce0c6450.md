The sequence along one chromosome is called a haplotype and is defined as an element of $[A, C, G, T]^L$, with $L$ being the length of the chromosome. A genotype is the matching of two haplotypes for an individual. As many of the positions are conserved, a conserved haplotype can be reduced to the variant sites. Sequencing errors can be easily corrected, as variants are bi-allelic, a variant occurs with a 50% frequency. Low-frequency variants are identified as sequencing errors and corrected with the dominant nucleotide. Variant sites are bi-allelic, they can only take one of two possible values, and a haplotype can be represented as an element of $[0, 1]^{L'}$, with $L'$ being the number of heterogeneous positions. Current technologies are not able to sequence chromosomes individually and only the genotype, the combined information of a chromosome, is available. In the example of Figure 16.8, the goal is to reconstruct the individual haplotypes from a sequence of binary pairs. The haplotype structure is important, as some genetic diseases are associated with linkage of specific mutant alleles on one haplotype, for example, Crohn’s disease (46).
