# 4.4 PERFORMANCE OF VGA ON SIMULATED DATA

Since the ground truth is unknown for sequenced viral populations, in Reference 5 we used simulations as a standardized way to assess the performance of viral assembly tools. The high-fidelity protocol allows to correct sequencing errors, giving access to highly sequencing data. Post-sequencing error correction techniques are available for reads obtained by regular protocol offering the possibility to partially correct sequencing errors trading off for real biological mutations. Grinder (29) is used to generate reads from both the high fidelity and regular sequencing protocol. Reads are generated from both real and synthetic viral variants with different sequencing parameters and viral expression profiles. Grinder is a state-of-the-art sequencing read simulator to avoid shotgun sequencing data from a viral population. We mapped the simulated paired-end reads to the consensus using Mosaik (30). The consensus was constructed using Vicuna (a de novo assembly assembly tool to produce a linear consensus from deep paired-end sequencing data without errors. See Section 4.3 for details).

In Reference 5, we use sensitivity and positive value (PPV) to evaluate the quality of viral genomes assembled by VGA. We consider fully assembled viral quasispecies that match true quasispecies, is, *Sensitivity = TP/(TP + FN)*, that is, PPV is defined as the proportion of true sequences among assembled quasispecies, that is, PPV = TP/(TP + FP). Additionally, we evaluate the ability of our method to estimate population size (i.e., number of viral genomes in the population). Accuracy of population size prediction is defined as a ratio between estimated and true population sizes. Finally, we use Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) to measure the “distance” between them, or in words, the quality of approximation of one probability distribution by the other. It is defined as the Kullback-Leibler divergence from distributions $P$ and $Q$ to their mixture. Formally, the JSD between true distribution $P$ and approximation distribution $Q$ is given by the formula

```
$JSD(P||Q) = \frac{1}{2}D_{KL}(P||M) + \frac{1}{2}D_{KL}(Q||M)$
```

where Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{KL}$ is

```
$D_{KL}(P||Q) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(i) \log \frac{P(i)}{Q(i)}$
```

and $M=\frac{1}{2}(P+Q)$. The motivation for JSD is a consequence of KL divergence being undefined when assembly methods fail to reconstruct some variant $i$ hence forcing $Q(i)$ to be 0. JSD agrees this by measuring the distance to the mixture, which contains all true and called variants (TP and FP).

Our first studied compares the assembly accuracy across different species. In Reference 5, we focus on the effect of read length and throughput on assembly quality for different types of viruses. Paired-reads of various length corresponding to high fidelity and regular sequencing protocols are simulated from HIV and HCV populations assuming uniform and power-law distributions. A power law distribution (i.e., frequency of an individual viral genome is a power of the previous) corresponds to population with several dominant variants and many rare variants. The uniform distribution has equal frequencies for all viral genomes. HCV population is presented by 1739-bp long fragment from the E1E2 genome of 44 real HCV sequences. HIV population consists of a mixture of 10 real intraviral variants from 1.3 kb-long HIV-I region, which included pol and part of the pol reverse transcriptase

The genomic architecture across virus species was investigated and its influence on assembly quality was studied. HCV exhibits more complex genomic architecture with lower population density and conserved regions (Figure 4.3) than HIV. Conserved regions were present in both viruses although HCV contains conserved regions longer than 450bp. Conserved regions longer than the average fragment length (450 bp) may introduce ambiguity due to a lack of direct evidence of subgenomes passing the conserved region. We performed sequencing experiment where the average amplification rate is 5, resulting in a five-time decrease in throughput due to the consensus error correction performed by the high-fidelity sequencing protocol. Also the simulation experiments were used to simulate a nonuniform amplification rate. Nonuniform amplification rate resulted in discarding fragments with insufficient amplification rate (less than 3). From real studies, it is known that around 10% of fragments are amplified the three times. Sequencing errors produced by the regular protocol limited the ability of VGA to accurately assemble a viral population. All assembled variants contained a large number of mismatches, in addition VGA significantly overestimated population size.

As expected, short read lengths dramatically inhibit reconstruction, which is evident by VGA failing to produce full-length genomes when given 22x36 bp reads (Figure 4.6). Since complex regions for HCV viral genomes are significantly longer than for HIV, it is not surprising that performance of VGA is worse on HCV data - 3M x 150 bp reads simulated from 44 1739-bp long genomes, sensitivity is 50%, and PPV is 80%. Results on HCV confirm that the lower mutation rate and the presence of conserved regions have a negative impact on the ability to reconstruct individual viral genomes. Surprisingly, increasing the read length for HIV from 100 bp to 150 bp yields no benefits for reconstruction accuracy, suggesting that 100 bp read length is enough to distinguish HIV viral variants with high mutation rate. Although further experiments are needed to determine optimum read length, our simulations suggest that 2 x 100 bp is recommended

In Reference 5, we separately analyzed the ability of our method to estimate the viral population size (i.e., number of genomic variants present in the population). Noncontinuous coverage limits the ability of the method to assemble full-length viral variants. To evaluate the accuracy of population size estimation, we compared the true population size known from simulated data with estimated results. Continuous coverage of each individual viral genome in the sample has a strong impact on the quality of population assembly. The probability of noncontinuous coverage increases dramatically for viral genomes with low abundance. Thus, the presence of coverage gaps for rare variants introduce additional challenges in the assembly process, making rare genomes unreachable by assembly tools. The number of partial genomes can be reduced by increasing sequencing depth; however, it does not guarantee completion. While complete assembly of all such genomes is unrealistic, it is still possible to estimate the number of viral genomes in the sample (population size).
