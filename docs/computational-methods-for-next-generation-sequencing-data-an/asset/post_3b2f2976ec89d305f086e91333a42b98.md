## 3.5.2 Results

The algorithmic approach described in Section 3.3 was used to reconstruct samples from experimental data described in Section 3.5.1. Before applying algorithms for samples recovery, the data were preprocessed in order to get rid of sequencing errors and PCR chimeras.

The reads from each pool were separated into clusters using Algorithm IGEM, each cluster was processed using NGS error correction algorithms KEC and ET (41) and the corrected reads were merged. Then the sequences were deconstructed using Algorithm IS. The obtained samples will be referred as *pooling samples*

For the verification of pooled samples, we compared them with the individually sequenced samples. The sequences were compared pairwise alignment; insertions and deletions were ignored (since indels are rare in HVR1 and, therefore, rather sequencing artifacts; moreover, some indel in sequences from individually sequenced and pooling samples may be introduced due to the inaccurate correction of homopolymer errors for the samples). For each sample, 10 reference sequences were taken from the set of individually sequenced-variants, and the correctness of sample reconstruction was assessed using alignment of sequences in the pooled samples with these. For alignment, Muscle was used.

In average, 259 unique haplotypes per sample from a pool were obtained (from 23 haplotypes in Sample $S_3$ to 548 haplotypes in Sample $S_4$), which exceeds the number of HCV haplotypes in other studies (43–45) after the standard sequencing of 99.634% of all analyzed sequences were correctly classified into samples. ## 3.6 CONCLUSION

In this study, we present a novel framework for massive NGS of highly mutable RNA viruses, such as HCV and HIV. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of the pooling strategy to highly heterogeneous viral samples. The developed framework takes into account specific aspects of viral sequencing, such as the extensive heterogeneity of viral samples, the large number of distinct viral variants sequenced from each sample, and the effects of PCR and sampling biases.

# 80 COMPUTATIONAL METHODS FOR NGS DATA ANALYSIS

The proposed strategy is highly effective in reducing the number of sequencing runs, while still providing sufficient amount of information in support of molecular surveillance and numerous applications of viral sequences in clinical and epidemiological settings. The novel clustering algorithm developed here significantly facilitates assignment of intracellular viral variants from massive sequence data to individual patients. The strategy of overlapping pools drastically reduces the cost of sequencing per specimen, especially when a large number of specimens requires to be tested. This computational framework is applicable to viral agents affecting humans and animals, and with further development of the experimental protocols, it should serve as a cost-effective foundation for accurate molecular surveillance of infectious diseases.

Ulradepencing of viral samples produces a wide range of intracellular variants and allows for detecting minority variants, some of which have shown to have clinical implications such as drug resistance (9, 30, 31). Pooling of numerous specimens reduces the depth of sequencing for each specimen. However, this reduction is not detrimental for identifying minority variants since each specimen is usually used in more than one pool in the strategy developed here. As sequencing is tested more than once, the number of sequenced variants is increased, so representative sampling of subpopulations can be improved. The experiments conducted here showed that comparable number of haplotypes were recovered from individual specimens and pools (Figure 3.9), at least at the pooling scale used in this study. Both individual and pooling produce sequences covering approximately the same areas of the sequence space, providing a consistent structure of a viral population.

The cost and accuracy of pool deconvolution are two major measures of quality of our computational framework. However, these two measures are in conflict with each other. While increase in pool size improves cost-effectiveness of sequencing by reducing the number of sequencing runs, it reduces accuracy of deconvolution. Considering that accuracy of deconvolution significantly depends on the genetic complexity of intracellular viral populations, an optimal pool size should be carefully selected for each virus and each genomic region.
