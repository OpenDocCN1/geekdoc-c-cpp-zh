## 15.1 NEXT-GENERATION SEQUENCING OF HETEROGENEOUS
VIRAL POPULATIONS AND SEQUENCING ERRORS

Highly mutable RNA viruses, as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV), are major causes of morbidity and mortality in the world. HCV infects approximately 130–170 million people of the world and is a major cause of liver diseases worldwide (1), while approximately 33 million people are currently infected with HIV (2). RNA-dependent polymerases of RNA viruses are error prone, which results in a high mutation rate (3). Owing to the accumulation of mutations, RNA viruses exist in infected hosts as highly heterogeneous populations of genetically close variants known as quasispecies (4, 5).

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies allow the analysis of an unprecedented number of viral sequences carried in samples of infected individuals, presenting a new opportunity for studying of viral populations and understanding the pathology epidemiology, evolution, drug resistance and immune escape. Currently, many of NGS platforms are available, including 454 Junior and 454 GS-FLX Titanium (Roche), Ion PGM and Ion Proton (Life Technologies), MiSeq and HiSeq (Illumina), PacBio RS (Pacific Biosystems), and SOLID (Life Technologies).

However, the quality of data has a detrimental effect on quality. NGS is prone, with error rates for different technologies being estimated in a number of studies. The estimated values are often different, and some of them are summarized in Table 15.1.

The types and nature of sequencing errors vary for different NGS platforms. For example, pyrosequencing in the 454 platform introduces more than one nucleotide at a time in the nascent DNA strand on homopolymer regions. The number of the incorporated nucleotides is estimated using a signal calibration (6, 14). However, the signal strength may be misinterpreted, causing underestimation or overestimation of the number of incorporated nucleotides. Thus, sequence from the 454 platform frequently have insertions and deletions in homopolymer regions, also used in the Ion Torrent platform, which is based on the detection of an electric ion released during nucleotide incorporation. But the error profile of the Ion Torrent platform is similar to the 454 platform. Unlike the 454 and Ion Torrent platforms, the Illumina technology extends DNA molecules by adding only a single nucleotide at each iteration (6, 14), which results in a high prevalence of nucleotide replacements than deletions or insertions.

Nevertheless, none of the NGS platforms produces only one type of errors. Error profiles of NGS platforms are fairly heterogeneous. For example, Figure 15.1 illustrates error profiles of reads obtained from 14 HCV Hypervariable 1 (HVR1) samples, each containing a single plasmid clone, which were sequenced using 454/Roche GS FLX platform (15).

Most of the early NGS error correction algorithms assumed sequencing errors are distributed randomly, which led to the conclusion that erroneous reads have low frequencies, and therefore, can be easily filtered using an appropriate frequency cutoff. The-cutoff filtering is still the most widely used approach for NGS error correction. However, subsequent studies have shown that the assumption of the random error distribution is not always correct. In particular, error rate is strongly affected by position in the sequence (with probability of error increasing at the read's end), length of a homopolymer or sequence read, and the spatial localization in PT plates (8, 15, 16). As a result, erroneous reads can have frequencies that are only comparable to such haplotypes (Figure 15.2), which significantly impacts the use of cutoffs.

Differences in error rates and types among NGS technologies, all studies agreeing that sequencing errors pose a serious problem, becomes especially challenging for heterogeneous samples. The need for identification and preservation of low-frequency natural variants in NGS reads makes the problem of detection and correction of NGS reads for viral samples especially challenging, because methods must distinguish between real and artificial genetic heterogeneity. By an example, consider a distance graph of reads from the NGS data set (15) (Figure 15.3). Here, only the most frequent haplotype (shown in red) corresponds to the real variant, while all other vertices representing artifacts. It is important to note that a similar pattern of heterogeneity from a single source after several rounds of replication is also expected in a real population originating after several rounds of replication.
