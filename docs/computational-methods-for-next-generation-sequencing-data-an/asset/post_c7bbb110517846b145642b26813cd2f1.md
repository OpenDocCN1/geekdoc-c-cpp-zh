## 7.3.1 Internal Segment Size-Based Approaches

The basic idea that underlies internal segment size-based approaches is that the alignment length $I(A)$ deviates from $I(R)$, the length of the internal segment of the read gave to $A$. That is, $I(A) = I(R) + L(D)$ for a deletion $D$ in the internal segment of $R$, see Figure 7.2 for an illustration. While $I(A)$ is known, $I(R)$ is not, however. Therefore, one estimates mean and standard deviation of the empirical distribution of $I(R)$ from uniquely mappable fragments using robust estimators (18, 19). For modern DNA sequencing protocols, the fragment length distributions are approximately Gaussian with low standard deviations. We note already here that well-shaped (Gaussian) distribution is essential for discovering mid-sized deletions.

See Figure 7.3 for such a distribution, derived from the NGS reads of one of the individuals of the GoNL project (1), all of which were sequenced by BGI in 2010.

## COMPUTATIONAL METHODS FOR NGS DATA ANALYSIS

After the determination of the internal segment size distribution, the following generic workflow for discovering deletions in NGS data is widely used:

- 1. Collect all reads whose alignments statistically deviate in terms of alignment interval length, the so-called discordant reads.
- 2. Cluster all such reads into groups that support the deletion.
- 3. Make predictions from the resulting groups of discordant reads.

The majority of approaches follows this workflow (e.g., References 7, 8, 12, 13, 20, 21). They can be distinguished by their definition of discordant read, clustering/grouping techniques, and their details in deriving predictions from groups of discordant reads. Note that handling reads that become mutant-mapped to repetitive sequence often plays a major role (22), see, for example, Reference 8 for a combinatorially principled approach.

The key factor is the definition of a discordant read, as those are supposed to represent fragments whose internal segment is affected by the deleted sequence. Again, the idea is that $l(R)$, the alignment interval length of a discordant read, significantly deviates from the distribution of $l(R)$. Thus, the definition of a discordant read depends on the standard deviation of the distribution of $l(R)$, which in current protocols amounts to about 15. Up to six times this standard deviation is required to obtain sufficiently low discovery rates. This translates into the fact that deletions shorter than 100 bp remain undiscoverable.

## 7.3.2 Split-Read Mapping Approaches

Split-read mapping approaches aim at making direct use of alignment information. As per usual workflow, a split-read mapper processes only that standard read alignments fail to align correctly. This is most often due to insertions and deletions that affect the read. Standard read aligners usually face difficulties in aligning such reads properly because placing larger gaps can be computationally (too) expensive.

Therefore, aligning reads by length induces extended techniques: "split-read alignments." A generic workflow common to many split-read aligners (e.g., References 14, 23–25) looks as follows:

- 1. Collect all reads where one remained unaligned and/or the other end became only partially aligned ("soft-clipped") by the standard aligner in use.
- 2. In case of entirely unaligned reads, split the end in parts, or "seeds," and try to align those parts, or "seeds" (see Figure 7.4 for a resulting alignment).
- 3. For such aligned reads and/or soft-clipped reads, try to align the remaining part(s) of the read somewhere "nearby."
- 4. For each read, collect all possible partial alignments and compute "split alignments," using banded alignment techniques, to connect them. Output the most likely such split alignment(s) as the alignment(s) of the read end.

To date, common split-read aligners can successfully align reads with non-negligible amounts of deletions of length up to 40–50bp. While aligning reads larger deletions is not impossible, the discovery rates of split-read aligners significantly decrease with increasing deletion size. Thereby, that is, the rate of discovered deletions, drops below 60–70% when reaching the 30-bp mark, which renders split-read approaches unable to discover sufficient amounts of "twilight zone deletions", if they follow the workflow from above.
The bottlenecks of split-read aligners are steps 2 and 3 in the above-mentioned workflow. Or split unaligned parts of reads can be small, which can drastically increase the number of locations in the reference genome where these parts can be aligned. The fact that genomes are highly repetitive in general can significantly add to these difficulties— resolving the ambiguity among multiple mappings is another involved step.

Therefore, one has to limit the size of the regions in which one searches for split parts. Due to those limitations, although substantially raising the limits of standard aligners, split-read aligners can quickly reach their limits— every in detail can count. Note that the internal segment size distribution also plays a decisive role here, as it is used to appropriately quantify "nearby" in step 3 and as a guide when placing alignment of split parts in step 2.

## 7.3.3 Hybrid Approaches

One decisive general advantage of split-read aligning approaches over internal segment size-based approaches is the base resolution of deletion breakpoints: if both an internal segment size-based method and a split-read aligner calls a deletion, the breakpoints predicted by the split-read aligner are usually more accurate than those of the internal segment size-based approach.
As outlined earlier, split-read aligners usually cannot detect many deletions larger than 30–40 bp. The motivation of the so-called hybrid approaches is to also call breakpoints at base pair resolution. A common generic workflow is as follows:

- 1. Run an internal segment size-based approach and collect all deletion calls.
- 2. Collect all deletions nearly deletion calls collected in step 1.
- 3. Split-align all unaligned and partially aligned read ends in those regions. Thereby, the split-aligner is "guided" by the deletion calls of the internal segment size-based approach when determining the correct placements of shorter read ends parts.
- 4. Output all variant calls with breakpoints corrected (or removed, if no split-ments could be determined) as per the split alignments determined in step 3.

## 7.3.4 The "Twilight Zone": Definitio

We conclude that both internal segment size-based and hybrid approaches can discover deletions at sufficient power only in the range of 100 bp (approximately six to seven times the standard deviation) and larger. Split-read readers, on the other hand, are able to discover deletions only of size 30 bp (approximately two times the standard deviation). We consequently suggest 2 to 6–7 times the standard deviation for NGS deletions.

## 7.4 DISCOVERING “TWILIGHT ZONE” DELETIONS: NEW SOLUTIONS

Since 2012, new solutions have been presented for discovering deletions of 30–100 bp, at both sufficient power and precision (9, 10). The earliest approach that immediately addressed the discovery of mid-sized deletions was MoDil (15). While successful by principle, MoDil is too slow in practice: a single genome, sequenced at the standard coverage of 30x, needs more than 3 days on a large cluster, which is no option in a population-scale project. In contrast, CLEVER (9) needs only 6–8 hours on a single CPU. CLEVER significantly outperforms MoDil in terms of both recall and precision in discovery. We therefore focus on CLEVER and its relatives in the following. We will also mention PINEL (14), as the most favorable contemporary split-read aligner, which can make considerable contributions in the twilight zone.
