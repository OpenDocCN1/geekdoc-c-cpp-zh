As previously discussed, two approaches have been adopted for characterizing the diversity of metagenomics. The first approach focuses on the sequencing of marker genes, such as the gene for 16S RNA (10–15). The second approach is based on whole genome sequencing. While genome sequencing is superior to 16S rRNA approaches primarily because it is to directly characterize the functional profile of the community. Typically, sequences are compared to public databases and genes by homology. Taxonomic identification can be helpful, but is not essential. The major limitation here is that functional annotations are far from complete, and accurately describing functions to predicted gene sequences will take many years. Ontology databases will not catch up to the available data any time soon.

As mentioned, the most popular approaches continue to be based on 16S rRNA (10–15). The functional analysis of 16S rRNA metagenomic data can focus on what "species" are present. In cases where the roles of the bacterial species are well understood, taxonomic identity can be an indicator of the more active functions in the community. Methods also allow for imputing functional profiles based on 16S data. These involve reconstruction of the ancestral state to predict of gene families in descendants (58). Although these methods have been tested with good results, problems remain. The 16S approaches cannot resolve taxonomy below the genus level, and it is known that different species or strains in the same genus can behave differently. Furthermore, taxonomic identification is limited by the completeness of marker gene databases. The number of sequences that cannot even be classified down to the genus can be very high in some studies (M. Fernandez and G. Narasimhan. Unpublished results.). Additionally, when analyzing genome sequencing data, the problem of HGT needs to be considered. There are indications that the HGT rate can be as high as 8% in some bacterial communities (59). Although there is merit to attempts to create functional profiles based on taxonomy, these methods may be useful for identifying environments, which would strongly benefit from whole genome sequencing.

Zhang et al. (60–63) have developed a novel approach to analyze the genome data of microbe. Their strategy is to compute a matrix, where the rows correspond to features such as genes, gene families, functional groups, or even pathways, and the columns correspond to OTUs. Instead of mapping OTUs to bacterial taxa, the matrix maps them to functional entities that enable us to ascribe functional characteristics. The matrix may be seen as a collection of columns (OTUs with a functional profile) or as a collection of rows (Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COGs) with measures of their correlation to each OTU). COG was used as functional entities to create an edge-weighted network of COGs where the edge weights are the correlations between row vectors.
