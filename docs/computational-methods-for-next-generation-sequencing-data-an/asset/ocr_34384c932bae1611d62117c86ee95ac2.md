For the proper estimation of methylation status and level of cytosines, it is essential to determine the PCR strand from which each read comes from. Reads from PCR+ and PCR- indicate methylation of cytosines on the positive genomic strand, while reads generated from PCR+ and PCR- account for methylation of the cytosines on the negative genomic strand, as seen in Figure 10.1c.

The accuracy of mapping is essential for inferring methylated cytosines. Sequencing errors, adapter contamination, end-repair, and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) may affect erroneous methylation calls (15). To avoid these problems, preprocessing procedures are used that check for quality of reads and trim low-quality ones, and identify and remove the adapter sequences from the reads. Postprocessing is also necessary for accurate inference; procedures usually remove extraneous copies of the reads mapped onto the same start-base (the so-called PCR- or copy-duplicates) and prevent double counting of methylated reads from overlapped paired-end read mates.

# 10.3 ALGORITHMIC APPROACHES TO THE PROBLEM OF MAPPING BS-TREATED READS

The most time-efficient methods for mapping short reads either use hashing or data structures based on the Burrows-Wheeler transformer or BWT (16) to reduce the search space. As mentioned earlier, BS-mapping introduces additional challenges because BS-treated reads do not match exactly due to conversion of cytosines to thymines. To allow for a T in read read to match a C in the genome (hereafter called BS-mismatches), different approaches have been introduced, all of which could be summarized into two categories: (i) all combinations of conversion of Ts to Cs are considered; (ii) reference/read conversions are applied. First, we discuss how these approaches are used with hashing and then consider methods using the BWT.

In hashing and BWT, letters A, C, G, and T are converted to the corresponding representations 00, 01, 10, and respectively. For the purpose of reducing search space, it is practical to first identify genomic regions from which each read was oriented. A seed of length k of a read (also a k-mer or a q-gram) is hashed from a reference into a table: the binary representation of a seed is used as the hashing index, and the genomic position of the seed is stored in the corresponding bin. For each read, a set of its seeds is hashed into the table and the read is aligned to only those genomic positions that share with the read. To allow for BS-mismatches, some tools use all combinations of Ts to Cs and use both genomic strands; for instance Verjinker (17), BMAP (18), Pash (19), and BISS (20). This method preserves the genomic complexity, but introduces a large number of candidates affecting time efficiency. To reduce search space, some tools (e.g., Pash) use heuristics that verify the distance between the genomic positions, to which different seeds of a read have been used, and keep only those candidate positions that are within appropriate distance from each other.
