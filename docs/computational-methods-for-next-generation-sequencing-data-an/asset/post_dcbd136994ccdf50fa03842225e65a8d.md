### 1.1 INTRODUCTION

Since the automated Sanger sequencing method dominated in the 1980s (1), considered as the first-generation sequencing technology, researchers first had the opportunity to steadily build an effective ecosystem for the production and consumption of genomic information. A large number of computational tools have been developed to decode the biological information from the sequence databases in the ecosystem. Due to the expensive cost of using the first-generation sequencing technology, only a few bacteria, organisms possess relatively small and simple genomes, were sequenced to publish. However, along with the completion of the Human Genome Project in the beginning of the 21st century, studies on large-scale genomic analysis became feasible despite an unprecedented proliferation of genomic sequence data, which was unimaginable only a few years ago. The advent of newer methods of sequencing, known as next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies (2), threatens the conventional genomics informatics ecosystem in terms of the storage space, as well as the efficiencies of transitional tools when analyzing such huge amounts of data. ### 1.2 CHALLENGES FOR NGS DATA ANALYSIS

Since the 1980s, the genomic ecosystem (Figure 1.1 (3)) for production and consumption of genomic information consists of sequencing lab, archives, power users, and casual users. The sequencing labs submitted to their data archival databases, such as GenBank of National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (4), European Bioinformatics Institute EMBL, database (5), and Sequence Read Archive (SRA), previously known as Short Read Archive (6). Most of these databases maintain, organize, sequencing data, and also provide data access and tools to both power users and casual users freely. Most users obtain information either via websites created by archival databases or by value-added integrators.

The basis for the ecosystem is Moore’s law (7), which describes a long-term trend first introduced in 1965 by Intel co-founder Gordon Moore. Moore’s law stated that “number of transistors that can be placed on an integrated circuit is increasing exponentially, with a rate of doubling in 18 months.” The trend has remained true for approximately 40 years across multiple changes in manufacturing. Similar phenomena have been noted for disk storage: hard drive capacity doubles annually (Kelly’s law) (9); and network capacity that the cost of sending a bit of information over optical networks halves every 9 months (Nielsen’s law and Butter’s law) (10). In terms of the prices of hard disk DNA sequencing, we illustrate this using a long-term trend (Figure 1.2). Note that exponential curves are drawn as straight lines in the logarithmic scale. According to the figure, it is clear that the cost of storing a byte was halved every 14 months during 1990–2010. On the contrary, the cost of sequencing a base was halved every 19 months during 1990–2004, which is more slow than the cost of storage did. With the widespread use of NGS technologies, the cost of sequencing a base was halved every 5 months, which leads to the drop in the cost of genome sequencing times faster than the cost. It is not difficult to predict that it will cost us to sequence a base of DNA than to store it on a hard disk sometime shortly. There is no guarantee to accelerate the trends all the time, but recently announced results by Illumina (15), Pacific Biosystems (16), Helicos (17), and Ion Torrent (18) ensure the continuing of the trends for least another half-century.
