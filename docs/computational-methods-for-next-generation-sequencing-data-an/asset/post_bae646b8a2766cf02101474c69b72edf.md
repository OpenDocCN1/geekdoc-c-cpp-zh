### 2.1 INTRODUCTION & OVERVIEW

The rise of next-generation sequencing technologies has created a veritable tsunami of environmental sequence information sourced from a variety of natural and engineered ecosystems. While this tidal wave of information is changing our understanding of microbial community structure and function in the world around us, a general lack of versatile and scalable software tools to process, organize, and interact with environmental data sets limits knowledge creation and translation. In this chapter, we describe the capabilities of MetaPathways, a modular pipeline designed for metabolic pathway reconstruction and comparative analysis of environmental sequence information.

### 2.2 BACKGROUND

We live in a world dominated by microorganisms (1). Interconnected members of this invisible microbial majority form distributed metabolic networks linking genomic and phenotypic expression from single cells to earth systems (2, 3). Such networks are continuously changing through mutation, gene transfer, and habitat selection creating redundant and resilient microbial modules driving matter and energy conversion in natural and engineered ecosystems (4–8). Plurality sequencing, also known as environmental genomics, ecological genomics, or metagenomics, generates vast amounts of environmental sequence information with the potential to transform our worldview (4, 9–11) and enable technological and therapeutic innovations through perception and manipulation of these networks (12–18).

Although progeny and single-cell sequencing technologies are rapidly expanding our capacity to generate environmental sequence information (19–22), there are a number of computational and analytical challenges that limit knowledge creation and translation (23–25). Collins and colleagues agree that similar problems are common to many areas of modern experimental research: “Data comes in all scales and shapes, covering large international experiments; cross-laboratory, single-laboratory, and individual observations; and potentially individuals lives. The discipline and scale of individual experiments and their data rates make the use of tools a formidable problem!” (26). In particular, computational and visual analytical bottlenecks present powerful limits to integration across different levels of biological information (DNA, RNA, protein, and scalable comparisons between replicate samples or communities. While good progress has been made in developing tools to integrate, user-friendly softwares that integrate novel statistical and distributed computing techniques and effective structures across multiple hierarchical levels are needed to fully realize the power and promote of environmental sequence information.

Several services integrate environmental sequence analysis and information storage. The Metagenome Rapid Annotation using Subsystem Technology (MG-RAST http://metagenomics.mpdr.org/) is a web-based portal using the SEED annotation framework for functional genes and subsystems that provides annotation and comparative analysis services (27). The Integrated Genomes and Metagenomes (IMG/M, http://img.jie.doe.gov/m/) (28, 29) and Community Infrastructure for Advanced Microbial Ecology Research and Analysis (CAMERA http://camera.caliti2.net/) (30) archive data sets provide annotation and comparative analysis services for selected sequencing projects. Other information resources such as the Genomes OnLine Database (GOLD) (31) and Marine Ecological Genomics (MEG) (32) curate and compile information about microbial genome and metagenome processes worldwide.

In addition to online information resources, a number of specific software tools have been developed to support microbial community structure and function studies. For example, the Metagenome Analyzer (MEGAN) provides a stand-alone tool that parses and queries the Basic Sequence Alignment Tool (BLAST) searches onto various taxonomic or functional gene hierarchies (33–35). Using tools such as Qiime, MLTreeMap (http://mlltreemap.org), and the Interactive Tree-of-Life (iTOL) focus on taxonomic identification through marker gene. Qime uses phylogenetic dense of 16S rRNA genes with multivariate statistical methods to infer relationship among and between sets (36). MLTreeMap uses selected-copy clusters of orthogonal groups (COGS) to discern the taxonomic composition of environmental data sets (37). iTOL enables the interactive display of phylogenetic relationships constructed from user-defined hierarchies (38). While these services and tools enable to compare environmental sequence information based on gene abundance, they also come with idiosyncratic bandwidth and formatting restrictions that can limit downstream analysis and data production.

In the following sections, we present Metapathways (39, 40), a modular annotation and analysis pipeline, that enables construction of environmental pathway/genome databases (PGDBs) from environmental sequence information using Pathways Tools (41, 42) and MetaCyc (43, 44). Metapathways provides a pathway-centric view of microbial community metabolism that overcomes several data processing and integration bottlenecks. We begin with an overview of pipeline processing stages, to introduce a refined task management system for high-performance computing on grids and clouds, a graphical user interface (GUI) and “Knowledge Engine” data enabling comparative analysis of embarrassingly large data sets, and outline data production using selected R packages like VennDiagram (45), pclustveg (46), and ggplot2 (http://ggplot2.org/) (47).

### 2.3 METAPATHWAYS PROCESSES

In this section, we review pipeline processes with special emphasis on design principles and algorithm selection. Metapathways can be compartmentalized into five operational stages encompassing 16 processing steps whose may vary incrementally between pipeline versions (Version 2.1). From initial quality control (QC) to remove poor quality sequences, Metapathways implements open reading frame (ORF) prediction. Translated ORFs are functionally annotated against protein databases. Following functional annotation, transfer RNA (tRNA) and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene are identified. The taxonomic affiliation of predicted ORFs is based on NCBI. The lowest common ancestor (LCA) is placed onto taxonomy hierarchy, and an automated tree building is used to profile community based on the recovery of selected genes. Annotated ORFs are refined and formatted for pathway prediction using Pathway Tools to generate ePGDBs. Finally, gene, reaction, and pathway information are extracted for downstream analysis.

A number of algorithms have been developed that accurately predict full-length genes in assembled sequences including GeneMark (49), Glimmer (52), and fgenes (http://linux.softberry.com/). These performance declines on unampled environmental data containing less than 100 bp in length (average prokaryotic gene length) (53) (Figure 2.2b). Moreover, while accuracy can be increased through training that processes tasks among closely related taxa, the genomic complexity of environmental sequence information presents an “anonymous sequence problem” (54). Given these constraints, short-read gene prediction algorithms need to sort incoming sequences into taxonomic bins or successively modify internal parameters for each sequence. Such algorithms also need to be robust to sequencing errors associated with short-read platforms.

A machine learning and statistical models have been developed to address the anonymous sequence problem (Figure 2.3).
