Bsmooth has applied a signal-to-noise statistic (SNS) to identify DMRs between groups of samples and take into account biological variation. For a window, the SNS is defined as $(\mu_T - \mu_C)/(\sigma_T + \sigma_C)$ where $\mu_T$ and $\sigma_T$ ($u_C$ and $\sigma_C$) represent the mean and standard deviation, respectively, of the treatment (control groups). Furthermore, the denominator was approximated by $\sigma 	imes \sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$ where $n_1$ and $n_2$ represent the number of samples in treatment and control groups, respectively. $\sigma$ is a location-dependent standard deviation to represent biological variation across samples in a window and was estimated by smoothing using a running mean with a window size of 101 the standard deviation, was computed empirically across groups and floored at their 75 percent. DMRs are determined if their SNS is greater than $c$ or less than $-c$ where $c$ is a cutoff based on the marginal empirical distribution. It was recommended to use only CpGs with some coverage in all samples to detect DMRs with three more CpGs, average methylation difference = 0.1, and at least one CpG every 300 bp.

The above-described algorithm was modified for cancer data because of its heterogeneity, cancer cells are highly variable while normal cells have similar methylation. The algorithm was also modified for detecting large hypomethylated regions in cancer as reported by Hansen et al. (36). To these ends, the window size of a DMR was increased to at least 50 kbp wide, and the cutoff for SNS was decreased. These modifications led to including more CpGs into DMRs and detecting large-scale changes by smoothing large, wider windows.
