CLEVER clusters all read alignments. To achieve low enough run-times in practice, CLEVER makes use of a highly engineered implementation of a max-clique technique as underlying clustering algorithm.

CLEVER solves this problem by collecting all read alignments into a read alignment graph, where each node represents a read alignment and edges indicate that overlapping alignments are likely to reflect identical alleles. Maximal cliques represent maximal groups of read alignments of all which reflect the same allele (the right panel of Figure 1 Reference 9). If there are indel alleles in the donor genome, the max-cliques reflecting such deviate from the internal size statistics. If sufficiently many reads participate in a max-clique, they give rise to significant signals when reflecting only relatively small indels, as revealed by common multiple-sample Z-tests. The statistical model of CLEVER further allows to address that read alignments can be ambiguous due to repetitive sequence and corrects for multiple testing, thereby keeping control of the false discovery rate.

Key to success in enumerating all max-cliques is a bitwector implementation of a max-clique enumeration algorithm that exploits the particular structure of read alignment graphs. See References 9 and 28 for details and corresponding run-time analyses.

### 7.4.2 MATE-CLEVER

While CLEVER discovers mid-sized deletions, the accuracy at both high recall and precision (see Section 7.6) of the predicted breakpoints suffers from the usual defaults that are common to segment size-based approaches. MATE-CLEVER, as a hybrid approach, aims at curing this and discovers deletions not only at high recall and precision but at high accuracy of their breakpoints (see Figure 7.5 for an illustration of MATE-CLEVER).

The workflow of MATE-CLEVER (10) is that of a common hybrid approach where step 2 makes use of CLEVER in the first step. Subsequently, in step 3, it makes use of a novel split-read aligner, LASER (29), which has been particularly trimmed to compute highly accurate split-read alignments reflecting larger gaps also. The outputs of MATE-CLEVER are mid-sized (and long-sized) deletions, discovered by CLEVER, where breakpoints are corrected and therefore highly accurate.

### 7.4.3 PINEL

PINEL (14) is a split-read aligner that specializes in also discovering deletions longer than 20bp at extremely high precision, with more than 90% of all calls being true positives that are also accurate in breakpoint annotations, across all size ranges. In doing so, it achieves clearly the highest recall rates among all (split-read) alignment-based approaches. Note that GSNAP (30, 31) achieves higher recall of deletions of 30–50 bp, which, however, comes at the price of reduced precision and (much) less accurate breakpoint annotations.

Overall, PINDEL follows the workflow com split-read aligners. Its achievements are due to an accumulation of improvements in the fine details, which in combination yield superior method. We refer the reader to (14), the original publication, for details.

### 7.5.1 A Maximum Likelihood Approach under Read Alignment Uncertainty

Let $G_i$ for $i = 0, 1, 2$ represent the genotypes of an indel, where $G_0$ indicates absence of indel, $G_1$ indicates the allele is heterozygous, and $G_2$ indicates the indel is homozygous. Let $A$ be a read alignment. $R$ be all reads. For $R \in R$, $A(R)$ be the alignment of $R$ with the region we wish to genotype. We write $A^+$ for the event that $A$ is the correct alignment and $A^-$ for it. Not that $P(A^-) = 1 - P(A^+)$. We formally consider each $R \in R$ as the disjoint union of the two events $A^+$ and $A^-$. Let $S \subset R$ be a subset of reads. In slight abuse of notation, we also consider $S$ as the event where precisely the alignments of $S$ are correct, while all others are not. Hence,

$$P(S) = \prod_{R \in S} P(A^+(R)) \cdot \prod_{R \in S} (1 - P(A^+(R))) \quad (7.1)$$

is the corresponding probability.

In the following, we consider a maximum likelihood (ML) setting, which in particular reflects that our prior belief in genotypes is for all for types:

$$P(G_0) = P(G_1) = P(G_2) = \frac{1}{3} \quad (7.2)$$

Making use of an ML approach allows to attain an efficient computation scheme. We point out below that a full Bayesian approach is infeasible of being exponential in the number of reads that align with the region of interest – note that we wish to genotype hundreds of thousands of reads of interest, such that run-time considerations a a crucial factor.

We are interested in maximizing

$$P(G_i \mid R) \propto P(G_i, R) = \sum_{S \in R} P(S) \cdot P(G_i \mid S). \quad (7.3)$$

By taking probabilities $P(S)$ into account, we would like to appropriately address alignment uncertainty, which can be due to several factors such as multiple mappings and alignment artifacts. Let $K := |R|$ be the number of reads that align to the region to be genotyped. By Bayes’ formula, Equation (7.2) further implies that

$$P(G_i \mid S) \overset{(7.2)}{\propto} \prod_{S \in R} P(A^+(R) \mid G_i) \cdot \prod_{S \in R} P(A^-(R) \mid G_i)$$
$$\overset{(7.2)}{\propto} \prod_{S \in R} P(G_i \mid A^+(R)) \cdot \prod_{S \in R} P(G_i \mid A^-(R)), \quad (7.4)$$

where the equality is justified by assuming that reads have been generated independently of each other. Note that the computation (7,4) is not possible in the frame of a fully Bayesian approach because only the assumption (7.2) of constant priors is infeasible. This renders such an undertaking infeasible. From (7,4), we conclude that

$$P(G_i \mid R) \propto \sum_{S \in R} P(S) \cdot \prod_{S \in R} P(G_i \mid S) \cdot \prod_{S \in R} P(G_i \mid A^-(R))$$
$$\overset{(7.1)}{\propto} \sum_{S \in R} P(A^+(R))P(G_i \mid A^+(R)) + (1 - P(A^+(R)))P(G_i \mid A^-(R)), \quad (7.5)$$

where the second row follows from the first row.
