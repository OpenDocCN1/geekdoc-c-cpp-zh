# GENOMIC VARIANTS DETECTION AND GENOTYPING

## 6.1 INTRODUCTION

Complete and accurate identification of genomic variation among species, populations, and even tissues is one of the most important steps towards understanding phenotypic variation. Recent developments in genomics have served as building blocks to produce major advances in fields including evolution and population genetics (1); medical research (3); crop breeding (4); and industrial production of food and biofuels. Since it was discovered that DNA molecules encode most of the genomic information as a long chain of four possible nucleotides: adenine ('A'), cytosine ('C'), guanine ('G'), and thymine ('T'), the process of determining the sequences is called sequencing (6). Once DNA sequences of two or more samples are compared through sequence alignment in order to identify variation, the most studied form of genomic variations are single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). As its name indicates, a SNP is a DNA stretch in which two sequences differ only in one nucleotide. The base pairs that differ are usually called SNP alleles, and the process of determining the alleles in a sample for a given SNP is called genotyping. SNPs have been thoroughly studied in different organisms because they are well distributed across the genome and automated methods have been developed to perform cost-effective and high-quality genotyping across several samples (7). These properties make SNPs amenable for evolution and population genetic studies (2), performing mapping of traits through QTL analysis or genome-wide association studies (GWAS) (4), and performing marker-assisted selection (8). However, because SNPs only produce minor effects in the DNA structure (with the exception of SNPs in regulatory or coding regions), they are not causative for phenotypic variation and their association with traits is normal due to high linkage disequilibrium (LD) (4). Hence, complete understanding of genomic variation requires the identification of other types of variants with larger footprints. These types of variants include insertions and deletions (usually summarized as indels), copy number variants (CNVs), inversions, and translocations (9). Figure 6.1 shows a schematic of the different types of variation that can be identified between the DNA sequences of two samples.

Indels can be defined as DNA sequences present only in a subset of samples. The determination of insertion or deletion status depends on choosing a particular sequence as reference if the sequence is inserted or deleted from the reference. Indels are also well distributed across the genome, but they are less frequent and not as to genotype as SNPs. Large indels can span whole genes and hence can be causative for significant phenotypic variation.
