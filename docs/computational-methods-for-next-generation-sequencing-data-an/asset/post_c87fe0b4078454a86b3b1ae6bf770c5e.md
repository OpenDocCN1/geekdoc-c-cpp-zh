## 16.2.6 Quasis Assembly Performance

Performance of quaspecies assembly depends on many factors: the input sample and sequencing data layout needs to be designed for underlying assembly. Sufficient overlap of amplicons is necessary to enable read assembly across amplicons. The heterogeneity of the underlying population including haplotype distribution along the genome, the read length, and error rate of the sequencing technology are closely coupled to haplotype reconstruction. Even if there is enough diversity to distinguish between haplotypes, if homogeneous regions are longer than the average read length, phasing is limited (Figure 16.7). Solutions to overcome homogeneous regions are either PacBio (Figure 16.7d) or paired-end reads (Figure 16.7c) that contain a sufficiently large insert size to bridge regions. Another strategy is to fix haplotype frequencies of flanking regions. In fact, short reads cannot be compensated by higher coverage (45). Reconstruction of low-frequency haplotypes demands ultradeep sequencing approach with uniform coverage.

The sequence along one chromosome is called haplotype and is defined as an element of $[A, C, G, T]^L$, with $L$ being the length of the chromosome. A genotype is the matching of two haplotypes for an individual. As many positions are conserved, a haplotype can be reduced to variant sites. Sequencing errors can be easily corrected as variants are bi-allelic, a variant occurs with 50% frequency. Low-frequency variants are identified as sequencing errors and corrected with dominant nucleotide. Variant sites are bi-allelic, they can only take one of two possible values, and a haplotype can be represented as an element of $[0, 1]^{L'}$, with $L'$ being the number of heterogeneous positions.

## 16.3.1.1 Efficient Human Phasing Algorithms

Apart from probabilistic modeling, one approach to human haplotype inference has been formulated with respect to the evolutionary history of a set of haplotypes, the population-genetic concept of coalescent (47). Given a single haplotype site of one individual, its history can be traced back in an acyclic graph to the site of one parent haplotype. This means that the history of two haplotypes at one site merges at one of their most recent ancestor. Under the infinite-sites assumption and without recombination, the coalescent model says that 2n haplotypes of 2n individuals can be displayed as a tree with 2n leaves m edges representing the individual sites. If haplotypes are binary and the root is labeled as a binary sequence, the tree displaying the evolution of the haplotype is called perfect phylogeny. The perfect phylogeny problem is to find the set of explaining haplotypes for a set of genotypes. Various phylogeny methods have been developed (48–50) and it was first introduced and solved in Reference 51.

The first probabilistic models in human haplotype belonging to the “gene cutting” models (52–56). These models compute the maximum likelihood estimate of haplotype frequencies from multilocus, unphased genome count data via an expectation maximization (EM) algorithm. This approach provides haplotype frequencies that can be used to compute the most likely pairing of haplotypes for a given genotype.

The maximum likelihood approach can be regarded as a special case of Bayesian inference with a flat prior for the haplotype frequencies, that is, all haplotypes are equally likely. An informative prior for a guided inference process has the potential to solve factors that respect an underlying genealogy and to estimate the haplotype frequencies in the software PHASE (57), which provides better results than its predecessors; the speed of convergence is slow for whole-genome data. To achieve the run-improvement in Reference 58, the partition–ligation strategy (54) has been adopted. A genomic region is split into multiple smaller regions. Haplotype inference is performed on adjacent regions individually. Using the current parameter estimates, adjacent haplotypes are merged into larger haplotype. This approach does not grow along the genome.
