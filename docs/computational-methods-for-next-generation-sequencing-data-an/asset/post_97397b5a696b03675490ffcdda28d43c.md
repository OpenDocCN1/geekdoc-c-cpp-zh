Cultivation-independent rRNA gene surveys over the past two decades have revolutionized our perception of the microbial world and revealed the presence of numerous candidate divisions, the so-called microbial dark matter, known solely on the basis of rRNA gene sequence information.

### 2.3.3 Clusters of Orthologous Groups

The use of conserved single-copy functional genes provides a powerful adjunct or alternative to rRNA amplicon sequencing when profiling microbial community structure. Indeed, COGs provide collections of anchor genes useful in estimating genome completion and coverage and in the construction of phylogenetic trees (78, 79). Each COG contains orthologous proteins shared between at least three lineages. Because orthologous proteins tend to share equivalent functions, this information can be propagated throughout a cluster providing a rapid route for functional annotation of individual genomes or metagenomic data (Figure 2.6a). Interestingly, sequencing projects have generated a large number of hypothetical ORFs that are conserved across taxonomic groups. While incorporating these sequences into COGs has traditionally relied on manual annotation, the sheer number of incoming sequences has driven the development of automated alternatives including the EggNOG database (80). In its second version, EggNOG uses reciprocal BLAST and the "triangle homology" method to build new clusters and append to existing ones (81). While the COG database is included in MetaPathways, the EggNOG database can be added as an unformatted fasta file to the database directory. This file will automatically be formatted to provide the next-and-extend search and results will be combined with other database outputs.

MetaPathways uses BLAST to query predicted protein sequences against reference databases using RefSeq and COG for taxonomic annotation. BLAST scores are calculated and reported as described in the functional annotations section. COG annotations are returned with their declination on the CO functional hierarchy with results exported to the results/annotation_tables/ COG annotations for each read are summarized in a tabular format for the results/annotation_tables/ or directly seen in the MetaPathways v2.0 GUI.

MetaPathways also supports functional gene profiling using MLTreeMap (37). MLTreeMap automatically places marker genes onto a highly resolved phylogeny from a multiple alignment or "supermatrix" of 40 universal COGs (82). Taxonomic assignment proceeds in three steps. First marker genes are identified using nucleotide or protein sequences using BLAST and GeneWise (33). Detected marker genes are added to curated reference alignments using hmmalind and Gblocks (84, 85). These aligned sequences are then placed into annotated reference phylogenies using RAAML (86, 87) (Figure 2.6b). In addition to universal COG reference phylogenies, user-defined trees can be appended to MLTreeMap supporting both phylogenetic and functional anchoring.

### 2.3.3.4 Lowest Ancestor

Taxonomic annotation of environmental sequence information is sensitive to read length and assembly. Although the presence of phylogenetic anchors such as rRNA genes on assembled contigs enables direct annotation of linked ORFs, individual ORF assignments based on BLAST or BLAST can be confounded by shared sequence homology between a number of different taxonomic groups. The LCA algorithm originally incorporated into MEGAN (34) uses BLAST scores to conservatively place sequences into the NCBI taxonomy hierarchy. The LCA represents the lowest node in the NCBI taxonomy to which a sequence can be assigned on the expected taxonomic range of its BLAST hits (Figure 2.6c). ### 2.3.4 ePGDB Construction

Pathway-centric approaches that predict metabolic networks using defined rules offer a more robust route for quantitative insights. Recently, pathway prediction and metabolic flux algorithms have been developed for microbial communities including the Human Microbiome Analysis Network (HUMANN) and Predicted Relative Metabolic (PRMT), respectively. HumanN combines gene prediction with MinPath (88), a parsimony approach for metabolic reconstruction based on KEGG pathway-mapping (12, 65). PRMT predicts metabolic flux using KEGG pathways across metagenomes. Neither HUMANN nor PRMT offers a coherent structure for exploring metabolic pathways. In addition to HumanN and PRMT, alternative algorithms for metabolic flux modeling within engineered consortia are emerging (90, 91).

MetaPathways uses Pathway Tools, a quality-control software environment developed at SRI that supports Pathway-Genome database (PGDB) construction (41, 42). The PGDB contains information on genes, reactions, and pathways that define metabolic and regulatory networks. The Pathologic algorithm in Pathway Tools allows users to construct new PGDBs from an annotated genome using MetaC (http://metacy.org/) (43, 44), a highly curated, nonredundant, and experimentally validated database of metabolic pathways representing all domains of life. Unlike KEGG or SEED subsystems, MetaC emphasizes smaller, evolutionary conserved or co-regulated units of metabolism and contains the largest collection (over 2000) of validated metabolic pathways (68). Navigable and extensively commented enzyme properties, pathway descriptions, and literature citations combined within a PGDB provide a coherent structure for exploring and interpreting metabolic networks. Although MetaPathways extends the PGDB concept to environmental sequence information enabling PGDB construction compatible with the editing, search, and navigation features of Pathway Tools (39).
