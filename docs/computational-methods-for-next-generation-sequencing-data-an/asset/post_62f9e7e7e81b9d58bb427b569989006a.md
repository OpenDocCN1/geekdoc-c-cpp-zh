## 3.2 DESIGN OF POOLS FOR BIG VIRAL DATA

The basic idea of the overlapping strategy for sequencing $n$ samples is to generate $m$ pools (i.e., mixtures of samples) with $m \ll n$ in such a way that every sample is uniquely identified by the pools to which it belongs (23). Then, after sequencing of pools, the obtained opinion reads can be assigned to samples by the sequence of set-theoretical intersections and differences of pools. The two examples show that a small number of pools can be used to identify a larger number of samples.

**Example 1.** Consider three samples $S_1, S_2, S_3$ and two pools $P_1 = S_1 \cup S_2, P_2 = S_2 \cup S_3$ (see Figure 3.2). These pools satisfy the separation requirement, and therefore, each sample can be recovered, for example, $S_2 = P_1 \cap P_2, S_1 = P_1 \setminus P_2$ and $S_3 = P_2 \setminus P_1$. Thus, pooling of all three requires two sequencing runs.

**Example 2.** As a more complex example, consider eight samples $S_1, \dots, S_8$ and four pools $P_1, \dots, P_4$ defined as follows: $P_1 = S_1 \cup S_2 \cup S_3 \cup S_4, P_2 = S_2 \cup S_6 \cup S_7, P_3 = S_1 \cup S_5 \cup S_6 \cup S_7$. These pools satisfy the separation requirement, and therefore each sample can be recovered by the sequence of intersections and differences of pools. For instance, $S_1 = P_1 \cap P_3 \cap P_4, S_2 = (P_1 \cap P_3) \setminus P_4, \dots, S_8 = (P_2 \setminus P_3) \setminus P_4$. **Theorem [23, 32].** If any subset of samples can form a single pool, then $n$ samples can be reconstructed using $m = [\log(n)] + 1$ pools.

**Proof.** As for simplicity $n$ is a power of 2, that is, $n = 2^k$ (the proof is analogous for any $n$). Then apply induction: if $k=1$, then $P=\{\{S_1\}, \{S_2\}\}$ clearly a valid pool design with $m=2$. ### 3.2.1 Pool Design Optimization Formulation

Sequencing of heterogeneous RNA viral samples imposes the following restrictions on the pool composition: (i) the maximal number of samples that can be pooled without losing detection of minority viral variants; and (ii) undesirability of mixing samples with drastically different viral titers, which may be epidemiologically much harder. Here, we formalize these restrictions and formulate the optimal pool design problem as an optimization problem on graphs.

Let $S=\{S_1, \dots, S_n\}$ be the set of samples. Let $P=\{P_1, \dots, P_m\}$ be the set of pools. A pool $P_j$ is a subset of $S$. Let $x_{ij} = 1$ if $S_i \in P_j$ and 0 otherwise. The goal is to find $x_{ij}$ such that:
- $\sum_{j=1}^m x_{ij} \ge 1$ for all $i=1, \dots, n$
- $\sum_{i=1}^n x_{ij} \le K$ for all $j$, where $K$ is the maximum capacity of a pool.
