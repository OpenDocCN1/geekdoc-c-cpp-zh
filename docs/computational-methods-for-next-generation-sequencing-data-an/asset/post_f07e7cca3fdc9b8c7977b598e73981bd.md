Bisulfite sequencing is the golden standard for DNA methylation, which provides a single-base resolution analysis. The whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS-seq) that incorporates bisulfite conversion with NGS has been successfully used to analyze the whole methylomes of several human cell types at the single-base resolution (3, 31–36). To perform WGBS-seq, the sheared genomic DNA (average size 200 bp) is first converted into a sequencing library by ligation to adaptors that contains 5-mCs. The adaptor-ligated DNA is then treated with bisulfite to convert unmethylated cytosines to uracil. After PCR amplification of the bisulfite-treated DNA, the library is sequenced by NGS. The WGBS-seq approach is capable of measuring the methylation status of more than 90% of cytosines in human genomes (3). WGBS is instrumental in the identification of CpG methylation in human ES cells and the identification of large partially methylated domains (up to megabases in length) in cancer genomes. However, the cost of WGBS approach is still quite expensive. In this regard, RRBS (reduced representation bisulfite sequencing) is a cost-effective method for sub-genome scale DNA methylation analysis. For RRBS, the high molecular genomic DNA is first digested with a methylation-insensitive restriction enzyme (MspI). A small fraction of the DNA (40–220 bp) is then extracted. After ligation to the methylated adaptors, the library is treated with bisulfite, amplified by PCR, and sequenced by NGS (37). Overall 8–14% of CpG sites in the human and mouse genome can be analyzed by RRBS. However, MspI restriction sites are biased distributed toward CpG regions (CpG island); therefore, more than 80% of CpG island and overall half of promoters in the human and mouse genomes can be surveyed by RRBS. One of the limitations is that the CpG sites analyzed by RRBS highly depend on the MspI restriction sites (CCGG) in the human genome. CpG sites outside of the MspI fragment will be analyzed by this method. Various targeted bisulfite sequencing approaches such as bisulfite padlock (38, 39), hybrid solution capture (40), multiplexed PCR (41) have been developed for analyzing DNA methylation patterns in specific gene of interest. However, most of these methods involve complex protocols and the upfront cost for synthesizing probes and primers is relatively high, and therefore hinder the adoption of these approaches (42).

Bisulfite treatment cannot differentiate 5-mC from 5hmC because it converts 5hmC to cytosine-5-methylsulfonate (CMS). CMS is read as “C” during sequencing. Booth et al. developed a novel method called “OxBS-seq,” utilizes perruthenate (Kru04) selectively oxidizing 5hmC to 5-fC. The subsequent bisulfite treatment converts 5-fc to uracil, unlike unmethylated cytosine. By subtracting out an OxBS-seq data from regular bisulfite results, Ox-seq can generate high-resolution 5-hmC map in samples (45). TAB-seq, another recently developed method, takes a different approach for single-nucleotide 5-hmC mapping (46). TAB-seq is based on the fact that Tet proteins can convert 5-mC to 5-hmC (12, 13), but also 5-hmC to 5-cAC, which behaves like unmethylated cytosines during bisulfite conversion. TAB-seq converts 5-hmC to 5-gC using β-GT in order to prevent the 5-hmC from being oxidized by Tet recombinant enzyme. After DNA samples are modified with recombinant mouse Tet1 enzyme during which all 5-hmC is converted to 5-cAC. The subsequent bisulfite treatment converts methylated cytosines and 5-cAC to uracil and 5-caU, leaving 5-ghmC unchanged. Once sequencing is complete, the modified 5-hmC (5-gC) are presented as cytosines. ## 9.2 ENRICHMENT-BASED APPROACHES

### 9.2.1 Data Analysis Procedure

#### 9.2.1.1 Sequence Mapping
Since enrichment-based sequencing captures DNA fragments with methylated CpGs, the read depth of each location is proportional to the level of methylation. To this, sequence reads are mapped to a genome using sophisticated programs such as BWA (50), Bowtie (51), and SOAP (52). Mapped reads are then processed in several steps to eliminate sequencing artifacts: PCR and sequencing duplicates. Specifically, all mapped reads to the same location are filtered except one so that each position has at most one mapped read. Since current sequencing technologies, the length of sequence reads is shorter than the length of fragments and methylated CpGs could lie beyond the sequenced region of a fragment, mapped reads lengthen the 3’-end up to the original (average) length of fragments.

#### 9.2.1.2 Methylation Quantification
As in RNA-seq analysis for gene expression, different numbers of sequence reads for samples should be taken into account absolute methylation level and to compare multiple samples. Like ChIP-seq, MeDip-seq and MB-seq require input sequencing to take into account genomic differences between the sequenced samples and the reference genome, for example, copy variants. There are many algorithms and programs to call significantly enriched regions (peaks) for ChIP-seq for transcription factors and histone marks: MACS (53), SICER (54), F-seq (55), and Useq (56). These were used for the analysis of enriched-based methylome sequencing data. In contrast, Batman (57), MEDIPS (58), and BALM (59) have been developed to identify enriched regions for methylome sequencing because the more methylated CpGs in a fragment, the more captured. In other words, if two loci have the same number of reads but a different number of CpGs, they have different methylation.
