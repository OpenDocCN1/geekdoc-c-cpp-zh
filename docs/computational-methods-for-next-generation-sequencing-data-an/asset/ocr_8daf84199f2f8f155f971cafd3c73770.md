In viral quaspecies studies, HIV-1 is a representative viral model. HIV-1 is the cause of a deadly pandemic, the acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), and is as such a major focus of viral research. There are 34 million people infected with HIV-1 worldwide. HIV-1 is composed of a small diploid single-strand RNA (ssRNA) genome with ~9.7 kilobases (kb) that evolves by point mutation and recombination, leading to a high intratype diversity that varies from a single to thousands of haplotypes (9). The genome is divided into three parts, the gag, pol, and env regions (Figure 16.3). The gag region contains genes for the protease, reverse transcriptase, RNase, and integrase. The env region contains genes for the receptor proteins. The genome is surrounded by a shell of capsid proteins, and an envelope. In the envelope, the protein env protrudes the surface. Env consists of a glycoprotein (gp) 120 cap and a gp41 gene. These protein surfaces enable the virus to attach and fuse with host cells to initiate the infectious cycle.

HIV-1 maintains a high intratype diversity that differs from the inpatient diversity (10). HIV diversity is due to two properties of HIV-1, a high reproductive capacity and a low-fidelity reverse transcriptase producing 2.5 x 10⁻⁵ substitutions per base and reverse transcription (RT). There are at least 10⁷–10¹⁸ HIV-1 infected cells per host (12). The effective population size is hard to estimate and has been reviewed in Reference 13. HIV-1 is able to generate every possible mutation along the genome by fate. Most of these mutations are not maintained because many of mutations result in loss-of-function or nonproductive viruses.

Research in the last 30 years has identified the genetic sequences and, where mutations of HIV-1 sequences are located. Very effective antiretroviral drugs have been developed to target diversity is high. Every patient may show different patterns of resistance mutations; therefore, treatment has to be personalized. In this case, genotyping, the identification of resistance mutations, is necessary to tailor the best possible regimen, that is, drug combination, for an individual patient (14, 15).
