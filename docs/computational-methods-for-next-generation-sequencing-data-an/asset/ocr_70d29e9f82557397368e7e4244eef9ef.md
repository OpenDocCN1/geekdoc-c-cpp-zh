Since adjacent CpGs have similar methylation to enable working together on regulating cell activity, a sliding window approach is frequently used for methylation profiling. Most studies have used 100 or 200 bp windows for hot spots of methylation change and often 1000 or 5000 bp for global methylation. There are two ways to compute the beta score of a sliding window. In Figure 9.10, a window has 4 CpGs. Its beta score is defined as average of beta scores of the CpGs or the fraction of the methylated CpGs to the total number of CpGs in reads: 0.63 or 0.6.

**9.3.1.3 Differential Methylation** One of the most important goals in DNA methylation studies is to identify DMRs (Differentially Methylated Regions) among groups. Differential methylation is quantified as the difference or fold change between the beta scores of samples. Since beta values are in the range of 0-1, the difference, hereafter called the delta score, is preferred. Therefore, hyper- and hypomethylation produce positive and negative delta scores, respectively. Statistical tests produce more relevant than descriptive analysis for differential methylation. If there is no replicate for each sample or one sample for each group, Fisher's exact test is performed for each CpG to a 2 by 2 contingency table for the number of methylated and unmethylated reads for two samples. If each sample has replicates or each group has two or more samples, then statistical tests such as the Student $t$ test, Wilcoxon test, Rao-Scott test, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test can identify DMRs for each CpG. Statistical tests generate a p-value of each CpG and a genome has many CpGs, the p-values should be adjusted for multiple tests using Bonferroni, or false discovery rate. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) can be used to compare three or more samples of groups simultaneously. Several algorithms have been developed for better or alternative DMR identification. DMEAS (87) measures methylation entropy represents the complexity of methylation patterns in a region using information content and examines whether a heterogeneous methylation pattern is allele-specific or cell-specific.
