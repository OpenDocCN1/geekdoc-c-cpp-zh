This would be a nice, simple pattern and you could claim to have found a biomarker for this disease. Unfortunately, in most studies to date, patterns like this have failed to emerge. There is simply too much variability between humans, and it is often found that richness and diversity tell you nothing about an individual's health status. (See Figure 18.1, where richness and diversity tell so little that is useful about the three groups being compared.) In addition, relative diversity levels tend to remain constant across body sites (7). In other words, someone who has high diversity at body site A is likely to have high diversity at site B. It is not known if diversity levels change when an individual changes, say from healthy to diseased. Although these broad standard measures merit tracking, they are probably useful as indicators of the complexity of the community being studied. It is generally more interesting to know which particular OTUs are present and how their abundance levels are changing at different stages of disease progression. This is the true starting point for constructing a network of interactions.
