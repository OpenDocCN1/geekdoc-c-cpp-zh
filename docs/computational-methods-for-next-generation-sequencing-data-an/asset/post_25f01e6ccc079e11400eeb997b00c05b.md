## 17.2 BACKGROUND

### 17.2.1 Infectious Bronchitis Virus

RNA viruses are causing a significant burden on the health and productivity of culturally important animals since the rapid evolution of RNA viruses within infected hosts is coupled in this context with frequent transmissions between animals, due to the typically high animal density in production environments. Poultry farms are particularly susceptible to viral infections, which cause significant economic losses worldwide in terms of impaired growth, reduced egg production and quality, and even mortality.

In the United States with infections with viral strains of Newcastle disease and highly pathogenic influenza, IBV is considered the biggest cause of economic loss. It infects the domestic fowl (1). Apart from causing respiratory disease, it may also replicate at many non-respiratory surfaces. First discovered in the United States in 1930, IBV has undergone exponential population growth (2) and is now distributed worldwide, with dozens of circulating serotypes that may differ by as much as 25% in the amino acid sequence of the variable region of the spike glycoprotein.

As serotypes cross-protect, chicken may get infected multiple times during their lifetime. IBV infection of broilers retards growth while in layers drops down egg production. Young chickens may die directly from IBV infection, but a greater number due to secondary bacterial infections. Vaccination, most commonly with attenuated vaccines, is short-lived and have to be revived multiple times during their lifespan, with different serotypes.

Several IBV strains can co-circulate in a region, creating conditions for recombination between strains and resulting in complex evolution and epidemiology (3). Cloning and sequencing have been used to show that IBV variants exist in infected poultry (4) as well as in widely used commercial attenuated vaccines (5). Furthermore, McKinley et al. (5) have shown that attenuated vaccines in vivo undergo selection following vaccination and can infect contact-exposed chickens. With significant field persistence of Arkansas-type vaccine viruses (6), this reinforces the evidence that infectious IBV strains can emerge from the evolution of attenuated vaccines in commercial flocks (7).

### 17.2.2 High-Throughput Sequencing

Recent advances in HTS technologies such as the Roche 454 FLX Titanium, Illumina Genome Analyzer, and ABI SOLiD have led to orders of higher

Indeed, at full capacity, each one of these HTS sequencers is capable of producing millions/billions of sequences per day. Coupled with continuously decreasing prices, HTS has profoundly transformed genomics, enabling a host of novel HTS applications ranging from transcription analysis (9, 10) and noncoding RNAs (11) to detection of epigenetic changes, such as DNA methylation (12) and histone modifications (13), and from genome sequencing (14, 15) to metagenomics (16, 17) and paleogenomics (18, 19). HTS is also emerging as a key technology for quasispecies analysis since it eliminates the cloning step and allows sampling a much larger fraction of the quasispecies (20). Initial studies have focused on using HTS to identify low-frequency drug-resistant variants in human patients infected with HIV (21–25). Recently, HTS has been used to monitor the effects of an siRNA therapy for the treatment of HIV infection (26) and to characterize the quasispecies of the Pandemic H1N Influenza virus (27). Just as PCR and automated Sanger sequencing revolutionized molecular biology, HTS is expected to transform research on phylodynamics of RNA viruses (28).

However, fully realizing the potential of HTS technologies requires the development of novel analysis methods. Analysis is challenging due to the huge amount of data generated by HTS technologies, on one hand, and to the short read lengths and high error rates, on the other. As a consequence, many tools developed for Sanger reads do not work at all or have impractical times when applied to HTS data. Even newly developed algorithms for *de novo* genome assembly from HTS data (29–32) are tuned for reconstruction of haploid genomes and work poorly when the sequenced sample contains a large number of closely related sequences, as Methods designed to reconstruct haplotypes from shotgun sequencing (33–39) cannot be extended to reconstruct the full spectrum of quasispecies in the sample.

## 17.3 METHODS

Many tools developed for Sanger reads do not work at all or have impractical times when applied to HTS data. Even newly developed algorithms for *de novo* genome assembly from HTS data are tuned for reconstruction of haploid genomes and work poorly when the sequenced sample contains a large number of closely related sequences. To address these shortcomings, we introduce reconstruction flows for accurate reconstruction of quasispecies and estimate their frequencies from HTS data where we incorporate different error correction methods, aligners, and genome assemblers (ViSPA (40), and ShoRAH (41), using different parameters. It should be noted other recent genome tools such as Quasicomb (43) (another program from ShoRAH's development team), BIOA:VirA (44), QuRe (45), and PredictHaplo (46). In the current work, we considered only ShoRAH ViSPA. We applied experiments on IBV 454 shotgun reads collected from commercial poultry farms. For method validation, we use viral clones (ground truth). The proposed reconstruction flows consist of the following stages (see Figure 17.1):

- 1. Read Error Correction. This step is necessary since the reads produced by Next Generation Sequencing systems are prone to errors, and it is important to distinguish reading errors from viral variants. Alternate methods, Life Sciences can erroneously sequence 1 bp per 1000 bp (47). The error rate is strongly related to the presence and size of homopolymers (48), that is, genome regions consisting of consecutive repetitive of a single base (e.g., TTTTT). We use KEC, SAET, and ShoRAH to do error correction prior to assembly. These error correction algorithms involve clustering of reads. While ShoRAH clusters the reads in Bayesian manner using the Dirichlet process mixture (41), and KEC clusters reads based on k-mers (49), SAET uses reads of quality scores for error correction (50).
