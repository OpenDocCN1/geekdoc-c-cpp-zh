“safe-SeqS”, has been proposed in Reference 11 and applied to detect rare somatic mutations, but its application on detecting rare viral mutations has been neglected. The protocol involves two steps. The first is the assignment of a unique barcode to each DNA template molecule to be analyzed. The second is the amplification of each uniquely tagged template so that many copies of a molecule with the preexcited mutation are generated. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) fragments with the barcode are considered mutant (“supermutants”) only if ≥ 90% of them contain the identical mutation. Errors occurring during the amplification steps or sequencing errors in base calling should not rise to supermutants.

In our work (5) similar to Safe-SeqS, we apply a special library preparation technique that eliminates errors during the de-duplexing step. The proposed approach attaches individual barcode sequences for every fragment and then amplifies each tagged fragment. After the fragments are sequenced, regardless of the original fragment are clustered based on the barcode. It follows that every sequenced position of the fragment would have multiple independent evidence, suitably promoting highly accurate consensus reads. Given that many reads are required to sequence each fragment, we trade off an increase in sequence coverage for a reduction in error rate. By applying an error-correction procedure of the protocol, we are able to address both sequencing and PCR errors, which leads to high accuracy. Deep coverage and highly accurate data allow for accurate estimation of the underlying diversity of a viral population. Importantly, in order to detect ultra-rare variants, the low per-base sequencing cost of the Illumina platform makes it realistic to increase coverage.

# 4.3 ASSEMBLY OF HIGH-FIDELITY SEQUENCING DATA

# 4.3.1 Consensus Construction

We apply the de novo consensus reconstruction tool, Vicuna (15) prior to assembly to produce a linear consensus from the sequence data. As far as our sequencing method does not contain any particularly low coverage region, we can reconstruct population consensus for viral use. Vicuna produces multiple contigs rather than a complete consensus, so we use BLAST to merge contigs. Specifically, we require 50nt overlap to merge any of pairs. This approach offers more flexibility for samples that do not have “close” reference sequences available. Traditional assembly methods (16–18) aim to reconstruct a linear consensus sequence and are not well suited for assembling a large number of highly similar distinct viral genomes. We instead take ideas from haplotype assembly methods (19, 20), which aim to reconstruct closely related haplotypes. However, these methods are not applicable for assembly of a large (a priori unknown) number of individual genomes. Many existing viral assembly methods estimate local population diversity and are not well suited for assembling full-length quasispecies variants spanning the entire viral genome. Available genome-wide assemblers are originally designed for low throughput and are impractical for high-throughput technologies containing millions of sequencing reads.
