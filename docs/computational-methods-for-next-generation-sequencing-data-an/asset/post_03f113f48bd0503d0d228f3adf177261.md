One of the first used measures of transcript/isoform abundance for RNA-seq is the count of RPKM (single-end reads; reads per kilobase per million) and FPKM (paired-end reads; fragments per kilobase per million; (18)). Obviously, thousands of reads mapping to a 10 kb gene body (5' UTR+) compared with the same number mapping to a 1 kb gene body does not represent the abundance level. Normalization methods attempt to take into account differences in gene length and library sizes between samples being compared. RPKM and FPKM are relative measurements of expression, not an absolute measurement. Some have found faults with FPKM, particularly introducing biases in genes that are expressed at low levels (19) and there are other ways of measuring abundance (20). This is a very fluid and evolving field, and one reason FPKM is common due to the popularity of the software packages, which many of us use here, that output value a related value that is also gaining acceptance, TPM (transcripts per million reads). This is also a term that biologists are becoming used to understanding and expect to see. Given that, RSEM, which we use below, can output a related value. Another important issue in terms of quantification is determining the minimum accepted abundance value that is believable. Reasons for this include difficulty in reliably quantifying abundance measurements when they are based on a very small number of reads or random transcriptional noise. A commonly accepted convention in RNA-seq quantification of this issue using to only accept FPKM values less than or equal to 1.0. A comprehensive analysis of this transcriptome data suggests that a lower cutoff of FPKM=0.3 is often justifiable (21). Alternatively, the use of spike-in samples can be used to give confidence to low expressed genes. ## 12.3 ASSEMBLY

Assembly of sequenced reads into transcripts can be broadly categorized into two types: reference-based or de novo. In a reference-based assembly, the reads are aligned with the reference genome; two classes of reads emerge, those that map above a stringent threshold (mapped to a continuous location with only a few mismatches/gaps allowed) and those that are unppable by those criteria. The latter represent reads that potentially map across junctions or simply bad reads. The mapped reads are used to generate a set of all possible exons and thus possible splice junctions between neighboring exons within a distance, which would depend on the genome of interest, and the previously unmappable reads are then aligned to this collection. Transcripts, isoforms, and their expression levels can be derived from these two sets of alignments.

On the other hand, de novo assembly, the reads are assembled into contigs without any prior assumptions about annotation or splice junctions, these contigs themselves represent transcripts. Expression values for a given data set can then be derived by the coverage of these contigs, de novo assembly is valuable for organisms with well-annotated reference genomes as this can allow either for discovery or for a more accurate assembly of a transcriptome from a nonreference isolate of species, which may be diverted at the sequence level from a sequenced genome and annotation. de novo assembly is essential for the analysis of many nonmodel organisms for which neither exists nor the genome is of poor quality.

Based on alignment methodology, the reference-based assemblers are grouped into two major categories, namely hash-table or prefix/suffix mapping algorithms. In the hash-table-based approach, we use some tools such as MAQ (24), Bowtie (25), TopHat (26), Genom (27), RNAmap (28), Stringtie (29), HISAT (30), Kallisto (31), Salmon (32), and Bowtie2 (33).
