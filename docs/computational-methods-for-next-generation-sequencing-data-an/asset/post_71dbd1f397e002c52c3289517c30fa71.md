### 7.1 INTRODUCTION

Although next-generation sequencing (NGS) experiments have become standard, the exploration of the data still poses challenges. NGS experiments usually aim at providing catalogs of genetic variants to be used in downstream analyses of interest. In population studies, such as the “Genome of the Netherlands” or the “1000 Genomes” initiatives (1, 2), such catalogs allow to reflect the full extent of genetic diversity of populations. In cancer genome studies (see Reference 3 for a global initiative), comprehensive lists of somatic variants are sought to help understand cancer (sub)types and disease progression and to select appropriate therapy protocols.

In this chapter, we focus on techniques for next-generation sequencing studies, which allow to show the differences between a *donor genome*, a genome to be investigated, and a reference genome. The workflow common to resequencing studies proceeds to the following steps:

- (1) The DNA of the genome of interest is broken into fragments.
- (2) The fragments are next-generation sequencing, which yields *reads*. A very popular and helpful technique is to generate *paired-end reads*, fragments both ends of which are sequenced with an internal part, the *internal segment*, which remains unsequenced.

### 7.1.1 Twilight Deletions

In this chapter, we focus on deletions that are hard to discover because of their length. As mentioned earlier, short deletions as well as long deletions are no longer fundamental difficulties, or even become part of best-practice workflows. Mid-sized deletions, which we refer to as “NGS twilight deletions”, have become substantial computational challenges after 2009. Most recent advances have made their discovery possible in population-scale practice (9, 10, 14) – earlier approaches had pointed out theoretical ways, but remained slower by orders of magnitude (15). Evidence of this is the fact that catalogs of deletions resulting from projects (1), where References (9, 10) have been used, contain comprehensive amounts of such twilight deletions, with excellent validation rates. This is stark contrast to related projects (in particular the 1000 Genomes Project (2)).

In this chapter, we review discovery and genotyping of mid-sized deletions has been difficult and explain the techniques by which this became possible. The organization of this chapter is as follows:

- In Section 7.2, we provide the necessary notation.
- In Section 7.3, we give the formal definition of “twilight zone” deletions. We briefly revisit the different approaches for deletion discovery in resequencing studies, and outline our pitfalls when it comes to discovering mid-sized (“twilight zone”) deletions.
- In Section 7.5, we present a novel maximum likelihood approach for genotyping deletions which achieves highly favorable performance rates on twilight zone indels.
- In Section 7.6, we evaluate a comprehensive selection of state-of-the-art tools on NGS reads from a genome containing rare variants (Venter's genome (16)), where NGS reads are simulated by means of the Assemblathon (17) read simulator and current NGS technology (Illumina HiSeq and MiSeq reads).
- In Section 7.7, we discuss all results presented and point out challenges that are still open.

### 7.2 NOTATION

We predominantly focus on paired-end read, the most widely used data in resequencing studies. Let Σ = {A, C, G, T, N} be the set of nucleotides. A paired-end read consists of two sequences $r_1$ and $r_2$ that are known to be separated by a distance $L$ on the reference genome. When $r_1$ and $r_2$ are mapped to the reference genome at positions $x_1$ and $x_2$ respectively, the distance between them is $|x_1 - x_2|$. The expected distance is $L$, but the observed distance $|x_1 - x_2|$ varies around $L$ due to the nature of the library preparation. The segment of the reference genome between $r_1$ and $r_2$ that is not sequenced is called the internal segment, its length is $I(A)$. If $x_1 < x_2$, and $r_1$ starts at $x_1$ and has length $length(r_1)$, then the internal segment spans from $x_1 + \text{length}(r_1)$ to $x_2$. The length of the internal segment is $I(A) = x_2 - (x_1 + \text{length}(r_1))$. If $x_2 < x_1$, the length is $I(A) = x_1 - (x_2 + \text{length}(r_2))$.

### 7.2.1 Alignments

Alignments $A=(A_1,A_2)$ can be mapped, where gaps indicate insertions or deletions. For the sake of simplicity, we assume that each alignment displays at most one gap. We write $u_g$ for the reference position that precedes the gap, and $z_g$ for the reference position that immediately follows the gap. In turn, we refer to the position in the read that precedes the gap as $u_A$ and the position in the read that follows the gap as $v_A$; that, reference $\mathcal{G}[u_g]$ aligns with $R[u_A]$ and $\mathcal{G}[z_g]$ aligns with $R[v_A]$. Depending on whether $z_g = u_A+1$ (see alignment D in 7.1) or $u_g = v_A+1$ (see alignment C), the gap indicates an insertion in the donor genome (where the inserted sequence is $R[u_A+1, v_A-1]$) or a deletion in the donor genome (where the deleted sequence is $\mathcal{G}[u_A+1, z_g-1]$).

### 7.2.3 Deletions

Let $D_L$ and $D_R$ be the reference coordinates of the left and right breakpoint of a deletion $D$. That is, nucleotides from (and including) position $D_L$ all (including) position $D_R$, which together form the sequence $\mathcal{G}[D_L, D_R]$, are missing in the donor genome. We define $C(D) := \frac{D_L+D_R}{2}$ be the center of $D$ (which need not be an integer) and $L(D) := D_R-D_L+1$ be the length of the deletion. We parameterize the deletion $D=(C(D), L(D))$ by its center $C(D)$ and its length $L(D)$.
