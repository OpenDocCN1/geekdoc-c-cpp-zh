This finding is likely to be attributed to treatment effect sizes they explored: in most clinical trials the ratio is fairly high, i.e. between-group variability is much smaller than within-group variability. With the landscape of clinical trials constantly changing, this assumption has to be evaluated carefully on a case-by-case basis; as budgets tighter and competition increases, many sponsors are forced into powering their trials more aggressively for larger commercially viable effect sizes. In these situations, blinded SSR methods should be applied with caution, as the key assumptions driving their performance (i.e. low signal to noise ratio) may get violated and are likely to increase sample size unnecessarily or fail to increase sample size when needed.

Additional methods for blinded SSR with normal endpoints include the EM (expectation minimization) algorithm, the block-sum estimation of variance, and the unequal allocation within blocks method [7-9]. These methods are less commonly used due to various statistical issues. In general, we will not discuss these challenges here and refer the reader to published reviews. For example, several references have a back-and-forth debate over the EM method [10-12].

For trials with a binary endpoint, several methods are also available. Gould describes a procedure for updating a sample size using the observed overall rate at the interim analysis [13,14]. A similar strategy in the context of a non-inferiority trial with binary endpoint is described in [15]. Variance estimation using blocks of unequal treatment allocation for binary data is proposed in [16].

## 3.3 Unblinded SSR Methods

### 3.3.1 Overview

Unlike blinded section, the unblinded SSR procedures utilize both the nuisance parameter and explicit treatment effect estimates in order to calculate the new sample size for the remainder of the trial. The methods vary in how they perform such adaptation but they all share a common feature: the methodology is focused on rigorously controlling the type I error. Type I error is very important to regulators in confirmatory trials (the majority of unblinded SSR trials are performed in confirmatory setting). Type I error inflation can potentially arise from multiple looks at the data (interim and final analyses) and data-driven sample size changes.

There are two major issues concerning unblinded SSR: 1) controlling the type I error rate mentioned earlier and 2) operational issues arising from interim unblinding (the potential to compromise the integrity of the trial). The first issue is statistical in nature and can be addressed by implementing an appropriate methodology assuring type I control. The second concern is operational, which is much harder to address because it is largely based on perception. While one can implement certain procedures to minimize the chances of accidental unblinding and loss of study integrity, the concerns can never be eliminated completely. Operational issues can very easily become an extensive discussion on their own; for reason we will not discuss it here and refer the reader to [17-18] for discussion of best practices for adaptive designs (unblinded SSR included).

Blinded SSR methods do not cause concerns about unblinding and their impact on type I is minimal, so they suffer from the key issues of unblinded SSR as much. For that reason, they were classified as "well-understood" by the FDA guidance [1]. It is important to understand the circumstances under which the "less-well understood" procedure of unblinded SSR became preferred over the "well-understood" blinded SSR procedure.

Conversely, in an unrestricted blinded SSR, a decrease in the actual treatment difference leads to reduced variance estimate, and a potential decrease in the sample size compared to the original planned size. These changes are the opposite to that that are actually needed. This can happen because blinded estimate of variance essentially ignores the between-treatment difference in its calculation. They are efficient only when there is a reason to believe that actual treatment difference is not too far from what was assumed at the design stage. In situations where there is great uncertainty about the underlying treatment effect, the unblinded SSR procedure may be well worth the additional effort to implement.

A second reason to consider an unblinded SSR procedure is that a sample size modification may not be the only objective of an interim analysis. In other words, other types of adaptation may be of interest. In more and trials routinely implement early stopping for efficacy or futility (e.g. classical GSD), both of which would require unblinding. If unblinding is already considered in the study plan, then using an unblinded SSR is a lot more precise than a blinded SSR. In fact, many unblinded SSR methodologies evolved as an adaptation to increase sample size on top of GSD-like boundaries for early stopping, i.e. as a way to expand the list of adaptation options available at the time of interim analysis. In general, it is very hard to get an overall recommendation on when to use one adaptation versus another. Each trial should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, preferably by simulation that includes a good variety of reasonable treatment effect/variance scenarios. Based on the resulting operating characteristics and the likelihood of underlying assumptions, a final choice between blinded vs unblinded SSR procedures should be made.

For the remainder of this section, we focus on the statistical issues (e.g. controlling type I error rate resulting from multiple looks at the data) surrounding unblinded SSR. There are three main statistical issues to address (and pre-specify) when considering a method for unblinded SSR:

- 1. How to determine the new sample size?
- 2. How to set the final decision rule to accept/reject the null hypothesis so that type I error is preserved?
- 3. How to estimate the treatment effect in the final analysis?

Among these three issues, type I error control is probably the most researched topic in the statistical literature. The issue of determining the new sample is closely related to controlling type error (just like power and type I error are related when planning a fixed-size trial) but it has some "degree-of-freedom." Most type I error control methodologies focus on defining an end-of-study rule for accepting or rejecting the null hypothesis following the unbiased SSR, but they leave some freedom as to how the new sample size is actually selected. This is understandable sample size decisions are often heavily influenced by non-statistical considerations such as budget, regulatory, and market requirements. Because of the close relationship between issues #1 and #2 for some methods, we will discuss SSR methods in conjunction with type I error control, on a method-by-method basis.

Concerns over the final study analysis (estimation and confidence intervals) are not discussed here because the related literature is generally less mature and is still an active area of research for some methods. Nonetheless, estimation deserves attention because computing classic-point estimates and associated confidence intervals following a trial with unbiased SSR may lead to biased results. Even if the final study inference is well defined and preserves the type I error rate, the methodology to compute point estimates and associated confidence intervals still needs to be addressed. At a minimum, simulations should be performed to quantify the magnitude of bias (often very small). For some methods, the theoretical framework addressing estimation at end of trial is available [19-20].
