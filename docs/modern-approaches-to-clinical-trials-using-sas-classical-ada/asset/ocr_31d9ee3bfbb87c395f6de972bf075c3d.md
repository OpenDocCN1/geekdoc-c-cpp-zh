Based on Operating Characteristics of Classical and Group Sequential Design, we can see that 0.19 is a clinically meaningful difference, then the classical design based on optimistic $\delta=0.25$ can be a risky option and can fail to achieve statistical significance, while the classical design based on pessimistic $\delta=0.19$ will need a total sample size of 870. The added flexibility with a GSD does help by decreasing the sample size marginally and ensuring a 36% probability of stopping the trial for efficacy the true $\delta=0.25$. However, the gain with a GSD is not as much compared to redesigning a classical design, especially if the true $\delta$ is around 0.19.

# Adaptive Design with Unblinded SSR in the Promising Zone

In this design there is an opportunity to start small and invest adaptively if results towards the promising zone. In our specific example, both classical designs do not provide any flexibility while the GSD, as discussed earlier, suffers from a large initial commitment. Further, as seen in Operating Characteristics of Classical and Group Sequential Design, the GSD does not provide much additional benefit as compared to the classical design with the pessimistic $\delta$. We now explore an unblinded SSR using the promising zone approach. The adaptive design gives us the opportunity to start with the initial sample size of 504 subjects in the optimistic scenario of the classical design and adapt at interim analysis.

We define the promising zone when the conditional power is between 30% and 80%. We take an interim look when 252 subjects reach the primary endpoint and stop the trial early if the results are compelling or the sample size would be re-estimated if the interim results fall into the promising zone or continue the trial if the results are favorable (conditional power $\ge 80\%$) or unfavorable (conditional power < 30%).
