## 9.4 Covariate-Adaptive Randomization

## 9.4.1 Stratified Randomization

Prior to randomization, there are some baseline prognostic factors (covariates) collected, such as age, gender, and disease severity, which may have an important impact on the treatment outcomes. It is thus desirable to balance treatment allocation over these covariates so that valid and efficient treatment comparisons can be conducted.

The simplest way to balance the treatment assignment over important covariates is through stratification. As shown in Figure 9.1 Illustration of Stratified Randomization, trial participants are first grouped into mutually exclusive strata based on the values of their prognostic variables, and then they are randomly allocated to each treatment within each stratum.

Figure 9.1 Illustration of Stratified Randomization

![](img/f4ec2665c3484c528370923aeb5cb99f_434_0.png)

If the number of strata is small, stratified randomization can strike a balance with respect to various combinations of the covariates. However, if the number of strata is relatively large in comparison to the number of subjects, stratification can be meaningless. Because the number of strata increases multiplicatively with the number of stratification factors, some strata may become very sparse or even empty. This, in turn, may yield a considerable imbalance among treatment arms [5].

## 9.4.2 Covariate-Adaptive Randomization by Minimization

When there are a large number of prognostic factors in a trial, it becomes difficult to achieve overall balance across all of the covariates. Taves [6] and Pocock and Simon [7] proposed the minimization method (also known as dynamic allocation, covariate-adaptive, or adjusted randomization) for sequential treatment assignment. During the randomization process, the allocation probability of each subject is determined by the current overall balance among the groups.

In detail, let $l$ denote the total number of prognostic factors, among which continuous factors need to be categorized into meaningful ordinal levels. In a two-arm trial, let $n_{i1}$ be the number of subjects that are assigned to arm 1 and $n_{i2}$ assigned to arm 2, then the difference for $i$ is given by

$g_i = |n_{i1} - n_{i2}|, i=1, \dots$.

The total imbalance is measured by a weighted sum over the marginal imbalance for each factor $G = \sum_{i=1}^l w_i g_i$, where $w_i$ is a weight characterizing the importance of the $i$th factor. Based on previous allocations of patients, each new patient is more likely to be assigned to the treatment arm that would lead to the least imbalance. For illustration, we present an example to demonstrate how to achieve balance on two prognostic factors: sex and tumor stage. Assuming equal weights $w1=w2=1$ for sex and tumor stage, suppose 40 subjects have been enrolled and the next subject entering the trial is a male patient with stage I. If this new participant were assigned to arm B, the total imbalance between the two treatment groups would be minimized, in contrast to being assigned to arm A. Therefore, this subject will be assigned to arm B with a larger probability, say 0.75, in order to reduce the imbalance.

> % MinimizationRandomize macro performs the minimization method to sequentially allocate the newly recruited subjects. To invoke the macro, users need to specify four input parameters:

- 1. TreatedPatients is the data set that contains the information of the patients who have been assigned treatments. The first column in the data set indicates the treatment for each patient, and the remaining columns correspond to prognostic factors, whose values should be discrete integers starting from 1.
- 2. FactorWeight is the data set that specifies the weights for prognostic factors.
- 3. NewPatient is the data set that stores the covariate information of the new patient.
- 4. p is a large probability ($p > 0.5$) which is specified to reduce the imbalance of treatment assignment.
