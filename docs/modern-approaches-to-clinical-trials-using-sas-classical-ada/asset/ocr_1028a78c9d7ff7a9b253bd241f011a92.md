Whether adjustment for multiple comparisons is needed or not, the understanding of "a dose that delivers treatment effect is different from placebo" could still vary a lot, depending on an individual's point of view. For example, let the useful effect defined above be denoted as $\zeta$, then for a given dose, a point estimate and a confidence interval can be constructed for $\zeta$. Suppose a positive value indicates a benefit effect, then the treatment response of the given dose of the test product subtracts the placebo response, which is the point estimate of the treatment difference could be as much as $\zeta$. This leaves a wide range of interpretations – for leaves a wide range of interpretations – for MinED, should that dose be such that the point estimate is greater than $\zeta$? Should that dose be such that the lower confidence limit to be greater than 0? Should that dose be such that the upper confidence limit is greater than $\zeta$?

The implementation of this concept of the "smallest dose with a discernible useful effect" can be very difficult, depending on the various interpretations of this definition. However, in practice, one feasible way of finding the MinED in a dose-ranging clinical trial is to find a dose that is lower than MinED. Regardless of which practical definition the project team uses to identify the MinED, there is a dose deemed to be not efficacious, and there is a higher dose that is efficacious, then it can be considered that the non-efficacious dose is below the MinED, and the efficacious dose is above the MinED. From a practical point of view, this information would usually be sufficient to help the team to design phase III clinical trials.

Of course in the selection of phase III doses, many other aspects will also have to be included in consideration; e.g., safety or toxicity findings, pharmacokinetic properties, formulations, etc. The information about MinED would be one of the most critical deliverables from phase II in supporting any phase III development plans.
