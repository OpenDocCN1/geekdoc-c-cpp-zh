In practice, a trial with 4 to 5 test doses, plus a placebo control will deliver a good understanding of where the test medication is most active, if the dose range is wide enough, and if the dose spacing is reasonable. Some simulation studies suggest that among the number of treatment arms (4, 5, 6, or 7, placebo arm included), three test doses (the 4-arm design) could be insufficient in some cases because it significantly limits the space of potentially relevant models to fit to the data with clear potential impact on the ability to identify the optimal dose [16]. The performance increases when more than three doses are studied. On the other hand, six test doses (the 7-arm design) may not necessarily deliver better results than the four or five test doses (the 5-arm or 6-arm design) comparisons. When the total sample size is fixed, the 7-arm design offers a smaller sample size per group when an equal total sample size is used, and the precision would be sacrificed. It may also be a good idea to consider an unbalanced sample-size allocation, e.g., allocate more subjects to the extreme doses (i.e., higher dose(s) and a placebo arm).

Consequently, a dose ranging design including four to five test doses (in addition to a placebo) and covering a wide dose range provides a very useful and pragmatic basis in designing the dose ranging clinical trial [20].
