# ### 3.1.2 Overview of Considerations and Issues

When considering a trial design involving SSR, one has to be aware of potential issues (statistical, regulatory, and implementation) that are associated with such designs. The specifics of these issues differ quite a bit between blinded and unblinded SSR procedures; therefore, we will discuss them in detail within their respective sections. In general, the core statistical issue for all methods is type error control from multiple looks (interim and final) at the same data. The unblinded SSR procedure is more prone to type error inflation than blinded SSR. Regulatory issues revolve primarily around classification of adaptive designs by FDA guidance [1] into “well-understood” (blinded SSR) and “less well-understood” (unblinded SSR). This classification is based on the maturity of the methodology and consequent regulatory experience with submissions involving each type of SSR. This classification is also closely related to implementation challenges: with unblinded SSR, there is always the risk of an accidental interim result leak or treatment effect reverse-engineering that results in compromised study integrity. Reverse-engineering refers to the ability of someone familiar with the interim decision algorithm to make guesses about the observed treatment effect based on the interim decision that was made. The blinded SSR procedure is less affected by such concerns. Because unblinded SSR methods are still considered “less well-understood”, additional planning time for regulatory consultation should also be factored-in when considering such studies.

Other than budgetary considerations discussed in [3], Chuang-Stein et al. [4] describe several scientific reasons why SSR may be desirable. These include (1) lack of information on how a new product candidate may affect a long-term clinical endpoint in the target population; (2) a newly approved product have become part of a treatment regimen to which a product candidate under development will be added; (3) emerging new medical or diagnostic practice that renders information from historical trials out of date; (4) newly issued regulatory guidance that departs from previous endpoint selection and statistical approaches.
