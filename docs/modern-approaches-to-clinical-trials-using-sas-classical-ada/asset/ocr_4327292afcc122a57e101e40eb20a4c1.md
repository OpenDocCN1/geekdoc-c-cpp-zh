With exception of the promising zone design, for most methods with a normal endpoint treatment effect estimation is usually accomplished by relying on the duality between Cls and a hypothesis test. Barnath and coauthors [19] extended the repeated confidence interval approach of [20] to flexible designs.

The promising zone design stands out from the other methods because it specifies the condition under which it can be used and does not require users to modify the test statistic or rejection region in the final analysis. The latter has an appealing property of simplicity. For example, one can use the standard Wald test statistic for final inference. All associated decisions that one has to make are “organically built into the promising-zone design,” including the new sample size selection rule and early utility/efficacy stop boundaries [3]. We would generally recommend the promising zone design, but with one word of caution. Because of the very feature of simplicity mentioned above, the duality property between hypothesis testing and confidence intervals is lost and treatment effect estimation at the end of such trials remains an open problem. For trials implementing the promising zone design, we suggest using simulation to carefully assess the amount of bias in treatment effect estimates at the end of trial on a case-by-case basis.

Finally, we want to point out that in the unblinded SSR methodology literature, $r_n, N_N$ and $M$ often represent the total number of patients at the interim analysis, the original planned total sample size, and the revised total sample size, respectively. To be consistent with the notations used in this chapter, we used these symbols to note their counterparts per group in this section. Because we assume two treatment groups with equal allocation in this chapter, the unblinded SSR methods reported in the literature apply equally with the use of our notations even though the two sets of numbers differ by a factor of two.
