As discussed briefly in 2.1 Introduction, the choice of boundary calculation method, number of stages, inclusion of futility analysis, etc., should take into account the objectives of the trial, the chosen primary and/or secondary endpoints, the initial assumptions of the effect size, subject recruitment, and others. If the effect size, although uncertain, is expected to be large, a boundary set that favors stopping early can be used, such as the Pocock boundary. If monitoring safety is important, then stopping too early may not be desirable and in such case, fewer stages or boundary options that discourage early stopping, such as the OBF method, can be used. Increasing the number of stages will certainly increase administrative costs and the chances that bias can creep into the trial. Sequential trials with too many stages are therefore not encouraged. When flexibility is needed, the $\alpha$ spending approach allows obvious comparison of the operating characteristics by simply adjusting the parameter of the spending functions. This is often useful when advocating and explaining a proposed group sequential method to the clinical team.
