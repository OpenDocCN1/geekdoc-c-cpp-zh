where $\gamma\in[0,1]$ is a tuning parameter ($\gamma=0$ corresponds to 1:1 randomization and $\gamma=1$ is the original Thompson's procedure [27]). Based on empirical evidence, Thall and Waten recommended using in practice $\gamma=1/2$ or $\gamma=n/(2N)$ (where $n$ is the current sample size and $N$ is the maximum allowable sample size) [15]. An application of this approach in a soft tissue sarcoma trial is described in [28]. Some authors described applications of Bayesian adaptive randomization in multi-arm randomized clinical trials in oncology where it was imperative to maximize individual patients' benefit from participating in the trial [29-31]. Korn and Freilin in [32] argued that ethics due to the number of non-responders (in the trial) are typically very modest compared to a fixed-schedule 1:1 or 2:1 randomization, and even these benefits can be outweighed by other complexities inherent to adaptive randomization (e.g. outcomes must be observed fast, patient inclusion rate must be low, and data must be periodically updated to modify treatment probabilities). In a follow-up note, Berry in [33] acknowledged that ethical gains of Bayesian adaptive randomization are indeed somewhat limited in two-arm trials, but they can be much more pronounced in multi-objective, multi-arm trials. The potential gains of RAR should be assessed not only in terms of ethics (e. reduction in the number of non-responders), but also in terms of statistical efficiency criteria (e.g. enhanced precision or increased power of statistical tests). In particular, phase II dose-ranging trials evaluating different experimental therapeutics with the common control arm provide a nice setting where RAR can provide a dual benefit of placing more patients on better-performing treatment arms and an increasing amount of statistical information for the chosen experimental objectives [21,31].
