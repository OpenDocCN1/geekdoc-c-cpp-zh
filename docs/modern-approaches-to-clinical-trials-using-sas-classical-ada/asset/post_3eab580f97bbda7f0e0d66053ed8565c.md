
$SCORE < 1/2$ for $p_E < p_C$, which implies that a greater proportion of patients are assigned to the more successful treatment.

Baldi Antognini and Giagnoli [40] considered the following compound criterion combining ethical and inference objectives:

$$\Phi_{\omega}(\rho) = \omega\frac{\Psi_E(\rho)}{\Psi_E^*} + (1-\omega)\frac{\Psi_C(\rho)}{\Psi_C^*}$$

Here $\omega(0,1)$ is a user-defined weight, $\Psi_E(\rho) = q_E\rho + q_C(1-\rho)$ is the expected proportion of treatment failures, $\Psi_E = \min(q_E, q_C)$.

$\Phi_I(\rho) = -p_E\rho + p_C(1-\rho)$ is the variance of the estimated treatment difference, and $\rho_I^* = \sqrt{\Psi_E p_E^* + \Psi_C p_C^*}$ is the minimum value of $\Phi_I(\rho)$ for $\rho \in(0,1)$. The motivation for using standardized criteria of ethics and inference is to bring them to a comparable scale. The optimal allocation $\rho_\omega^*$ minimizing $\Phi_\omega(\rho)$ is the unique solution in $(0,1)$ of the equation

$$\omega\frac{\rho_{PE}-\rho_{PC}}{\min(q_E, q_C)} + 1^2 = \frac{\{\frac{\Psi_{QC}-1}{\Psi_{QE}}\rho_{\omega}^*\}^2+\rho_{\omega}^*-1}{\{\rho_{\omega}^*(1-\rho_{\omega}^*)\}^2} (10.4)$$

Baldi Antognini and Giagnoli [40] explored different choices of the weight. In our presentation, we set $\omega=1/2$ (equal interest in ethics and inference), and this allocation rule will be referred to as **compound optimal allocation**. There are other useful optimal allocation rules for two-arm binary outcome trials which we omit here for a limited space. The interested reader is referred to [41-44].

## 10.3 Response-Adaptive Randomization for Implementing Allocation

### 10.3.1 Preliminaries

Optimal allocation rules discussed in Section 10.2 depend on $p_E$ and $p_C$. For the time being, let us assume that $p_E$ and $p_C$ are known. Then, if we want to implement, say RSIHR (2), we would have to split $n$ into two groups:

$$n_{E} = n \cdot \frac{p_E}{p_E+p_C}$$

However, there are several issues with this approach. First, it is infeasible to have all $n$ patients at once. Second, in practice $p_E$ and $p_C$ are unknown. Third, we cannot simply split $n$ into two groups. The idea of RAR is to update the allocation proportions as the trial progresses. We now describe the basic setup of RAR. It starts with $m_0$ patients randomized between $E$ and $C$ using some initial rule, such as 1:1 allocation. After $m_0$ patients, we estimate $p_E$ and $p_C$ and use these estimates to determine the randomization probability for the next batch of patients.
