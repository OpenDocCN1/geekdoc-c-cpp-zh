
## 3.3.4.2 Promising Zone Example

Let us consider a hypothetical two-arm trial with equal allocation to be designed to test the efficacy of an experimental new treatment compared to the control which is also the standard of care. The primary endpoint is a continuous endpoint with high scores indicating improvement from baseline. We denote $\mu_t$ as the difference between the mean change from baseline for the treatment and $\mu_c$ as the difference between the mean change from baseline for the control arm. The treatment effect, i.e., the gain in efficacy between treatments is denoted by $\delta = \mu_t - \mu_c$.

The null hypothesis $H_0: \delta = 0$ which is tested against the one-sided alternative hypothesis $H_1: \delta > 0$. Based on previous studies, the new treatment is expected to have a $\delta$ of at least 0.25 units with between-subject standard deviation to be around 1. Though the treatment difference is expected to be 0.25 units, a difference of 0.19 units and above is considered clinically and commercially meaningful. Hence, the sponsor would like to have a back plan to cover for uncertainty around the actual treatment difference to ensure that the trial can be appropriately powered in case the alternative hypothesis is less expected but still in the range of clinical and commercial relevance.

While designing this study, we will consider few design options, including a classical design, i.e., fixed sample size GSD, and an adaptive design with SSR. However, there are pros and cons for each of the design options; however, we specifically explore scenarios under which adaptive design with SSR would be beneficial.

## Classical Design

It is expected that the treatment difference $\delta$ of at least 0.25 units with between-subject standard deviation ($\sigma$) around 1. For a classical design with only a single look, a total of 504 subjects (252 subjects per arm) are needed to achieve 80% power to detect a difference of 0.25 at a one-sided $\alpha = 0.025$. If the treatment difference is indeed as expected, then classical design with 252 subjects per arm will have at least 80% power to detect the true difference. If the treatment difference is lower than 0.25, then this trial would be underpowered with the power drop as low as 57% if the true treatment difference is 0.19. There are several ways to deal with this situation. For example, the sponsor could decide to increase the initial sample size to total of 870 subjects (435 per arm) so that the trial is adequately powered at all values with at least 0.19. Operating Characteristics of Classical and Group Sequential Design shows the sample size and the power in this scenario. We can see that in this case, the trial is overpowered if the actual treatment difference is 0.25. The option of powering with 0.19 would be the preferred and default option if the finances and resources for this project are not a constraint. However, in this example we assume that the sponsor does not have the flexibility to make such a large (i.e. total sample size of 870) commitment upfront. However, if the results are in the clinically and commercial relevance region, then the sponsor would be willing to invest more resources. Given this set up, this scenario blends into more flexible designs with more than one.

## Group Sequential Design

We will consider a GSD with one interim look to detect a treatment difference of 0.19 with a possibility of stopping early if the interim treatment difference is more than 0.25. As mentioned before, GSD can be a very appealing option if the savings in the potential study duration are adequately balanced with the savings in the sample size. In our hypothetical example, we conduct an interim analysis when approximately 50% of the subjects have observed the primary endpoint. For this design, we would assume that the sponsor is interested in stopping for efficacy and does not want to stop the trial for futility. Based on this assumption, we will choose the error spending function to evaluate at the interim look. In most cases, the type I error is spent very conservatively in the early look so that a decision of efficacy stop is triggered if only the results are compelling towards the new treatment. Hence, we will use an error spending function that mimics the O’Brien-Fleling boundary. Example Code 3.3 Sequential Design with Efficacy Boundaries shows how this can be done in SAS using method (alpha)=ERRFUNC OF Using method (alpha)=ERRFUNGAMMA(GAMMA=-4) in proc seqdesign [32].

Example Code 3.3 Sequential Design with Efficacy Boundaries

```
proc seqdesign altref=0.19 errspend pass(cref=0.5 0.5 1)
stoprob(cref=0.5 0.5)
plots=(asn power errspend)
boundscale=std2;
OneSidedErrorSpending: design nstages=2

```
method(alpha)=ERRFUNCGAMMA (GAMMA=-4)
 alt-upper stop=reject
alpha=0.025 beta=0.2 info=cum(1);
samplesize model=twosomemean(stddev=1 weight=1);
```

Again we emphasize that this is a hypothetical example, the sample size is presented based on completers. However, sample size savings consider overruns. Here, we assume no overruns. GSD saves expected sample size; however, for populations and indications that the study enrolls rapidly or if the endpoint is observed after a delay, then the potential benefit is severely diminished.

## Adaptive Design with Unblinded SSR in the Promising Zone

In this design there is an opportunity to start small and invest adaptively if results towards the promising zone. In our specific example, both classical designs do not provide any flexibility while the GSD, as discussed earlier, suffers from a large initial commitment. Further, as seen in Operating Characteristics of Classical and Group Sequential Design, the GSD does not provide much additional benefit as compared to the classical design with the pessimistic delta. We now explore an unblinded SSR using the promising zone approach.

We define the promising zone when the conditional power is between 30% and 80%. We take an interim look when 252 subjects reach the primary endpoint and stop the trial early if the results are compelling or the sample size would be re-estimated if the interim results fall into the promising zone or continue the trial if the results are favorable (conditional power $\ge 80\%$) or unfavorable (conditional power < 30%). This approach of data-dependent decision making based on conditional power follows the method that is outlined in [3].
