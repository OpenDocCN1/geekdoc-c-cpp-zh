Other ways of subdividing are also possible. Instead of splitting the initial interval $[0, T/2]$ and $[T/2, T]$, we may use $1/p$ cut — $[0, T/p]$ and $(T/p, T]$ — where $p \ge 2$ and then repeat the procedure described above. The BDS design is nonparametric because it does not depend on any underlying models other than the two assumptions: knowledge of MTD and a non-decreasing dose-response relationship. It provides a way to identify doses that are below the MTD and a means to potentially estimate the MinED.

In addition to BDS, other algorithms could be useful, e.g., log-dose spacing, the Fibonacci series, and the modified Fibonacci series approach can also be considered $[17,18]$. However, it is important to note that most of these methods propose dose spacing from lower doses to higher doses. In practice, dose spacing should be considered starting with the MTD, and move down to lower doses. Hence, when applying any of these algorithms, the dose allocation would be reversed (from high to low, instead of low to high).
