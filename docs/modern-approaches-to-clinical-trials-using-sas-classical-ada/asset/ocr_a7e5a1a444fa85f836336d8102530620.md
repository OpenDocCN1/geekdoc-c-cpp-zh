# 2.1 Introduction

One of the major advances in modern clinical trials is the invention and application of sequential analysis in experimental design. This type of design is often called the group sequential design. A group sequential design consists of multiple stages and employs a repeated significance testing strategy. It is one of the most commonly used adaptive designs. The Encyclopaedic Companion to Medical Statistics [1] defines sequential analysis as “a method allowing hypothesis tests to be conducted on a number of occasions as data accumulate through the course of a trial. A trial monitored in this is usually called a sequential trial.” For example, in a phase III randomized clinical trial that has several stages; an interim analysis is planned at each stage. An interim analysis is intended to assess the treatment effect with respect to efficacy or safety based on accumulated data at a planned time prior to the completion of a clinical trial. Based on results from the interim analysis, such as a given test statistic is compared to a critical boundary, a decision can be made either to stop to reject the null hypothesis of no treatment effect, to accept the null hypothesis, or to continue on the next stage.

There are three different types of group sequential designs: (1) early efficacy stopping design permitting only early claiming of efficacy, (2) early futility stopping design permitting only claiming of futility, and (3) early efficacy or utility stopping design permitting either an efficacy or futility claim. Based on prior knowledge, if we believe that the test treatment is promising, an early efficacy stopping design should be used. If we are concerned that the test treatment may not work, an early futility stopping design should be employed.

In clinical studies, there is great interest in sequential procedures for ethical, economic, and administrative reasons and the methodology has a role to play in all phases of clinical research. Therefore, this type of trial design is attractive to clinical scientists and researchers because it provides a method to add flexibility to the analysis of ongoing trial data. The most compelling reason for monitoring trial data is, ethically, it is desirable to terminate or modify a trial when evidence has emerged concerning the particular hypothesis of interest.
