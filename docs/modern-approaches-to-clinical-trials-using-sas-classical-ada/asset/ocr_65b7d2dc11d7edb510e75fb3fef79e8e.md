# 6.2.2 Finding the Effective Dose

ICH E4 defines the minimum effective dose (MinED) as the "smallest dose with a discernible useful effect" [4]. Finding the MinED is an important step in drug development. To illustrate, let's consider the following reported example. In a clinical development program for a drug to treat osteoarthritis [7], the first dose-response study included a placebo, 80 mg, 120 mg, and 160 mg of the test drug. Results from this study indicate that all three doses are efficacious (the left panel of Figure 6.2 Dose-Response Results from the Three Studies of Osteoarthritis Drug). These results also show that 80 mg may have already been at the high side of the dose-response curve. A second study was designed to explore the 40 mg dose, and results from this second study are given in the middle panel of Figure 6.2. With these findings, the project team phase III studies using a dose of 120 mg to confirm the long-term efficacy of this drug.

An end-of-phase II meeting with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) was commented that the minimum effective dose (MinED) was not found. The main issue here is that the efficacy response does not change as the dose level changes and all the three testing doses almost equally reach the full efficacy, so the dose-response relationship was not detected. The project team designed a third dose-response study to explore the lower dose range. Doses included in the third are 2.5 mg, 10 mg, and 40 mg. As shown in the right panel of Figure 6.2 Dose-Response Results from the Three Studies of Osteoarthritis Drug, the third study successfully established the dose-response relationship, i.e., the response increases as dose increases. Based on these results, it is clear that the phase III studies are designed with a dose being too high. The impact of this process can cause a major delay in the development program and considerable waste of resources.
