
However, from a study design point of view, sample size may also be calculated using a PoC hypothesis. Again, this PoC can be based only on the highest dose against a placebo, or based on a contrast that includes more than just two treatment groups. Of course, if the interest is to write a contrast that reflects the underlying model, more variety of contrasts can be considered.

## 6.2.7.2 Selecting a Treatment Difference from a Placebo

The concept of identifying the MCID is necessary in designing dose-ranging clinical trials. Although, in practice, it is sometimes difficult to objectively establish a widely accepted MCID, it is critical to use a treatment difference ($\delta$) as the target when calculating sample sizes for a clinical trial. In other words, without $\delta$, sample size cannot be calculated. Hence, if there is a MCID for a given indication, it will be very helpful in clinical development of new products.

Typically in phase III clinical trials, the treatment differences used for trial design are mainly based on those observed from earlier studies from this product. But in early phase II, such $\delta$ is not easily obtained. In general, there are three ways in practice to obtain a $\delta$ for designing an early phase II clinical trial. One popular way is to use data from other products. Especially when there are products already approved for general patient population use. In the US, after a product is approved, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) publishes a summary basis for approval (SBA) for the product. In Europe, the European Medicinal Agency (EMA) publishes European Public Assessment Report (EPAR) to explain why a product is approved for public use. Typically, the treatment difference can be observed from either SBA or EPAR. In some situations, information on treatment difference of other products can be found from publications, product labels or a registries website, such as clinicaltrials.gov. Therefore, by using observed treatment differences from other products, a $\delta$ could be selected to help the design of this new study.

A second way of selecting a treatment difference is by evaluating earlier data observed from the products under development. Project team members use pre-clinical data, PK data, biomarkers, or other data obtained from this product and propose a reasonable $\delta$ such that it is likely the product candidate will be able to deliver. Usually, a $\delta$ obtained either using other products (based on publications, labels, SBA or EPAR) from the product itself, there could be long discussions among team members. The main reason is that in the early development phase, there is no direct efficacy data to support a clinical benefit of this candidate.

A way to choose $\delta$ is to identify a value such that the sample size calculated using this $\delta$ is feasible. Typically, in the early phase of clinical development, the team does not have very strong confidence that the test product will be efficacious and safe. Hence, the investment, being a high risk, tends to be limited. The total number of patients to recruit would have to not exceed a certain number. On this basis, a $\delta$ can be inversely calculated using the number of patients and the pre-specified type I $(\alpha)$ and type II $(\beta)$ errors. The mathematical formula for sample size calculation that the number of patients needed for the study is inversely related to the pre-specified $\delta$. If the budget is limited, and hence the sample size is small, that a large $\delta$ will be necessary to have a reasonable likelihood to detect a treatment effect. The author understands that this may be happening in practice, but does not recommend this approach for informative decision making. When designing such early efficacy trial with a feasible sample size (which is not very large, indicating a greater $\delta$ is used or a higher $\alpha$ or a higher $\beta$ in sample size calculation), the most important thing is to set-up Go/NoGo decision criteria prior to data access and data analysis, and understanding the risks that are associated to it (i.e., to stop a promising drug and risk to carry forward an ineffective drug).

## 6.2.7.3 Choice of Type I and Type II Errors

In clinical development of new products, it is well known that $\alpha$ is the probability of making a type I error; i.e., $\beta$ is the probability of developing a placebo of stopping development of a potentially good product. In most phase III development programs, Authorities generally expect the $\alpha$ to be set at a two-sided 0.05 (or one-sided at 0.025). In phase III, $\alpha$ can be thought of as a regulatory risk because it represents the risk of approving a product without sufficient efficacy for the targeted patient population, but could cause adverse events or other safety concerns. On the other hand, $\beta$ can be thought of as the sponsor’s risk because if a potentially good product stopped for further development due to a wrong decision, then the sponsor's investment on this candidate would have been wasted. For this reason, regulatory agencies do not strictly require any particular $\beta$ to be used in any phase of the clinical studies. Hence, the choice of $\beta$ is usually left for the sponsor to determine. Usually in phase III, sponsors tend to choose a smaller $\beta$ (or a higher power) for study designs so that a larger sample size will increase the likelihood of meeting the decision criteria with more accurate estimates (i.e., narrower confidence interval around the estimates).

However, the choice of $\alpha$ or $\beta$ in phase II designs can be flexible. The two main reasons are: (i) decisions after a phase II clinical trial are primarily a sponsor decision rather than a regulatory decision and, hence, the sponsor gets to select the level of risks that they are willing to take; (ii) there is insufficient evidence at phase II for the sponsor to commit a huge amount of investment. Accordingly, the sponsor might accept higher risks (i.e., the $\alpha$ or the $\beta$).
