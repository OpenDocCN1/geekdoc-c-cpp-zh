
In Sections 10.3.2, 10.3.3, and 10.3.4 we discuss several approaches to facilitate optimal RAR procedures. All these procedures possess the following important asymptotic properties:

- 1. The maximum likelihood estimators $\hat{\varrho}_{Em}$ and $\hat{\varrho}_{Cm}$ in (10.6) are strongly consistent for $\hat{\rho}_E$ and $\hat{\rho}_C$; they are asymptotically uncorrelated and have an asymptotically bivariate normal distribution.
- 2. The sample treatment allocation proportion $N_E(m)/m$ is strongly consistent for the target allocation $text{} \rho$ and has an asymptotically normal distribution with a known structure for the asymptotic variance.

Therefore, standard asymptotic estimators and tests can be used for statistical inference following optimal RAR procedures [35,36].

## 10.3.2 Sequential Maximum Likelihood Estimation Design

With the sequential maximum likelihood estimation (SMLE) design, treatment randomization probabilities are set equal to the current estimates of the target allocation proportions [22,46]. In particular, Rosenberger and co-authors in [10] proposed the following SMLE procedure targeting RSIHR allocation (10.2):

$$\phi_{m+l} = \frac{\sqrt{\hat{p}_{Em}}}{\sqrt{\hat{p}_{Em}} + \sqrt{\hat{p}_{Cm}}}} \quad (10.7)$$

They proved strong consistency and asymptotic normality of both estimators and the sample allocation proportions with the procedure (10.7) and showed that the Wald test
$$Z_W = (\hat{p}_{Em} - \hat{p}_{Cm})\left(\frac{\hat{p}_{Em}}{N_{Em}(m)} + \frac{\hat{p}_{Cm}}{N_{Cm}(m)}\right)^{-1/2}$$ is asymptotically standard normal under $H_0: p_E = p_C$. They also showed via simulation that the procedure (10.7) can lead to a modest reduction in treatment failures with minimal or no loss in power compared to equal randomization designs. A limitation of the SMLE design is that it can be quite variable, which can have a negative impact on power. More elaborate RAR procedures are described in Sections 10.3.3 and 10.3.4.

## 10.3.3 Doubly Adaptive Biased Coin Design

The Doubly Adaptive Biased Coin Design (DBCD) is a class of RAR procedures that can target any allocation proportion $\rho$ provided that some mild regularity conditions are met [47,48]. The key ingredient of DBCD is the allocation function $g(x, \rho)$, defined on $[0,1] \times [0,1]$. Hu and Zhang [48] proposed the following allocation function:

$$g(x, \rho) = \begin{cases} \frac{\rho^\gamma}{x^\gamma} & 0 < x < 1 \\ \frac{(1-\rho)^\gamma}{(1-x)^\gamma}}{1-x} & x=0,1. \end{cases} \quad (10.8)$$

The user-defined parameter $\gamma \geq 0$ controls the degree of randomness ($\gamma=0$ is most random and $\gamma \to \infty$ is almost a deterministic procedure). The role of $\gamma$ will become clear momentarily.

Suppose that the target allocation has been chosen as $\rho = \rho(p_E, p_C)$. After $m$ patients, the target allocation is estimated as $\hat{\rho}_m = \rho(\hat{p}_E, \hat{p}_C)$. The DBCD assigns the $m+1$st patient to $E$ with probability

$$\phi_{m+1} = g\left(\frac{N_E(m)}{m}, \hat{\rho}_m\right)$$

where $g(x, \rho)$ is as (10.8). If $\gamma=0$, the DBCD is equivalent to the SMLE design. Hu and Zhang [48] proved that under the condition $\rho = \rho(p_E, p_C)$, the allocation is continuous in its arguments and the sample proportions and estimators are strongly consistent and asymptotically normal. Specifically, the variance of $N_E(m)/m$ is

$$\sigma_{\gamma}^2 = \frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{(1+\gamma)^2} + \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p_E}\right)^2 \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p_C}\right)^2$$

A small value of $\sigma_{\gamma}^2$ is desirable from the standpoint of power. When $\gamma=0$, $\sigma_{\gamma}^2$ is largest. When $\gamma \to \infty$, $\sigma_{\gamma}^2$ is smallest, and the procedure is almost deterministic. Rosenberger and Hu [35] recommended using $\gamma=2$ for a tradeoff between randomness and power.

## 10.3.4 Efficient-Adaptive Design

Hu and co-authors in [50] considered the following question: Can an adaptive design be found that preserves randomization, can target any allocation and has minimal variability? They proposed a class of efficient-adaptive designs (ERADE) for which the $m+1$st patient is randomized to $E$ with

$$\phi_{m+1} = \frac{\sqrt{\hat{p}_{Em}}}{\sqrt{\hat{p}_{Em}} + \sqrt{\hat{p}_{Cm}}}$$

where $0 \leq \zeta \leq 1$ is a user-defined parameter that controls randomness. The theoretical properties of ERADE are similar to those of DBCD; however, a fundamental difference is that ERADE is discrete, whereas DBCD is continuous. In fact, ERADE is better than DBCD as $\zeta \to 1$. The variance of $N_E(m)/m$ is

$$\sigma_{\zeta}^2 = \frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{(1+\zeta)^2} + \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p_E}\right)^2 \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p_C}\right)^2$$

which is the lower bound for RAR procedures.

## 10.3.5 Theoretical Comparison of RAR Designs

Hu and Rosenberger in [34] derived a relationship between optimality, variability and power that can be used to facilitate a comparison among RAR designs. They considered a second-order Taylor expansion of the non-centrality parameter of the chi-square test following RAR and showed that three important factors impacting the power of the test are the allocation target, the speed of convergence to the target, and the variability of the sample allocation. For RAR procedures with the same allocation target, only variability can be compared. A procedure with small variability is desirable. Hu and co-authors in [51] defined an asymptotically best RAR procedure as one with the smallest possible variance of $N_E(m)/m$.

**Theorem 1** Consider a two-arm binary outcome trial using a RAR procedure with target allocation $\rho=\rho(p_E, p_C)$. Suppose the following regular conditions hold:

- 1. $p_E, p_C \in (0,1)$
- 2. $N_E(m)/n \rightarrow \rho$ almost surely
- 3. $\sqrt{N_E(m)/n-\rho} \rightarrow N(0,\sigma^2)$ in distribution, where $\sigma^2>0$.

Then we have $\sigma^2 \ge B(p_E,p_C)=\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p_E}\right)^2\frac{\rho p_E p_C}{\rho}+\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p_C}\right)^2\frac{\rho^2 p_C}{1-\rho}}$. $B(p_E,p_C)$ is called the lower bound for the variance of RAR procedures with allocation target $\rho$. A RAR procedure that attains the lower bound is called an asymptotically best RAR procedure.
