+   3. $\delta$ is the weighted sum of the effect based on all doses

+   4. $\sigma$ is the standard deviation

+   5. $f_i$ is the fraction of number of patients in treatment group

+   6. $c_j$ is the contrast coefficient that is associated with treatment group

+   7. $k$ is the number of dose groups, plus placebo, where placebo is denoted as the 0 dose group.

In the calculation of sample sizes for a dose-ranging study, two popular types of patient allocation are possible – balanced allocation where each treatment group receives an equal number of patients, or imbalanced allocation where the highest dose and the placebo groups receive more patients, and other dose groups receive fewer patients per group. The sample size formula above works well with either type of patient allocation. Examples of these types of allocation can be found in [20]. The above formula applies to cases where the primary endpoint is a continuous variable. In, a sample size formula based on other types of response variables such as binary or time-to-event, can also be found in [21].

In order to demonstrate how an early II dose-ranging trial is designed, a real-world example is discussed in the next section. This example reflects three key features of the thinking process: (1) dose-range; (2) number of treatment groups; and (3) sample size. Note that in the real-world setting, other considerations are also important – selection of primary endpoints, choice of clinically meaningful differences $(\gamma$), determination of type I and II error rates, as well as statistical hypothesis to be tested. However, all of these considerations are part of Step 3 – sample size calculation. Therefore, in dose-ranging designs, the dose-range and number of treatment groups are determined, the other design considerations are very similar to those for designing an ordinary clinical trial.
