# 7.1 Introduction

A well-known problem of failed phase III programs is poor dose selection resulting from inappropriate knowledge of the dose-response relationship (efficacy and safety) at the end of the learning phase of drug development, i.e., phase II. Selection of a dose (or doses) to carry into confirmatory phase III trials is the most difficult decisions in drug development. Although the exact numbers are unknown, it is believed that the high attrition rate plaguing the pharmaceutical industry in phase III studies are due, at least in part, to inadequate selection for confirming safety and efficacy in the intended population – doses that are too low to achieve adequate benefit, as well as doses that are too high and lead to dose-related safety events.

The basic difficulty in getting the right dose is the trade-off between wanted and un-wanted effects. A prerequisite for informed decision making and dose selection at the end of phase II is, hence, a solid characterization of the dose-response relationships. Historically, dose finding studies were designed and analyzed based on multiple pairwise comparisons of the active doses against a placebo. Such traditional methods are mostly performed within the analysis-of-variance hypothesis testing paradigm and known to be inefficient in the sense that they fail fully incorporate the available information across the doses [1]. Modeling approaches assume a functional relationship between response and dose, taken as a quantitative factor, according to a certain parametric model, such as the model. The fitted model is then used to describe the dose-response relationship and estimate target doses of interest. Such an approach will often lead to more precise estimates of the dose-response relationship, due to the interpolation of information across doses. In addition, it provides flexibility in investigating the effect of doses not used in the actual study. However, the validity of its conclusions will highly depend on an appropriate choice of the dose-response model, which in practice is often known.
