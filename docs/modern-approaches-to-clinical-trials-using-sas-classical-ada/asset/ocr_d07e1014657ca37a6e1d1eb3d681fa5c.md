In the actual trial, the RPWR stopped after 12 infants were randomized (one infant was randomized to the conventional therapy and died, and 11 infants were randomized to ECMO treatment and all survived). As such, the study failed because did not provide conclusive results about the relative merits of the two therapies. According to the modern theory of RAR, the failure of the ECMO trial can be explained by the trial's small sample size, the RPWR's high variability, and the dependence on the initial composition of the urn (initially the urn contained one ball of each type, and after several assignments the urn composition became extremely skewed in favor of the ECMO arm, leaving almost no chance for the conventional therapy to be chosen). Despite this, the ECMO trial is a good setting where RAR designs with robust statistical properties could have been used. One method that could have been beneficial in this setting is the drop-the-loser, an urn design with the same limiting allocation as the RPWR, but with much lower variability and higher speed of convergence to the target allocation [25]. Many other urn models with established statistical properties are available; refer to §10.5 of [22], Chapter 4 of [14], and [26] for further details.

# 10.1.3.2 Bayesian Adaptive Randomization

Bayesian adaptive randomization, originally proposed by Thompson in [27], is another heuristic approach to facilitate RAR. Let $p_E$ and $p_C$ denote the success probabilities for experimental (E) and control (C) treatments, respectively. Assume that $p_E$ and $p_C$ follow independent prior distributions, and the posteriors of $p_E$ and $p_C$ are updated sequentially by accumulating patient outcome data. The current eligible patient is randomized between treatments E and C with probabilities $\lambda_E = P(p_E > p_C|data)$ and $\lambda_C = 1 - \lambda_E$. In other words, the current patient is assigned with higher probability to the treatment that is viewed more successful given current data. Thall and Waten in [15] noted that such a randomization procedure, while holding ethical promise, can be highly variable due to the random nature of $\lambda_E, \lambda_C$. The authors suggested stabilizing the treatment allocation probabilities as:

```
$$\lambda_E = \frac{\{Pr(p_E > p_C|data)\}}{\{Pr(p_E > p_C|data)\}+\{Pr(p_E < p_C|data)\}} \text{ and } \lambda_C = 1 - \lambda_E,$
```
