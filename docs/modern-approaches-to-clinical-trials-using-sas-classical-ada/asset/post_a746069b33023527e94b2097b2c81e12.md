The BAR enrichment design can be naturally extended to multiple treatments and multiple markers. For a patient with a baseline characteristic following into marker group $j$, assuming the posterior estimate of effect size in treatment $i$ is $\delta_{ij}$, the randomization ratio to treatment $i$ can be expressed in the form of $\lambda_i / \sum \lambda_j$, where $\lambda$ is the tuning parameter. Note that when $\lambda=0$, it corresponds to equal randomization. When it becomes the "play-the-winner" design, in which the next patient is assigned to the current winner treatment based on the available data, and no randomization is involved. The larger the $\lambda$ is, the more imbalance the randomization will have.

The BAR enrichment design has been implemented successfully in several high-profile clinical trials in oncology. One example is the Biomarker-integrated approaches of targeted therapy of the lung cancer elimination (BATTLE) trial [56]. The BATTLE project consists of one umbrella trial and four parallel II studies with biomarker-based targeted therapies previously treated with chemotherapy but subsequently failed. The main objective of study was to estimate and test the disease control rate at 8 weeks for each treatment given the patients' biomarker profiles. The Bayesian probit model was used to characterize the disease control rate for each treatment by a marker subgroup with adaptive randomization. Another notable example is the I-SPY 2 (investigation of serial studies to predict your therapeutic response with imaging and molecular analysis) 2 phase II in the neoadjuvant setting for women with locally advanced breast cancer. It neoadjuvantly designed to test investigative agents in combination with standard chemotherapies in a curious population. By employing an innovative BAR design, the study aims to test new treatments in half the time, at a fraction of the cost and with significantly fewer participants [58]. Regimens that show a high Bayesian predictive probability of being more effective than the standard therapy will graduate from the trial with their corresponding biomarker signature(s). Regiments will be dropped if they show a low probability of improved efficacy with any biomarker signature. New drugs will enter as those that have undergone testing are graduated or dropped.

One obvious limitation of the BAR design is that adaptive randomization requires a quick turnaround to learn from the interim data and adjust the randomization rate. As a result, the BAR design is applicable only for trials when the endpoint can be assessed in a relative short period of time.

Korn and Freidlin [59] conducted simulations comparing the outcome-adaptive randomization design with the fixed 1:1 or 2:1 randomization design in a randomized two-arm setting. The comparisons were done in terms of required sample sizes, the numbers and proportions of patients having an inferior outcome. The authors found no benefits to outcome-adaptive randomization over 1:1 or 2:1 randomization and, as such, recommended using a fixed 2:1 randomization design when a higher proportion of patients being randomly assigned to the experimental treatment is preferred. In a commentary paper, Berry [60] argued that the greatest potential for adaptive randomization is in multi-armed trials, as in the BATTLE and I-SPY 2 studies, despite that in two-arm trials the benefit of adaptive randomization is limited.

# 11.4.3.2 Bayesian/Frequentist Design for Biomarker Treatment Interaction

In the setting of survival endpoint and only 1 biomarker, Karur and Simon [61] proposed a two-stage Bayesian design similar to the frequentist designs of Freidlin and Simon [29] and Wang et al. [45]. Specifically, it is a Bayesian version of the adaptive design of Wang et al. [45] where the posterior distribution of treatment effects within the marker-positive and marker-negative strata based on an interim analysis where the first stage data is used to make decisions on patient recruitment and trial progression. It is essentially a hybrid Bayesian/frequentist design that takes advantage of the flexibility of Bayesian methods of incorporating prior confidence in the candidate predictive biomarker, and at the same time controls the type I and II error rates that meet the standards for conventional frequentist phase III trials. At the interim analysis, the posterior probabilities are compared to a prespecified threshold value, and 3 possible actions are taken:

- 1. Stop the trial
- 2. Stop recruitment to the marker negative group at the interim stage
- 3. Continue recruitment of both negative and positive patients.

The Karurui and Simon design enables limiting the number of test negative patients to the test drug if data suggest no benefit. It is also demonstrated that the design offers a number of advantages over the frequentist version of the Wang et al. [45] design.

# 11.5 Summary

Clinical researchers face increasing challenges and are presented with unique opportunities in the new era of personalized/precision medicine to meet the needs of patients, payers, and the ever increasing costs of health economics. In this chapter, we gave an overview of population enrichment designs that are growing in the statistical literature, with a focus on designs of predictive enrichment strategies as as to increase the power and efficiency to detect an effective therapy with regard to a predictive biomarker in a clinical trial. We categorized the existing designs into classical designs, frequentist adaptive designs, and Bayesian adaptive designs. We also discussed the efficiency of a biomarker enrichment design compared to a traditional all-comers population, sensitivity/specificity of the assay, and the relative treatment effect of the biomarker positive group versus the biomarker negative group.

Our focus was on designs that involved one or multiple predictive biomarkers that correlate with clinical efficacy endpoints of interest and the experimental treatment. The case of biomarkers that relate to safety outcomes is an important research area but not covered in this chapter. In addition, prognostic biomarkers, pharmacometric biomarkers, and surrogate biomarkers were out of the scope of this chapter.
