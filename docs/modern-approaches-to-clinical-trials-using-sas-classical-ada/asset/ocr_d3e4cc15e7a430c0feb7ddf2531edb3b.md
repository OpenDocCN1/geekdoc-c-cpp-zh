specified level $\alpha$. Let $q_{1-\alpha}$ denote the multiplicity adjusted critical value. A dose-response signal is then established if $T_{\max} \geq q_{1-\alpha}$. In fact, any dose-response model with a test statistic larger than $q_{1-\alpha}$ can be declared statistically significant at level $\alpha$. These models then form a reference set $\{M_1,...,M_L\}$ of significant models, provided $L \geq 1$. Every single contrast test translates into a decision procedure, while a selected dose-response curve is significant given while controlling the FWER at pre-specified level $\alpha$. If no candidate model is statistically significant, the MCP-Mod procedure indicates that a dose-response signal cannot be established from the observed data. But such a result does not necessarily mean that the compound has no effect at all. In general we will assess that there was not enough evidence to get statistical significance. Possible reasons could include small sample sizes or high variance. In addition, the initial candidate set might have been poorly chosen, such that the candidate models do not fit the true curve (perhaps an umbrella-shaped curve) – although in such cases the compound has a pronounced effect and may be far from ineffective, as suggested by the insignificant p-value.

The fourth step is to select one or several weighted models out of the reference set of models. Information criteria like AIC or BIC can be used for this purpose.

The fifth and final step consists of fitting the selected model(s) to the data and estimating adequately the target dose(s) of interest using inverse nonlinear regression techniques. Note in this step all parameters are estimated from the data for the different dose-response models $(\theta_0, \theta_1$ and $\theta_2)$.
