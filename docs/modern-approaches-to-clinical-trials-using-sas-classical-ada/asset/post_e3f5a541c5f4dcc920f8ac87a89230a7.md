
$p_C = 0.745$ (placebo), implying $p_E - p_C = 0.172$. With these numbers, the target allocation proportion (unknown to the investigator at the outset) of the AZT treatment is 0.525 for the RSIHR design and 0.607 for the compound optimal design. We use the total sample $n=477$ to match the real trial setup in [66]. In the simulation of two RAR designs, the first four patients are assigned to the AZT or placebo using the PBD with block size 4; afterwards the allocation is performed using the ERADE procedure with the corresponding target allocation. In our simulation, we assume each patient's outcome is observed before the next patient is enrolled in the study, and treatment randomization probabilities are updated sequentially by RAR algorithms. In practice, the outcome data from some patients may not be available yet at the time of the current patient's entry (i.e. responses are delayed), in which case one would just use the data accrued far thus to update parameter estimates and randomization probabilities. However, this scenario is not explored here.

Table 10.5 (supplemental program ch10.7.1.sas) shows the simulated operating characteristics of the four designs. All four designs achieved their target allocation and are very similar in terms of quality of estimation. The RSIHR design is more variable than CRD (empirical standard deviation of the allocation proportion for RSIHR, compound optimal, and CRD is 0.006, 0.033, and 0.023, respectively). On average, the RSIHR design assigns 12 more patients to the AZT treatment, and the compound optimal design assigns 53 more patients to the AZT treatment compared to the balanced randomization designs (CRD and PBD). Also, on average the RSIHR design results in 2 fewer treatment failures, and the compound optimal design results in 8 fewer treatment failures than the balanced designs. Therefore, in this example, the ethical gains of the RSIHR design are rather modest, whereas the compound optimal design provides the best balance between individual and collective ethics.

## 10.7.2 Implementing DBCD

### 10.7.2.1 Three Cohort Design

Here we describe two examples that can be modified by the reader in order to implement RAR designs in practice. Sample results are provided in the supplemental materials.

Supplemental program `ch10.7.2.sas` illustrates a practical implementation of the DBCD [$\gamma=2$] for a three-cohort design. In this example, 60 patients are randomized to either E or C using PBD with block size 4, with responses generated from a Bernoulli distribution. The second cohort (20 patients) is randomized to either E or C using DBCD [$\gamma=2$], given the data. The last cohort is randomized to either E or C using the sequential design.

## 10.8 Summary

In this chapter we gave an exposition on optimal RAR designs for trials with binary outcomes. The main objective of this chapter is to provide an exposition on the methodology of optimal RAR, but not to give a comprehensive guidance on how to choose the “best” design for use. Our discussion focused primarily on two-arm binary outcome trials where adaptive randomization is inferior to 1:1 randomization in terms of benefit to future patients. Note that the papers [32,67] examined only two specific types of RAR procedures (Bayesian adaptive randomization using Tall and Wathen’s approach [15], and the RAR design targeting RSI allocation [10]). However, the scope of optimal RAR designs is much broader—it also includes other types of optimal allocation (e.g. compound optimal design—Design IV in Section 10.7), and multi-arm RAR trials, with binary, count, continuous, and time-to-event outcomes [14]. In more general settings, RAR can provide more substantial improvements, both in terms of statistical efficiency and ethics, over equal allocation randomization [17-19,31]. One important topic that we have not discussed in this chapter is a combination of RAR with group-sequential designs that allow stopping the trial early for futility (if there is lack of treatment effect) or efficacy (if treatment effect is pronounced). Some authors theoretically showed advantages of group-sequential RAR designs [68-71].

It is important to note that RAR designs are operationally more complex and require more upfront planning than designs with fixed randomization schedules. RAR is applicable in trials where the recruitment period is relatively long and the outcomes can be observed quickly; the data must be of high quality to mitigate the potential for bias throughout the trial conduct. See [2,22,45] for further considerations on implementing RAR designs in practice. In conclusion, if used judiciously, optimal RAR designs can be useful in randomized comparative clinical trials with objectives primarily related to statistical efficiency and ethics.
