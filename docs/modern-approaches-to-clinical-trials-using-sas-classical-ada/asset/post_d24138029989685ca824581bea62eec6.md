
## 10.4.2 Operating Characteristics of RAR designs via Monte Carlo Simulation

By simulating a RAR trial (say, 10,000 times) one can generate empirical distributions of important design characteristics, such as treatment allocation proportion, number of treatment failures, etc. This can enable a comparison of various randomization designs under different experimental scenarios. Supplemental program ch10.4.2.sas can be used to simulate operating characteristics of five types of randomization procedures:

- I. Completely Randomized Design (CRD)
- II. Permuted Block Design (PBD)
- III. SMLE procedure
- IV. DBCD(γ = 2) procedure
- V. ERADE procedure

For procedure I, every patient is randomized between E and C with probability 1/2. For procedure II, treatment assignments are made at random in blocks of size $2m_0$ (where $m_0$ is a pre-specified positive integer) that treatment balance is achieved after every cohort of $2m_0$ patients. For procedures III, IV, and V, first permuted block randomization, and the remaining patients are randomized between E and C using according to a RAR algorithm with RSIHR target allocation $\rho = PRSIHR = \frac{\sqrt{E}}{\sqrt{E}+\sqrt{C}}$

The design characteristics include the following:

- Allocation proportion $N_E(n)/n$;
- Number of trial subjects assigned to superior treatment: $N_E(m)\{p_E \ge p_C\} + N_C(m)\{p_E < p_C\}$;
- Total number of failures in the trial: $F(m) = F_E(n) + F_C(n)$, where $F_k(n)$ is the number of failures on treatment $k=E,C$;
- Estimated treatment difference $\hat{\Delta}(n) = \hat{\rho}_E - \hat{\rho}_C$;
- Empirical (average) power of the Wald test (with 2-sided $\alpha=0.05$).

Supplemental program ch10.4.2.sas was used to simulate the operating characteristics of the five designs I–V for a scenario when $p_E=0.7$ and $p_C=0.4$, based on 10,000 simulations. The trial sample size was set $n=120$ which corresponds to $\sim92\%$ power of the Wald test for the equal allocation design. The target allocation is 0.5 for designs I and II and it is 0.57 for designs III, IV, and V.

Figure 10.3 shows simulated distributions of the allocation proportion. For design I (CRD), the distribution is centered around 0.5 but variability is nontrivial (the range for the allocation proportion is 0.33 to 0.68). For design II (PBD), the treatment sample sizes are always perfectly balanced; therefore, the allocation is constant at 0.5. The RAR designs III (SMLE), IV (DBCD with $\gamma=2$) and V (ERADE) have approximately normal distributions that are centered around target allocation 0.57. Consistent with theory, ERADE has the lowest variability and SMLE has the highest variability among the three RAR designs.

Table 10.3 shows selected summary statistics from the simulation. Since in our example $p_E=0.7$ and $p_C=0.4$, treatment $E$ is superior. The average number of treatment $E$ assignments is 66 for SMLE, 68 for DBCD and ERADE, and it is 60 for balanced designs I and II (note the different ranges of this characteristic among the five designs). In regards to the total number of treatment failures, RAR designs yield 2–3 fewer failures on average compared to the balanced designs. All five designs result in unbiased estimates and have similar mean squared errors (MSEs) of the treatment difference. The empirical power of the Wald test for the designs is 0.92–0.93.

Overall, in this example, RAR designs provide some reduction in treatment failures that are almost identical in terms of quality of estimation and power to balanced randomization designs. While in our simulation we considered the Wald test, other tests can be considered as well. Gu and Lee in [52] performed a comprehensive simulation study to explore the properties of nine different test statistics in two-arm binary outcome RAR trials. They found that for small trials $(n=30)$, the Cook’s correction to the chi-square test and Williams’ correction to the log-likelihood ratio test have the best performance.

## 10.5 Power and Sample Size for Response-Adaptive Randomization

For a randomized design with a fixed allocation $\rho$ (e.g., $\rho=1/2$ or $\rho=2/3$), both the total sample size $n$ and treatment group sizes $n_E=np$ and $n_C=n(1-\rho)$ are fixed, appropriately chosen to achieve the desired power of a test. In a RAR trial, the total sample size $n$ is fixed, but the treatment group sizes $N_E(n)$ and $N_C(n)$ are random. Therefore, when planning a RAR trial, special considerations are required for sample size and power.

Hu and Rosenberger in [14] presented an approach to determine appropriate sample size for a RAR design with target allocation $p_E, p_C$. Considering the one-sided hypothesis $H_0: p_E=p_C$ versus $H_a: p_E>p_C$. For a given significance $\alpha$, $H_0$ is rejected if the test statistic

$$\frac{\hat{p}_E-\hat{p}_C}{\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_E}{N_E(n)}+\frac{\hat{p}_C}{N_C(n)}}} > z_{1-\alpha}$$

where $z_{1-\alpha}$ is the $(1-\alpha)$th quantile of the standard normal distribution. Under the assumptions $N_k(n) \to \infty$ almost surely, $\hat{p}_k q_k \to p_k$ in probability, and $\sqrt{n}(\hat{p}_k-p_k) \to N(0, p_k)$ in distribution for $k=E,C$ (which holds for RAR procedures considered in Sections 10.3.2, 10.3.3, and 10.3.4), the power function can be approximated as

$$P[p_E, p_C, N_E(n), N_C(n)] = \Phi \left( \frac{p_E-p_C}{\sqrt{\frac{p_E}{N_E(n)}+\frac{p_C}{N_C(n)}}} - z_{1-\alpha} \right), (10.11)$$

where $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. Importantly, quantity (10.11) is a random variable because $N_E(n)$ and $N_C(n)$ are random. Under further asymptotic assumptions (that are met for RAR procedures considered thus far):

- $N_E(n)/n-\rho \ge N(0,1)$ almost surely and
- $\sqrt{n(N_E(n)/n-\rho)} \to N(0, \sigma^2)$ in distribution for some $\sigma^2 > 0$,

one can obtain a second-order Taylor's approximation to the power function of a RAR design [14]. It is shown that the power of a RAR design with target allocation $\rho$ is mainly determined by the power of a fixed allocation design with allocation $\rho$ and the values of $N_E(n)/n-\rho$ and $(N_E(n)/n-\rho)^2$ (the expected value of the latter is the variance of the allocation proportion). Therefore, one can facilitate the planning of a sample size for RAR designs based on asymptotic approximations.

Three types of sample size can be distinguished: 1) sample size to achieve power $1-\beta$ for a fixed allocation design (type I sample size), 2) sample size to achieve power $1-\beta$ on average (type II sample size), and 3) sample size to achieve power with probability $1-\xi$ (for some positive $\xi$) (type III sample size) [14].

If consider a type I sample size. For a fixed allocation, the treatment group sample sizes are $N_E(n)=np$ and $N_C(n)=n(1-\rho)$, and the sample size to achieve power $1-\beta$ is obtained from solving

$$\frac{p_E-p_C}{\frac{p_E+p_C}{np(1-\rho)}}z_{1-\alpha} = 1-\beta$$

The solution is

$$n_I = \frac{(\frac{p_E}{\rho}+\frac{p_C}{1-\rho})(z_{1-\alpha}+z_{1-\beta})^2}{(p_E-p_C)^2} \quad (10.12)$$

(if necessary, $n_I$ in (10.12) can be rounded to the next largest integer).

To determine type II sample size ($n_{II}$), we should consider the average power function. By our assumption, $\sqrt{n(N_E(n)/n-\rho)} \to N(0, \sigma^2)$; the average power function is then approximated as

$$EP[p_E, p_C, N_E(n), N_C(n)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{p_E-p_C}{\sqrt{\frac{p_E}{np+\sigma\sqrt{n}}+\frac{p_C}{(1-\rho)n-\sigma\sqrt{n}}}}z_{1-\alpha}\phi(x)dx \quad (10.13)$$
