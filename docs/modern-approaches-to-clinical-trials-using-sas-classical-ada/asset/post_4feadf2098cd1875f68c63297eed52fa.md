## 5.6 Summary

The continual reassessment method and its variations have been studied extensively and their performances are in general better than algorithm-based methods, such as the 3+3 design. All the cumulative data can be integrated and modeled for better decision making. In the Bayesian paradigm, more direct comparisons and early stopping rules can be implemented, which can help pin down the right dose faster.

## 6.1 Introduction

In the drug development process, a candidate compound needs to go through a stringent series of testing for toxicity, pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics, efficacy, and safety before it can be released to the market for patient use. The process involves different phases of nonclinical studies on animals and clinical studies in healthy volunteers and the targeted patient population with the disease [1, 2]. Clinical studies can be categorized into 4 phases (phase I-IV), corresponding to four levels of stage-gate drug development: human pharmacology, therapeutic exploratory, therapeutic confirmatory, and therapeutic use studies [3]. Phases I and II are generally considered exploratory and learning phases. The goal of phase III studies is mainly for therapeutic confirmation. Phase III trials are post marketing studies. Because phase III pivotal studies are typically large scale and so very high cost (the cost in some of phase III studies could be even more than the total cost spent from the very beginning of a compound to the end of phase II studies), phase II studies are one of the most critical components addition, regulatory agencies generally recommend that a full dose range of the test therapy has been explored prior to initiating phase III and that identification of minimum effective dose (MinED) be encouraged [4]. Given the substantial resources invested in phase III and beyond, a well-designed phase II study could be beneficial to both patients and sponsors and, consequently, reduce the time to market. This chapter describes the general consideration dose response, and demonstrates two examples using SAS.

In addition to efficacy response, there are also toxicity dose–response curves. We will assume, as shown in Figure 6.1, when the dose increases, both efficacy and toxicity increase. D ranging trial typically includes a small number of doses. One common design is a four-group study with placebo, low dose, medium dose, and high dose. When designing a ranging study, it is important to keep the following questions in mind:

- 1. What dose-range should be considered?
- 2. Was the concept proven?
- 3. How do you space the dosages from the placebo to the maximum tolerated?

A phase II development program usually consists of a small-scale PoC followed by moderately sized dose ranging studies.
