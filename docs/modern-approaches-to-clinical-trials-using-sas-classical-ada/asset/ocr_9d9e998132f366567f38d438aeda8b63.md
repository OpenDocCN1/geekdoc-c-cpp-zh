# 9.3.2 Biased Coin Design

Another well-known restricted randomization method is the biased coin design (BCD), which aims to provide an approximated balance among the treatment arms [2]. Each step of the BCD favors the treatment that has been underrepresented in the trial thus far. For illustration, let $n_1$ and $n_2$ denote the number of subjects that are assigned to arm 1 and arm 2, respectively. Let $ho$ be the prescribed randomization probability for arm 1. As the trial proceeds, a new patient will be allocated to arm 1 with a probability of

```
$$\text{(patient assigned to arm 1)} = \begin{cases} \rho, & n_1 < n_2 \ 1/2, & n_1 = n_2 \ 1-\rho, & n_1 > n_2. \end{cases}$
```

There are many choices for the selection of $ho$: Efron recommended $p=2/3$, such that the trial can be simply conducted by tossing a biased coin [2]. Based on a constant $ho$, the treatment allocation cannot characterize the extent of unbalance between the two groups. To accommodate the sample size information, Wei recommended the allocation probability to arm 1 as

```
$$\rho = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \frac{\Delta}{n})$$
```

where $\Delta = n_1 - n_2$ and $n = n_1 + n_2$ [3]. As one step further, Antognini and Giovagnoli proposed an adjustable BCD to correct the patient distribution towards balance: the more imbalanced the two groups, the more strength is to pull the samples back to balance [4].

The BCDRandom macro implements the BCD of Wei [3] to generate the treatment assignment for each new patient. Users need to input the accumulated number of patients in each group ($n_1$ and $n_2$).
