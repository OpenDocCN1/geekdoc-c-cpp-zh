
hence, there is no MCP adjustment. For a simple trend contrast with low to discrete doses, the treatment groups are simply ordered from high. The placebo response is assumed to be 0, the lowest dose (2.5%) of MTD is assumed to have 20% response, the next lowest (5% of MTD) is assumed to have 40% response, the medium dose (12.5%) of MTD is assumed to have 60% response, the second highest dose (25% of MTD) is assumed to have 100% response. Then the coefficients -5, -3, -1, 1, 3, and 5 are assigned to these treatment groups [16]. Here, the primary hypothesis to be tested is:

```
$H_0: -5\mu_0-3\mu_1-\mu_2+3\mu_3+5\mu_4 \le 0$.
$H_1: -5\mu_0-3\mu_1-\mu_2+3\mu_3+5\mu_4 > 0$,
```

where $\mu_0$ denotes the mean of placebo group, $\mu_1$ denotes the mean of the 5% dose group, $\mu_2$ denotes the mean of the 12.5% dose group, $\mu_3$ denotes the mean of the 25% dose group, and $\mu_4$ denotes the mean of the 75% dose group.

In the case of a dose-response study with $k$ dose groups and a placebo, the sample size estimates based on a contrast can be derived as follows [21]. Let $\mu_i$ be the population mean for group $i, i = 0, \dots, k$, where $\mu_0$ is the mean from the placebo group, and let $c_i$ be the corresponding coefficients in the contrast. The null hypothesis of no treatment effect (i.e., weight sum of treatment effect across all doses) can be written as:

```
$H_0: L(\mu)=\sum_{i=0}^k c_i\mu_i \le 0$ vs $H_1: L(\mu)=\sum_{i=0}^k c_i\mu_i > 0$, with $\sum_{i=0}^k c_i^2 = 0$.
```

Under the alternative hypothesis and the condition of homogeneous variances, the sample size per group (i.e., equal sample size in each group) can be obtained as

```
$n = \frac{(z_{1-\alpha}+z_{1-\beta})^2\sigma^2}{\sum_{i=0}^k c_i^2}$
```

where $z_{1-\alpha}$ and $z_{1-\beta}$ are the corresponding quantile points of a standard normal distribution.

The standard deviation used to generate these data is set as 4. From the observed placebo response, and the anticipated response from this test drug, possible targeted clinical differences (δ) can be considered, including 1.00, 1.25, or 1.50. From such a large standard deviation, and a relatively small delta (δ), the required sample size could be very large. For example, if a pairwise comparison is used, then under one-sided α of 0.05, with 90% power, and delta of 1.25, the sample size for each treatment group would be about 176 subjects. For a 7-arm study, this will take a total sample size of 1,232 subjects. To power the study using the trend contrast as specified above, the sample size would reduce to 126 per group (882 overall).

The study using the same assumptions is proved to detect a significant trend contrast test under a balanced design with equal number of subjects in each treatment group. Sample Sizes under Various α, Power, and δ, each of the scenarios is considered for different α and power. The standard deviation is assumed to be 4, and the 100% response is assumed to be 1, 1.25, or 1.5.

Because the test drug is at an early stage of clinical development, and the concept has not been proven, it is difficult to justify a huge budget of 882 subjects in an early phase II study. Upon review, the company decided they were willing to take a higher risk on α, to reduce the sample size with a reasonable investment. The compromise was that the one-sided α can be increased to 0.1, and keep the power at 90%.

For this study, a treatment difference from a place of approximately 1.25 was selected. Sample Sizes under Various α, Power, and δ, a sample size of 97 per treatment group provides an estimated power of 90% for a one-sided α of 0.10, and for this sample size, the power is over 80% for a one-sided α of 0.05.

A sample of 100 subjects per treatment group is sufficient to detect differences larger than 1.25 in the mean change from baseline in the WOMAC OA Index Pain Subscale, using the aforementioned linear contrast. Since a single test for trend is employed, no adjustment for a multiple comparison is needed. If this test is significant at α of 0.1 (one-sided), the minimum effective dose (MinED) will be estimated.

## Example Code 6.1 SAS/IML to Calculate Orthogonal Polynomial Contrast

```sas
%polyorth(f_in);
data
    set &f_in nobs=k;
    call_("nlevel", put(k,8.0));
run;

data niv;
    length level $8;
    do i=1 to &level;
        level=trim(left(i));
        output niv;
end;
```

```sas
run;
data deg;
 do i=1 to &level-1;
  degree=trim(left(i));
  output deg;
end;
run;
```

```sas
proc iml;
start;
  use &f_in;
  read all(dose) into x;
  use niv;
  read all(level) into labco;
  use deg;
  read all(degree) into labco;
  degmax=row(x)-1;
  p=orth(x,degmax);
  pp=p[2:ncol(p)];
  create poly from p [rowname=labco colname=labco];
  append from pp [rowname=labro];
finish;
run;
quit;

title "Orthogonal polynomial contrasts"
title2 "--------------------------------------"
title3 ""
proc print data=poly noobs label;
labro="Polynomial degree";
run;
%end polyorth;
```

```sas
data pol;
input dose @@;
cards;
0 2.5 5 12.5 25 75;
run;
%polyorth(&f_in=pol);
```
