## 10.6 Additional Considerations

### 106.1 More Than Two Treatments and Non-Binary Outcomes

Many RCTs involve more than two treatment arms. For instance, in a phase II dose ranging study, the treatment arms may be a placebo and/or an active control and several doses of an investigational drug. The study objectives may include estimation of various treatment contrasts and testing hypotheses of equality of treatment effects. Frequently, the optimal allocation ratio is unequal across treatment arms [53]. As noted in [33], “...the adaptive randomization light brightest in complicated multi-arm settings,” and indeed, optimal RAR designs are expected to provide more substantial gains over equal randomization in trials with more than two treatment arms (e.g., by placing more patients in the better arm).

A general framework for finding optimal allocation in multi-arm settings was proposed by deCets and co-authors. For multi-arm trials, one can use DBCD or an urn model. The adaptive randomization light brightest in complicated multi-arm settings, and indeed, optimal RAR designs are expected to provide more substantial gains over equal randomization in trials with more than two treatment arms (e.g., by placing more patients in the better arm).

### 10.6.2 Delayed Response

One assumption for RAR is that outcomes are instantaneous. In practice, if the recruitment period is long enough and outcomes are observed quickly, RAR can still be applied. However, if there is a significant delay in observing response, the advantages are diminished.

### 10.6.3 Misclassified Response

RAR requires access to accurate data. In some trials, binary responses may be misclassified, which can lead to bias. If the misclassification mechanism is known, one can adjust for it.

### 10.6.4 Time Trends

In some clinical trials there may be systematic trends in prognostic factors. For instance, the earliest patients may have a worse prognosis than later patients. One of the major criticisms of RAR is that it can lead to biased estimates if time trends are present, but one can use covariate-adaptive randomization designs.

## 10.7 Examples

### 10.7.1 Redesigning a Real Clinical Trial

In this section we give an example of a published clinical trial where an optimal RAR design could be useful. Connor and co-authors in [66] reported the results of a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of the efficacy and safety of zidovudine (AZT) in reducing the risk of maternal-infant transmission of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) type 1. In, 477 pregnant women were enrolled and randomized equally between AZT [nc=239] and a placebo [nc=238]. In the study, 409 women gave birth to 415 infants. The estimated proportions of uninfected infants at 18 months were 0.917 for the AZT group and 0.745 for the placebo group; that, the AZT treatment reduced the risk of maternal-infant transmission by approximately two thirds compared to placebo. An optimal RAR design would assign the AZT treatment more often, thereby achieving a more effective trial design.

We consider a redesign of this trial by simulation. Four competing randomization procedures will be compared:

- Completely Randomized Design (CRD): Every patient is randomized between the treatment groups with a probability 1/2. (While such design would not be used in practice, it is included here to enable a comparison of variability with two RAR designs.)
- Permuted Block Design (PBD): Randomization is applied in blocks of size 4 such that treatment numbers are equalized after every four patients.
- RSIHR Design: The target allocation $\rho_E=\sqrt{\rho_{PE}}/(\sqrt{\rho_{PE}}+\sqrt{\rho_{PC}})$ is implemented using ERADE (Section 10.3.4) with $\gamma=0.5$.
- Compound Optimality Design: The target allocation (obtained as the unique solution to equation (10.4) with $\omega=0.5$) is implemented by using the ERADE procedure (Section 10.3.4) with $\gamma=0.5$.
