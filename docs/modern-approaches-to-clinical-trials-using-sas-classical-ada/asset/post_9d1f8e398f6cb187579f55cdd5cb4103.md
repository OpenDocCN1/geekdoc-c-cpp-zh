Based on empirical evidence, Thall and Waten recommended using in practice $\gamma=1/2$ or $\gamma=n/(2N)$ (where $n$ is the current sample size and $N$ is the maximum allowable sample size) [15]. An application of this approach in a soft tissue sarcoma trial is described in [28]. Some authors described applications of Bayesian adaptive randomization in multi-arm randomized clinical trials in oncology where it was imperative to maximize individual patients' benefit from participating in the trial [29-31]. Korn and Freilin in [32] argued that ethics due to the number of non-responders (in the trial) are typically very modest compared to a fixed-schedule 1:1 or 2:1 randomization, and even these benefits can be outweighed by other complexities inherent to adaptive randomization (e.g. outcomes must be observed fast, patient inclusion rate must be low, and data must be periodically updated to modify treatment probabilities). In a follow-up note, Berry in [33] acknowledged that ethical gains of Bayesian adaptive randomization are indeed somewhat limited in two-arm trials, but they can be much more pronounced in multi-objective, multi-arm trials. The potential gains of RAR should be assessed not only in terms of ethics (e' reduction in the number of non-responders), but also in terms of statistical efficiency criteria (e.g. enhanced precision or increased power of statistical tests). In particular, phase II dose-ranging trials evaluating different experimental therapeutics with the common control arm provide a nice setting where RAR can provide a dual benefit of placing more patients on better-performing treatment arms and an increasing amount of statistical information for the chosen experimental objectives [21,31].

## 10.1.3.3 Optimal Response-Adaptive Randomization

As was mentioned in Section 10.1.2, modern research on RAR has focused on the development of optimal RAR procedures for multiob clinical trials with two or more treatment arms and different types of primary outcomes (binary, continuous (Gaussian), and time-to-event). The idea is to determine an allocation design that is optimal for selected trial objectives and construct a RAR procedure with minimal variability that converges to the chosen optimal allocation [34]. The RAR component is required because optimal allocation typically depends on model parameters that are to be estimated using accumulating outcome data from the trial. Optimal RAR designs have firm theoretical basis, and they can frequently be more efficient and more ethical than conventional equal allocation randomization designs [14,17-19]. Under widely satisfied conditions, optimal RAR designs have similar asymptotic proportions and maximum likelihood estimators) to the fixed randomization designs [35]. A recent review of proper statistical inferential procedures following optimal RAR designs can be found in [36]. It should be acknowledged that many optimal RAR designs are relatively new methods that have not yet demonstrated their potential in practice. To facilitate implementation of these designs, validated information systems (to perform interactive data entry and dynamic update of treatment randomization probabilities) must be developed. In addition, input from the health authorities (FDA, EMA) will be essential for successful implementation of optimal RAR designs in clinical trials.

## 10.1.4 Organization of this Chapter

The purpose of this chapter is to provide an exposition on the methodology of optimal RAR designs to a statistician or a clinical trialist who is new to this subject, and to provide some SAS code to simulate treatment allocation sequences and operating characteristics of selected RAR designs. A particular class of RAR designs (optimal RAR for two-arm binary outcome trials) is chosen because it is quite basic, but at the same time, it incorporates all of the key elements of RAR, and is well suited for illustration purposes. The chapter is not meant to be a comprehensive study on how to choose the best RAR design for use in practice. For book-length discussions on this subject please refer to [14,37].

In Section 10.2, we review and compare several optimal allocation rules for two-arm binary outcome trials. Section 10.3 describes RAR designs that can be used to converge to the chosen optimal allocation. We also discuss theoretical aspects of optimal RAR designs - the relationship between optimality, variability and power, asymptotically best RAR procedures, and statistical inference following RAR. In Section 10.4, we discuss Monte Carlo simulation of optimal RAR designs. In Section 10.5, we discuss the issues of sample size and power for RAR trials. Section 10.6 describes some additional important considerations and practical issues for RAR trials. In Section 10.7, we illustrate utility of RAR by redesigning a real life clinical trial. Concluding remarks are given in Section 10.8.

## 10.2 Optimal Allocation

Consider a binary outcome clinical trial to compare an experimental treatment, $E$, versus a control treatment, $C$. Let $p_E$ denote the probability of success on $E$ ($q_E=1-p_E$) and $p_C$ denote the probability of success on $C$ ($q_C=1-p_C$). Suppose the trial has a sample size $n$ which is fixed and predetermined. Let $\rho$ denote the allocation proportion for the experimental treatment where $0 < \rho < 1$. This means roughly $n_E=n\rho$ patients are assigned to $E$ and $n_C=n(1-\rho)$ patients are assigned to $C$. An experimental design problem is to find an optimal allocation $\rho^*$ as a solution to some formal optimization problem. In a two-arm comparative trial, a common objective is to have sufficient power to test the study hypothesis. Consider testing the hypothesis $H_0: p_E=p_C$ versus $H_1: p_E \neq p_C$ using the Wald test $Z_W = \frac{\hat{p}_E-\hat{p}_C}{\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_E(1-\hat{p}_E)}{n_E}+\frac{\hat{p}_C(1-\hat{p}_C)}{n_C}}}$, where $\hat{p}_E$ and $\hat{p}_C$ are consistent estimators of the corresponding parameters. Under $H_1$, $Z_W$ has asymptotically normal distribution with mean $\theta$ and variance $V$. Using the large sample approximations $S=n\rho$ and $F=n(1-\rho)$, where $\rho=(n_{EP}+n_{PC})/n$, the variance of the score statistic is approximated as $V=n_P\rho(1-\rho)(\rho q_{EE}+(1-\rho)q_{CE})(\rho q_{AE}+(1-\rho)q_{CE})$.

The allocation that maximizes the value of $V$ for fixed sample size is known as Neyman allocation and is given by:
$$\rho_N = \frac{\sqrt{q_E}}{\sqrt{q_E}+\sqrt{q_C}} (10.1)$$

The Neyman allocation assigns a greater proportion of patients to the variable treatment. Unfortunately, when $p_E+p_C>1$, the Neyman allocation is skewed in favor of the less successful group. Rosenberger and co-authors in [10] considered the following question: For fixed variance of the Wald test, which allocation minimizes expected number of treatment failures? The optimization problem in this case is formulated as follows:
$$\min_{\rho\in(0,1)} n\rho q_{EP}+(1-\rho)q_C$$

The optimal solution is RSI allocation named after Rosenberg, Stallard, Ivanova, and Ricks [10]:
$$\rho = \frac{q_E}{q_E+q_C} (10.2)$$

Unlike the Neyman allocation, RSI allocation always assigns a greater proportion of patients to the more successful treatment.

Now consider testing the hypothesis of equality of treatment effects using the score test based on log-odds ratio, $\theta=\log\{q_{EP}/q_{CE}\}$. For testing $H_0:\theta=0$, one computes $Z=(n_{SE}-n_{SC})/n$ and $V=n_{SE}n_{SC}/n^3$, where $S_E$ and $S_C$ are the number of successes on $E$ and $C$, $S=S_E+S_C$ and $F=n-S$. When $n$ is large, $Z$ is approximately normally distributed with mean $\theta$ and variance $V$. Using the large sample approximations $S=n\rho$ and $F=n(1-\rho)$, where $\rho=(n_{EP}+n_{PC})/n$, the variance is $V=n_P\rho(1-\rho)(\rho q_{EE}+(1-\rho)q_{CE})(\rho q_{AE}+(1-\rho)q_{CE})$.

Magirr in [39] considered the problem of minimizing expected number of treatment failures $n-\rho q_{EP}+q_C(1-\rho)$ subject to the fixed value of $\nu$. It can be shown using simple algebra that this is equivalent to minimizing the value of $\rho(1-\rho)\rho_{EE}+(1-\rho)\rho_{PC}^{-1}$ for $\rho\in(0,1)$. The optimal solution is:
$$PSCORE=\frac{p_E^2-2p_Ep_C+\sqrt{p_E^2-4p_Ep_C+p_C^2}}{3(p_E-p_C)} (10.3)$$
