If a drug has a narrow therapeutic window, then the drug will need to be developed even more carefully, and physicians will prescribe the drug with caution.

Oftentimes, products developed for life-threatening diseases (e.g., cancer) tend to have narrow therapeutic windows. On the other hand, drugs developed to treat chronic diseases (e.g., high blood pressure, depression, high cholesterol, or arthritis) are required to have wide therapeutic windows because these drugs are frequently used in older patients, or patients with other concurrent diseases, and sometimes used in children.

In clinical development, phase III trials are designed to confirm product efficacy. Results obtained from phase III studies provide evidence to help regulatory agencies make decisions on whether to approve a product, while the phase III program engaged significant resources. Therefore, it is critical that dose-ranging trials from phase II deliver the information to help with phase III design. In other words, if the appropriate dose or doses for product registration is not included in phase III trials, it is very likely that the product candidate will miss the opportunity of being approved for market authorization according to plan. Hence, information obtained from dose-ranging trials needs to provide sufficient support for dose selection in phase III study designs.

## 6.2.2 Finding the Effective Dose

ICH E4 defines the minimum effective dose (MinED) as the "smallest dose with a discernible useful effect" [4]. Finding the MinED is an important step in drug development. To illustrate, let's consider the following reported example. In a clinical development program for a drug to treat osteoarthritis [7], the first dose-response study included a placebo, 80 mg, 120 mg, and 160 mg of the test drug. Results from this study indicate that all three doses are efficacious (the left panel of Figure 6.2 Dose-Response Results from the Three Studies of Osteoarthritis Drug). These results also show that 80 mg may have already been at the high side of the dose-response curve. A second study was designed to explore the 40 mg dose, and results from this second study are given in the middle panel of Figure 6.2. With these findings, the project team phase III studies using a dose of 120 mg to confirm the long-term efficacy of this drug.

An end-of-phase II meeting with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) was commented that the minimum effective dose (MinED) was not found. The main issue here is that the efficacy response does not change as the dose level changes and all the three testing doses almost equally reach the full efficacy, so the dose-response relationship was not detected. The project team designed a third dose-response study to explore the lower dose range. Doses included in the third are 2.5 mg, 10 mg, and 40 mg. As shown in the right panel of Figure 6.2 Dose-Response Results from the Three Studies of Osteoarthritis Drug, the third study successfully established the dose-response relationship, i.e., the response increases as dose increases. Based on these results, it is clear that the phase III studies are designed with a dose being too high. The impact of this process can cause a major delay in the development program and considerable waste of resources.

Therefore, the MinD is a very important concept in product development. Unfortunately, there is not a universally accepted definition of this term [8]. Finding MinD is critical because it is generally believed that toxicity increases as dose increases. Hence, a lower dose that is effective implies that it could be safer than higher doses.

Clinical results obtained from phase III studies are used to obtain an investigative product marketing authorization. Based on these data, if a dose is efficacious and safe, then that dose can be approved, and the product label specifies the dose or the range of doses that the product is approved for. However, after approval and broader use, excessive adverse events are observed that impact the benefit-risk balance for patients, then a question as to whether the approved dose is low enough could be raised. At this time, it would be very challenging to ask what the MinD is for this product. Therefore, it is critical to find MinD in phase II, before it is too late.

Drug price in the US is not directly associated with dosage. However, in many other countries, the price of a medicinal product is linked to its dose. In other words, if the dose of the product is higher, then it is more expensive. Now suppose that a certain product is approved in such a country, and the price is set on the lowest approved dose. If later it was detected that the MinED for the product is in fact, lower than the approved dose, then a dose reduction is needed at a post-market stage. Accordingly, the price of this product has to be lowered further. When this happens, there is a huge impact on the earnings of such a product. Therefore, from a marketing strategy point of view, it would be critical to identify the MinED before the product enters its phase III development.

Finding the MinED is a phase II practice. As discussed above, if the MinED was not identified at phase II, it would be very difficult and expensive to find the MinED at a later stage of product development. In fact, phase II could be the only opportunity to characterize the relationship between doses and product efficacy. Therefore it is very important to pay more attention to phase II, and to carefully design dose-ranging trials.
