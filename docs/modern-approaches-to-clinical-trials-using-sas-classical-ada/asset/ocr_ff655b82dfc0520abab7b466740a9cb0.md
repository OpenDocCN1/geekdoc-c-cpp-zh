multivariate normal with

$E[S(t_j)] = \eta_j, Var[S(t_j)] = t_j$

and for $t_j < t_{j2}$

$Cov[S(t_{j1}), S(t_{j2})] = t_{j1}$.

We can readily see that for $t_{j1} < t_{j2}$, the random variables $S(t_{j1})$ and the increment, $S(t_{j2}) - S(t_{j1})$ are independent.

There are a few important implications following this result. First, in most clinical trials that use normal, binomial, or survival endpoint, the test statistic $Z(t_j)$ is often utilized. Therefore, by above theorem, the distributional structure of these statistics after transformation is asymptotically the same as $S(t_j)$'s. Therefore, one can construct group sequential stopping boundaries for the $S(t_j)$ stochastic process using the property that the overall probability of crossing a boundary is limited to the desired I error rate, $\alpha$, under the null hypothesis $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ or equivalently when $\eta = \Phi$. This efficiency to monitor trials with normal, binomial, or survival endpoint, or even more general endpoints. Details on boundary construction will be given in 2.4 Types of Stopping Boundaries.

Second, having generated the appropriate boundaries, one may compute efficacy boundary probabilities for the stochastic process $S(t_j)$ under the alternative hypothesis $H_1: \theta = \theta_1$ or equivalently when $\eta = \eta_1$, where $\eta_1 = (\theta_1 - \theta_0)\sqrt{t_j}$. One can, therefore, search for the value of $\eta_1$ at which the overall boundary crossing probability equals the desired power, $1 - \beta$. By substituting this value of $\eta_1$ into equation

```
$l_j = \left[frac{\eta_1}{\theta_1 - \theta_0}\right]^2$.
```

one can estimate $l_j$ and hence $n_j$, the maximum sample size needed to attain the desired power, $1 - \beta$, at any pre-specified clinically meaningful treatment difference $\theta = \theta_1$.
