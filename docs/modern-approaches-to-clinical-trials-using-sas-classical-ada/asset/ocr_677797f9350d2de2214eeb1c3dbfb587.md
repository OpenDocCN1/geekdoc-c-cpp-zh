# 6.2.5 How to Space the Test Dosages

Hamlett et al. proposed to use a binary dose spacing (BDS) for dose allocation, since this method of dose allocation is based on dividing dose intervals into halves [15]. Over the years, BDS has been applied successfully in many dose ranging designs [12,16]. In order to determine the doses using the BDS approach, it is assumed that the MTD is $	au$, thus the dose space is $[0, 	au]$. Without loss of generality, $	au$ can be taken as 1. It is assumed that the dose-response relationship is monotonic. It is further assumed that the number of doses is known. Given this setting, doses are chosen from the interval $[0,1]$. Suppose we want a design with three treatment groups, including placebo, a low dose and a high dose. The placebo dose is taken to be zero. The challenge then is to select a low dose and a high dose, keeping in mind that we may not want a high dose close to 1 (the assumed MTT). An intuitive approach might be to split this interval into two $[0,2]$ and $(1/2,1]$ and select a dose in each of these intervals. A natural choice is to select the midpoint of each interval (split each interval in half), giving the doses, $x_1=1/4$ and $x_2=3/4$. Note that if we choose doses in the upper interval (greater than 1/2 and approaching 1) then these doses will be toxic and are generally not of primary interest in the dose-selection process. On the other hand, activities of the lower doses are very important information for drug development. Hence, the basic idea for BDS is to search for the lower end of the dose range.

In another case, suppose we want to consider a design with four treatment groups including a placebo, low, medium and high doses. In order to avoid selecting too high a dose, which can be toxic, we may want to keep $3/2$ as the high dose; that is, leave the interval $(1/2,2]$ unchanged. Since we want to use some low doses to help identify the MinED, we can divide the lower interval $[0,1/2]$ and $[1/2,1]$ in half, giving the midpoint of these two intervals and split the two intervals into halves — giving the three doses $x_1=1/8, x_2=3/2^3$ and $x_3=3/22$.
