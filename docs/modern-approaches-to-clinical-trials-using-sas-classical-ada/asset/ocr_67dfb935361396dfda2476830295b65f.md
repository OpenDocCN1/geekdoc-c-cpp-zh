If the second option is selected, then there could be many varieties in writing such a contrast.

For example, in a four-group design with a placebo, low dose, medium dose and high dose, the PoC can only be based on the high dose versus a placebo (let $\mu_L$ be the mean response of low dose, $\mu_M$ be the mean response for the medium dose, and $\mu_H$ be the mean response of high dose):

$H_0: \mu_H = \mu_P$ VS. $H_1 \mu_H > \mu_P$.

Or, the contrast can be a trend test, which is based on all doses [12, 16], assuming a monotonic dose-response relationship and equally spaced doses:

$H_0: -3\mu_P - \mu_L + \mu_M + 3\mu_H = 0$ VS. $H_1: -3\mu_P - \mu_L + \mu_M + 3\mu_H > 0$.

Another possible PoC contrast may also include equal weight to each dose (i.e., testing the weighted sum of the three doses versus placebo):

$H_0: -3\mu_P + \mu_L + \mu_M + \mu_H = 0$ VS. $H_1: -3\mu_P + \mu_L + \mu_M + \mu_H > 0$.

Assuming only the high dose is effective (i.e., testing the highest dose versus the weighted sum of the other groups):

$H_0: -\mu_P - \mu_L - \mu_M + 3\mu_H = 0$ VS. $H_1: -\mu_P - \mu_L - \mu_M + 3\mu_H > 0$.

As can be found from the above examples, if a contrast (that includes more than two treatment groups) is used for the PoC hypothesis, then there could be many possible ways of writing such a contrast, but it is really important that the project team reflect on choosing the most meaningful one for the particular compound and therapeutic area.
