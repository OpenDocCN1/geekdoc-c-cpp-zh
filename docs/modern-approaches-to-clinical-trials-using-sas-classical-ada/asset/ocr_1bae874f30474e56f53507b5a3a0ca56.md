# 3.2 Blinded SSR Methods

Blinded SSR does not require knowledge of the treatment assignment at the individual patient level. It is designed to modify sample size due to inaccurate assumptions of the variability associated with a continuous endpoint or an assumed background event rate in the control arm for trials with a binary endpoint. Even though the treatment assignment information is very helpful in assessing such parameters, methods have been developed to allow their estimation without the actual treatment assignment information.

Because blinded SSR does not involve treatment unblinding and can be conducted without worrying about introducing bias into a trial, blinded SSR is classified as a “well-understood” adaptive feature in the FDA guidance. From a practical standpoint, it is also easier to implement than unblinded SSR. For example, there is no potential risk of trial integrity loss due to accidental interim results, and no independent data monitoring committee (DMC) required. However, this simplicity comes at a cost: most blinded SSR methodologies rely on initially assumed treatment effect to value. In case of the actual treatment effect being larger than planned, these methods can mistakenly infer that the variance is larger than expected and increase the sample size unnecessarily. Alternatively, if the treatment effect is smaller than expected, the sample size increase will be insufficient, resulting in an overpowered trial, unable to reach meaningful conclusions. In other words, when nuisance parameters are assessed at the time of interim analysis in a blinded manner, it is impossible to determine for sure whether increased/decreased variability in the pooled results comes from changes in the treatment effect alone or if also. For this reason, blinded SSR methods are typically reserved for situations where there is reasonable confidence to believe that treatment effect assumptions will hold and only nuisance parameter assumptions must be reassessed at the interim analysis.
