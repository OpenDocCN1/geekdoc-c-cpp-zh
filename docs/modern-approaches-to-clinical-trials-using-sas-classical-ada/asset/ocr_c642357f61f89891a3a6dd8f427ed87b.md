# 9.5.4 Bayesian Response-Adaptive Randomization

In the aforementioned randomization methods, the allocation probability ratios only depend on the the point estimates of the response probabilities. However, at the early stage of a clinical trial, the variabilities of these point estimates are typically large due to the small sample size. To address this problem, there is usually a pre-phase of equal randomization at the beginning of a trial, such that more stable allocation probabilities can be attained. On the other hand, the Bayesian approaches can naturally incorporate the estimation variability in determining the randomization probabilities.

Specifically, let $p_1$ and $p_2$, denote the rates of the two treatments, respectively. Based on the accumulated data ($D$) in the trial, the posterior probability that treatment 2 has a higher response rate than treatment 2 is $\lambda=P(p_1>p_2|D)$.

By comparing the posterior distributions $p_1$ and $p_2$, $\lambda$ automatically accounts for both the point and variance estimates of the treatment response rates. Thall and Wathen [16] suggested randomizing each new treatment to treatment 1 through a power transformation,

$$\pi(\lambda\gamma) = \frac{\lambda^\gamma}{\lambda^\gamma+(1-\lambda)^\gamma}$$

where $0\le\gamma\le1$. If $\gamma=0$, the randomization scheme reduces to equal randomization regardless of the value of $\lambda$. In contrast to using a constant value for $\gamma$, the power parameter $\gamma$ may be allowed to depend on the accumulating sample size $n$, $\gamma=n/2N$, where $N$ is the maximum sample size planned for the study. We apply the Bayesian adaptive randomization to the previous example by using the macro `BayesianRandomize`. To implement the procedure, one needs to specify the following parameters:

+   1. $n_1,n_2,y_2$ are the total sample size and number of successes for treatment A and B, respectively.
2. $alpha1,beta1,alpha2,beta2$ are the hyperparameters in the beta prior
