A closer explanation of rule (3.2) and comparison with (3.1) reveals that the CHW method is a special case of a p-value combination method with $w_1 = \sqrt{n/N}$ and $w_2 = \sqrt{(N-n)/N}$. The type I error rate property of the CHW test statistic does not depend on what rule is used to determine the sample size $M$ per group and it does not require any cap on $M$. The original CHW paper suggests selecting sample size $M$ based on the conditional power argument or setting $M=g(\Delta_0/\Delta_L)^2$, where $\Delta_L$ is the estimated treatment effect at the interim analysis and $\Delta_0$ is the treatment effect used to determine the original sample size. Another way to select the sample size $M$ is to plug-in an interim estimate of treatment effect $\Delta_L$ into a sample size calculation formula for a normally-distributed endpoint. Regardless of how the sample size is selected, the type I error rate will be preserved as long as the sample size adjustment is the only modification.

The only difference between the naïve statistic $Z_M$ and the CHW statistic $\hat{Z}_M$ is that the result from the second part of the trial (i.e. $Z_{M-n}$) is down-weighted when $N < M$. This has led to numerous criticisms of CHW statistics as violating the principle of "all patients being equal". The same argument expressed in mathematical terms states that the CHW statistic $\hat{Z}_M$ is different from the sufficient statistics $Z_M$, therefore leading to a loss of efficiency. While the debate over the loss of efficiency has generated a lot of theoretical research and publications, in practice this loss is not huge, especially if the sample size re-estimation rule is implemented reasonably, e.g. without multiple fold increase from $N$ to $M$
