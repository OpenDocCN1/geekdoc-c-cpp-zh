
## 4. N: total sample size.

Likewise, the power of the subset test can be calculated using the macro by specifying these parameters in the sensitivity subset.

For example, if the expected probability of response for a patient receiving the experimental treatment is 0.5, and the expected probability of response for a patient receiving the control treatment is 0.2, assume the one-sided significance level is 0.05 and the total sample size is 100, then the power of the overall test is calculated by Example Code 11.4 Power for Adaptive Signature Design. In this example, the power is 94.3%.

## Example Code 11.4 Power for Adaptive Signature Design

```sas
%macro power(pe, pc, alpha, N);
data result;
pe=&pe;
pc=&pc;
alpha=&alpha;
N=N;
nb=1&alpha;
pbar=(pe+pc)/2;
za=quantile('Normal', b);
num=(pe-pc)-2*sqrt(pbar*(1-pbar)*4/N);
den=pe*(1-pe)*2/N+pc*(1-pc)*2/N;
alpha=num/den;
power=CDF('Normal', a);

run;

proc noobs;
var pe pc N alpha power;
run;
mend power;
%power(0.5, 0.2, 100);
```

The advantage of this design is its ability to de-risk losing the label of the broader population in pivotal trials. According to Freidlin and Simon [29], this design may be ideal to use phase II clinical trials for developing an assay or signatures for patients who respond better to targeted therapies. One drawback of this design is that only half of the patients are used for development or validation. If prevalence of the subgroup of biomarker sensitive patients is low then this design will require a large number of patients to be screened. This may not be economically feasible, and it will be operationally challenging and will extend the enrollment period. The other drawback is that with a larger number of sub-groups lead to high dimensional data, a large sample size may be needed to adequately power the trial.

### 11.4.2.2 Biomarker-Adaptive Threshold Design

In this design [32], the test for overall treatment effect is combined with the development and validation of a pre-specified cutpoint for a biomarker-based subgroup believed to be most sensitive to the targeted treatment. The statistical hypothesis test is carried out by splitting the overall type I error rate α. First, compare the treatment response on the overall population at α1, and if not significant, then perform the second test at α-1. For example, if the null hypotheses of no benefit (a) is rejected at a desired significance level of, say, 0.04, then the testing is stopped or else the testing is carried out to the hypothesis in (b) 0.01 to control an overall α below the 0.05. A simplified cutoff model proposed by Altman et al. [33] is used to develop the proposed procedure. The overall patient population is divided into two groups: biomarker-based (sensitive) and non-biomarker-based (non-sensitive). This categorization may result in some loss of information, but the proposed testing procedure is robust to departures from the simplified cut-off model. A permutation resampling approach is used for selection of the optimal cutpoint to avoid multiplicity problems [34].

An approach to sample planning for clinical trials when the treatment effect is restricted to a subpopulation is presented in Appendix B in [32]. Assume that in a trial with time-to-event outcomes, the required number of observed events for the overall test to detect the target treatment effect (at significance level α) with power 1-β could be calculated by events macro in Example Code 11.5 Number of Events for Biomarker-Adaptive Threshold Design.

## Example Code 11.5 Number of Events for Biomarker-Adaptive Threshold Design

```sas
%macro events(delta, alpha, beta, pi);
  data result;
    alpha=&alpha;
    power=1-&beta;
    delta=&delta;
    pi=&pi;
    za=quantile('Normal', 1-&alpha);
    zb=quantile('Normal', 1-&beta);
    logd=log(delta)/log(delta);
    D=4*(za+zb)/log(delta)**2;
    DS=D/(pi**2);

  label delta='hazard ratio';
  label pi='treatment effect fraction required';
  label Ds='events required';

run;

proc print noobs label;
    var alpha power delta pi Ds;
run;
%mend events;
%events(2, 0.05, 0.2, 0.8);
```

The parameters to be specified in the %events are:

- 1. delta: ratio of the hazard of the experimental arm over that in the control arm
- 2. alpha: significance level (one-sided type I error rate)
- 3. beta: false-negative rate (type II error rate)
- 4. pi: fraction of the population where treatment effect delta is limited; if treatment effect applies to the entire study population, pi=1.

For example, if the hazard ratio is 0.5, the treatment effect is limited to 80% of the overall population, the number of observed events required for a 80% power study (at a one-sided significance level of 0.05) is 80.4261, which would round up to 81 events (Example Code 11.5 Number of Events for Biomarker-Adaptive Threshold Design).

Biomarker development and validation is usually expensive and time consuming. Often a reliable biomarker is not available during the late phase of the trial. If the treatment effect is not detected in the overall population but an increased response is observed in the subgroup, another pivotal trial is required for regulatory approval. Ravaud et al. [35] presented a randomized study in renal cell patients. The overall survival showed no statistical difference p=0.29 with median 43.1 weeks of standard of care versus 46.9 weeks (Lapatinib). Among the subgroup of patients with over expression of EGFR, the overall survival rate was statistically significant p=0.02 with median 37.9 weeks of standard of care versus 46 weeks (Lapatinib).

In the scenarios outlined above, this design and statistical testing procedure can combine the formal hypothesis test for overall population with the uncertainty in the effect in the biomarker defined subset. There is a substantial gain in efficiency providing statistically rigorous phase evaluation of new therapies. The procedure preserves the overall type I error rate, in addition to giving the flexibility to detect a treatment effect in the overall population and at the same time providing a statistically valid test if the targeted therapy is beneficial to a biomarker based subset. This sequential and structured approach is carried out within a single randomized clinical trial, thereby negating the need for a pivotal trial. This design is appropriate when the marker is known but the threshold or the cutoff for defining the positive or negative biomarker based subgroup is unknown.

### 11.4.2.3 Cross-Validated Adaptive Signature Design

This design which is an extension of the adaptive signature design was proposed to optimize the efficiency of both the development and validation components of the adaptive signature design [36]. This design enables complete cross-validation of the entire sample available in the study. This procedure preserves the overall study-wise type I error rate and increases the statistical power to detect a clinically meaningful treatment difference in the subgroup of patients who benefit from the targeted therapy. Similar to the adaptive signature design, the initial null hypothesis to test the benefit of the targeted therapy against the control is conducted at a slightly lower significance level α than the overall α. If this hypothesis is rejected, the targeted therapy is declared superior to the control treatment for the overall population and analysis is completed. If the first hypothesis is not rejected; the signature component of the design is used select a potentially promising biomarker subgroup, also known as the sensitive subset. The sensitive subset is determined by developing the classifier using the full population. It is done by the following steps:
```
