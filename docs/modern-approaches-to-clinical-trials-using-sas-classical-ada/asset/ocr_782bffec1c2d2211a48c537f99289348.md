Baker Kramer [21] proposed a special case of biomarker analysis design for rare events. In this design, all subjects are randomized to either the targeted therapy or the control. The specimens are collected but not examined at the time of randomization. Subjects are randomly selected at the end of the trial to test for the presence or absence of the marker. The probability of random selection is ascertained based on the positive outcome of interest. King et al. [22] proposed testing for the marker only for the subjects with a positive outcome of interest. This type of design can be referred to as biomarker-nested case-control design.

# 11.2.2.5 Marker-Based Strategy Design

In this design, patients are randomly assigned to treatment dependent or independent of the marker status (Figure 11.3 Marker-Based Strategy Design). All patients randomized to the non-biomarker based arm receive the control treatment. In the biomarker based arm, the patients receive the targeted or experimental therapy if the marker is positive and they receive the control treatment if the marker is negative [6-7]. The outcome of all patients in the marker based subgroup is compared to that of all patients to investigate the predictive value of the marker. One downside of this design is that patients treated with the same regimen are included on both the marker-based and the non-marker-based subgroup resulting in a substantial redundancy, which leads to many patients receiving the same treatment in both groups. Hence, this design can reduce the treatment effect especially if the prevalence of the marker is high requiring a large sample size. This is illustrated in the following example, in ERCC1 trial [23] and presented in [6] about 57% of the biomarker-based strategy arm patients were assigned to the same regimen of cisplatin+docaxel as done in the standard of care arm. Thus, the comparison weakens between the treatment effect difference and the statistical power to reject null hypotheses. This can lead to either getting incomplete information or it may miss a valuable biomarker in addition to delaying the evaluation of the biomarker due to the increased sample size required to achieve a desired power. One disadvantage of this design is the inability to examine the effect of targeted therapy in patients in the negative status group as none of these patients receive it.
