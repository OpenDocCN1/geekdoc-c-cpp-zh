## 3.1 Introduction

### 3.1.1 Motivation. Why Sample Size Re-estimation?

Sample size re-estimation (SSR) has received a lot of attention in recent years as a result of exploding interest in adaptive clinical trial designs in general. In February 2010, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a draft guidance on Adaptive Clinical Trials for Drugs and Biologics, which defined an adaptive trial design as “a study that includes a prospectively planned opportunity for modification of one or more specified aspects of the study design and hypotheses based on analysis of data (usually interim data) from subjects in the study. Analyses of the accumulating study data are performed.

By definition, adaptive design can have many types of adaptations [1]. SSR occurs at prospectively planned time points, can be performed in either blinded or unblinded manner. Blinded SSR does not require knowledge of assignment, while unblinded SSR does.

When sample size is planned for clinical trials, it depends heavily on assumptions regarding effect size and nuisance parameters. There is usually a considerable degree of uncertainty in these parameters, which translates into a wide range of possibilities for sample size. Unfortunately, when these assumptions are violated, the power of the study is negatively affected. If the trial does not have interim analysis (i.e. fixed trial design), it is impossible to detect the gap between assumptions and reality until the study ends and all resources are used. SSR attempts to address this by looking at interim results. For example, in case of a normal end point with unknown variance, the blinded SSR procedure will estimate only the variance. The hypothesized mean difference along with the updated variance estimate will be used to calculate the adjusted sample size. In contrast, unblinded SSR will look at patient-level data and calculate both the treatment effect and variability estimates.

It is worthwhile to note that the SSR methodology is closely related to that of group sequential designs (GSD) discussed in Chapter 2. In fact, unblinded SSR was originally developed as an extension of GSDs. Because of this relationship, these two classes of adaptive design are often compared and contrasted. Even though GSDs satisfy the definition of adaptive design from FDA guidance described earlier, many people prefer to refer to them as classical rather than adaptive because their methodological development preceded that of unblinded SSR. There are important similarities and differences between GSD and unblinded SSR: the methodology for the latter builds upon the same distribution theory that was developed for GSDs (Chapter 2), allowing sponsors to adjust sample size upwards as well as downwards. In contrast, GSD only allows sample size reduction through early stopping. For that reason, the unblinded SSR methods are particularly appealing to developers with limited initial budgets. In such cases, the study can be initially small and additional sample size invested only if the interim results look promising [3]. Even if a larger budget is available upfront, more and more sponsors are exploring SSR options (both blinded and unblinded) in order to use their drug development resources efficiently.
