type I error under such a decision is controlled at a level less than α. If an observed p-value is large (p greater than α) and a No Go decision is made, then the probability of making a type II error is smaller than β.

In phase III, the selection of β reflects the level of commitment of moving the candidate product forward (a smaller β implies a stronger commitment commitment). It also represents the degree of investment that the sponsor is willing to spend. However, in phase II, the choice of β may need to be balanced with a choice of α. In most situations, β is set at a higher level than α. For example, a typical α may be 0.05, while a β is set at 0.02 (indicating 80% power). This does not necessarily have to be in the case in phase II designs. In practice, phase II studies are designed with an α of 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, or sometimes a one-sided value of 0.15. A β of greater than 0.2 is rare. But β values of 0.15, 0.1 or 0.05 are not uncommon. The project team must discuss and agree with the level of risk that the team is willing to take — give up a good product, or to develop a placebo. It is very important to note that these discussions should take place at the study design stage after seeing the study results, the coin has landed, and it would be too late to discuss probabilities or risks.

# 6.2.7.4 Sample Size Calculation

Once the statistical hypothesis, the clinical difference, and the corresponding α and β have been determined, sample size for a given dose-ranging trial can be calculated. Based on the above discussion, the sample size of a dose-ranging study can be calculated using the formula below [21]:

```
n = \left| \frac{(z_{1-\alpha} + z_{1-\beta})\sigma}{\delta} \right|^2 \sum_{i=0}^{k} \frac{\sigma^2}{f_i}
```

where

+   1. $n$ is the total sample size of all treatment groups combined

+   2. $z_{1-\alpha}$ and $z_{1-\beta}$ are the critical values of the standard normal distribution corresponding to type I and type II error, respectively
