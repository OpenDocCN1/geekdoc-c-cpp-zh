
Baker Kramer [21] proposed a special case of biomarker analysis design for rare events. In this design, all subjects are randomized to either the targeted therapy or the control. The specimens are collected but not examined at the time of randomization. Subjects are randomly selected at the end of the trial to test for the presence or absence of the marker. The probability of random selection is ascertained based on the positive outcome of interest. King et al. [22] proposed testing for the marker only for the subjects with a positive outcome of interest. This type of design can be referred to as biomarker-nested case-control design.

## 11.2.2.5 Marker-Based Strategy Design

In this design, patients are randomly assigned to treatment dependent or independent of the marker status (Figure 11.3 Marker-Based Strategy Design). All patients randomized to the non-biomarker based arm receive the control treatment. In the biomarker based arm, the patients receive the targeted or experimental therapy if the marker is positive and they receive the control treatment if the marker is negative [6-7]. The outcome of all patients in the marker based subgroup is compared to that of all patients to investigate the predictive value of the marker. One downside of this design is that patients treated with the same regimen are included on both the marker-based and the non-marker-based subgroup resulting in a substantial redundancy, which leads to many patients receiving the same treatment in both groups. Hence, this design can reduce the treatment effect especially if the prevalence of the marker is high requiring a large sample size. This is illustrated in the following example, in ERCC1 trial [23] and presented in [6] about 57% of the biomarker-based strategy arm patients were assigned to the same regimen of cisplatin+docaxel as done in the standard of care arm. Thus, the comparison weakens between the treatment effect difference and the statistical power to reject null hypotheses. This can lead to either getting incomplete information or it may miss a valuable biomarker in addition to delaying the evaluation of the biomarker due to the increased sample size required to achieve a desired power. One disadvantage of this design is the inability to examine the effect of targeted therapy in patients in the negative status group as none of these patients receive it. Even if the patients in the negative marker status group respond to the targeted therapy, this cannot be assessed. The treatment difference between the new treatment and the control treatment can be diluted by marker-based treatment selection and sometimes it can be a poor choice as compared to the randomized design.

A modified version of this design has been proposed where the negative marker status group undergo randomization and receive the targeted therapy or the control. Thus, the modified design allows assessment of the targeted therapy in both the biomarker positive and the negative subgroup. It helps to assess whether the efficacy of the marker positive patients to therapy is because of the marker status positive or to an improved treatment regardless of the marker status. The assessment can also be done retrospectively if the classification of the marker needs to be revisited.

## 11.2.2.6 Hybrid Design

Hybrid designs should be considered when there is strong evidence from pre-clinical or prior studies that there is efficacy of some treatment(s) on the marker-based sub-group [1]. This makes it almost impossible, due to ethical reasons, to randomize patients with a particular marker to other treatment options. It is very similar to the enrichment designs, and all patients are examined for marker status and randomly assigned to treatment or standard-of-care treatment for patients with positive biomarker values. However, only a marker-positive subgroup of patients are randomly assigned to treatments, whereas patients in the marker-negative group are assigned to a control or standard-of-care treatment (Figure 11.4 Hybrid Design). The study is powered to detect treatment difference only in the marker-positive group. Samples are collected from all of the subjects to help testing for additional markers in the future.

## 11.3 Efficiency of Classical Enrichment Designs

Simon and Maitournam [24] and Maitournam and Simon [25] considered a phase III randomized trial setting to evaluate molecular targeted treatment using a binary endpoint or continuous endpoint. The evaluations were done under the assumption that the assay was predictive of a subset of patients who benefited from the targeted therapy. They compared the all-comers design where all the patients are allowed to participate in the trial with the enrichment design with restricted subset population. They compared the operating characteristics based on average screened and randomized sample size.

For a binary endpoint, the relative efficiency of the targeted design versus the untreated design can be expressed as

```
$$\frac{n}{n^T} = \left[\frac{\zeta_1}{\zeta_0+(1-\gamma)\zeta_1}\right]^2 f$$
```

where $2nT$ and $2n$ denote the number of patients that are needed to randomize in the enrichment and all-comers design, respectively, to achieve the same statistical power for testing the null hypothesis. $\zeta_1$ and $\zeta_0$ represent the effect size (e.g. difference in response rate) of the treatment group versus the control group in the biomarker positive and biomarker negative groups. $\gamma$ accounts for the proportion of the biomarker negative group of the population, and $f$ is the relative efficiency factor close to 1 [24]. For the calculation of relative efficiency in light of the number of screened patients, simply multiply the equation by $(1-\gamma)$.

For a continuous endpoint, the relative efficiency of the targeted design versus the untreated design can be expressed in the form

```
$$\frac{n}{n^T} = \frac{1+\frac{\gamma(1-\gamma)}{2\sigma^2}}(\mu_1-\mu_0)^2+(\mu_T-\mu_0)^2}{1-\gamma(1-\frac{(\mu_T-\mu_0)^2}{\mu_T-\mu_1})^2}$$
```

where $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$ denote the mean responses for control patients in marker negative and positive groups, $\mu_{OT}$ and $\mu_{IT}$ denote the mean responses for treatment patients in marker negative and positive groups, respectively, and assume a constant variance $\rho^2$ within each treatment group and subset. Similarly, for relative efficiency of the screened patients, simply multiply the equation by $(1-\gamma)$. The above results are valid only asymptotically. Alternatively, Maitournam and Simon [25] derived sample size formulas that should be more accurate for small sample sizes using the Mann–Whitney Wilcoxon test.

Macros ($\beta_{norm}$ and $\beta_{sigma}$) are developed for calculating the sample size for a certain power based on formula (6) in [25].

```
Power = 1-\Psi\left(\frac{0.5n^2+z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{\frac{2(n+1)}{12}Var(W_{XY})}}-0.5n^2p_1\right)
```

$Var(W_{XY})$ is the variance of the Mann-Whitney statistic given by

$Var(W_{XY}) = n^2\{p_1(1-p_1) + (n-1)[p_2+p_3-2p_2^2]\}$, where $p_1=Pr(X<Y)$ is the probability that a control outcome $X$ is less than a treatment outcome $\gamma$, and $p_2=Pr[X<Y_1,X<Y_2]$ is the probability that a control outcome is simultaneously less than two independent treatment outcomes, and $p_3=Pr[X_1<Y,X_2<Y]$ is the probability that a treatment outcome is simultaneously greater than two independent control outcomes. $\Psi$ denotes the cumulative distribution function for the standard normal distribution. Please refer to $efficiency\_ct.sas$ in the supplemental material of the book.

The $\%MSpower$ macro (Example Code 11.1) computes the smallest sample size that provides a specified power $1-\beta$ for rejecting the null hypothesis using formula (6) in [25]. The parameters to be specified for $\\%MSpower$ are

- 1. beta: type II error rate

- 2. alpha: type I error rate

- 3. pi: probability that a control outcome is less than a treatment outcome
