For this reason, regulatory agencies do not strictly require any particular β to be used in any phase of the clinical studies. Hence, the choice of β is usually left for the sponsor to determine. Usually in phase III, sponsors tend to choose a smaller β (or a higher power) for study designs so that a larger sample size will increase the likelihood of meeting the decision criteria with more accurate estimates (i.e., narrower confidence interval around the estimates).

However, the choice of α or β in phase II designs can be flexible. The two main reasons are: (i) decisions after a phase II clinical trial are primarily a sponsor decision rather than a regulatory decision and, hence, the sponsor gets to select the level of risks that they are willing to take; (ii) there is insufficient evidence at phase II for the sponsor to commit a huge amount of investment. Accordingly, the sponsor might accept higher risks (i.e., the α or β being larger) and contain the level of investment directly associated to the maximum sample size.

When designing a phase II study, if σ has been determined, and the sample size is limited, then the only choice left with the project team would be to take a larger risk of making a type I error or a larger risk of making a type II error. This choice is critical because a discussion about probability of making a wrong decision should take place at the design stage, not after the study is unblinded and ready. From a frequentist point of view, the probability of observing a head or a tail of a coin can only be contemplated before the coin has landed. After the coin has landed, the observation is either a head, or a tail. There would be no probability associated with it. In clinical trials, the coin has landed when the blind is broken. At that time, clinical data are ready for statistical analysis, and statistics used to assess the Go/No criteria can be calculated (e.g., p-value, observed statistics, lower or upper limit of confidence interval). It is important to understand that a Go/No Go statistic (e.g., p-value, confidence interval) is an observed statistic with the data in hand, and there is no uncertainty associated with it. The underlying truth that the test product is in fact efficacious or not – is still unknown. However, a Go or No decision will have to be made.
