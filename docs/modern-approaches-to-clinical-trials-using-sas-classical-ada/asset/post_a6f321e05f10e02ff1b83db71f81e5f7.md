The most relevant assay is the one that identifies patients who are more responsive to the new treatment than the control. It is especially important when the control is active.

Maitournam and Simon [25] did extensive simulations assessing the relative efficiency of the enrichment design versus the all-comers design and conclude:

- When the treatment effect is limited to marker+ patients and the specificity of the assay is close to 1, the enrichment design is generally much more efficient than the all-comers design with regard to the number of randomized and number of screened patients. The efficiency decreases as the specificity of the assay degrades.

- If the treatment effect for the marker- patients is half as large as for the marker+ patients, the enrichment design requires more patients to be screened than the unselected design even if the assay is perfect. The increased number of screened patients for the enrichment design depends critically on the sensitivity of the assay. Under these conditions, there is no substantial advantage of the enrichment design in terms of the number of randomized patients unless the assay has specificity close to 1. – if that is the case, the selection of the preferred design will depend on other considerations such as relative costs of randomizing, safety and tolerability profile, and commercial value of a broader labeling indication.

## 11.4 Adaptive Enrichment Designs

It has been well recognized and documented that increased spending in pharmaceutical and biotech research does not reflect an increase of the success rate of clinical development. Woodcock [26] outlined some of the reasons for the declining success rate of pharmaceutical development namely (i) high bar for proving drug benefits due to diminished margin for improvement, (ii) increased costs and complexity potentially decreasing the ability to bring many candidates forward into the clinic, (iii) genetics and related disciplines are not yet fully developed, (iv) chronic diseases are harder for the study sponsor to choose easy targets for investigation and investment. The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) hosted a “Critical Path Initiative” to identify key scientific challenges underlying the medical product pipeline and then released a *Critical Path Opportunities List* in 2006, especially learning from prior experience and ongoing innovative clinical trials, and accumulated data. The innovation implies the use of adaptive design methods and the potential use of a Bayesian approach. Chow et al. [27] define “Adaptive design as a design that allows adaptations to trial and/or statistical procedures of the trial after its initiation without undermining the validity and integrity of the trial”. The PRMA Working Group paper by Gallo et al. [28] refer to “An adaptive design as a clinical trial that uses accumulating data to decide on how to modify aspects of the study as it continues, without undermining the validity and integrity of the trial.” In the past decade, the application of adaptive design methods has become very popular due to its flexibility and efficiency. However, this is at times gained at cost of increased complexity, logistical challenges. In most cases, intensive planning must to define the go/go criteria, the sponsor should have adequate resources to implement and execute. Given the evolution of the trials, some of the adaptive designs are:

- (i) group sequential design,
- (ii) sample size estimation design,
- (iii) dose-finding design,
- (iv) drop-out design,
- (v) adaptive randomization design,
- (vi) treatment-switching design,
- (vii) multiplicity design,
- (viii) biomarker selection design,
- (ix) enrichment design.

Here we will primarily discuss the designs related to the enrichment.

## 11.4.2 Frequentist Methods

### 11.4.2.1 Adaptive Signature Design

The classical designs involve only one biomarker and it is assumed that the cut-off is a priori. The signature design is used when the potential biomarkers are unknown; however, there is some evidence that the targeted therapy may work. The signature design is done by the following steps:

1. The study population is split into a training sub-sample and a validation sub-sample.
2. The training sub-sample is used to develop a model to predict the treatment difference between targeted therapy and control as a function of baseline variables.
3. This model is also called the benefit function, which is used to validate the results from the validation-sample.
4. The benefit function used with this design is the minimum odds ratio [29] for the treatment interaction with a pre-specified number of biomarkers and the significance level.
5. The risk difference is another benefit function that has been used which is the difference between the targeted therapy and control in the probability of response as a function of baseline variables [20,31].
6. The prediction is done for each subject in this sample and a predicted score is calculated to classify the subset as sensitive or non-sensitive. own7. The subgroup is selected using a pre-specified cut-off for this predicted score.
8. The second hypothesis test is conducted in this subgroup to see the benefit of the targeted therapy against the control. This test is conducted at a much lower significance level (example: 0.01 or 0.02).

If the initial hypothesis is rejected at the lower significance, the targeted therapy is declared superior to the control for the overall population. The test of the population is complete at this stage.

Generally, as the proportion of the sensitive patients increases, so does the difference in the objective response rates of the experimental arm and the control arm. Therefore, for prevalence above a certain threshold, the power of the overall effect test will dominate the power of the sensitive subset. The adaptive signature design preserves the ability to detect the overall effect in this case. However, its advantage over the traditional design (testing for overall effect with broad eligibility) is reduced. The formulas that can assist the investigators in assessing the power relation between the sensitive subset and the overall test are presented in [25].

The power macro is developed for calculating power based on formula (1.1) in [25]. When calculating the power of the overall test, the following parameters need to be specified:

- pe: the expected probability of response for a patient receiving the experimental treatment
- pc: the expected probability of response for a patient receiving the control treatment
- alpha: one-sided significance level
