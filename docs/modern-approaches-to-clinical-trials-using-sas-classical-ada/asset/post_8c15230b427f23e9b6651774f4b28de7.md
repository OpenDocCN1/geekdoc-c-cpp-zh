Prior to implementation of a phase I clinical trial, extensive simulation studies should be conducted to examine the operating characteristics of the design, so that the design parameters such as the skeletons and the prior distributions can be properly calibrated. A good design should have robust and acceptable behaviors under various toxicity scenarios. To investigate the practical performance of the CRM and BMA-CRM, we consider six dose levels with monotonically increasing toxicity. The target toxicity probability is $\phi=30\%$. We use a non-informative prior distribution for $\alpha_i \sim N(0,\sigma_i^2)$ and $\sigma_i^2=2$. The first cohort is treated at the lowest dose level, and the maximum sample size is 30. Scenario 1 of Simulation Study Comparing the BMA-CRM and CRM, Scenario 2 of Simulation Study Comparing the BMA-CRM and CRM, and Scenario 3 of Simulation Study Comparing the BMA-CRM and CRM summarize the true toxicity probability, the selection percentage, the number of patients treated at each level, the total number of observed DLTs, and the percentage of trial early stopping, where 1000 trials are replicated under each scenario. As expected, the BMA-CRM generates more robust dose-finding in comparison to the four individual CRMs using a single skeleton. Macros %CRMSIM %BMACRMSIM generate the results for Table 5.3 through Table 5.5 and are included in the supplemental material for the book available from the companion website.

The input parameters in the %CRMSIM macro are listed below.

- 1. skeleton stores the prespecified skeleton (first column) and the true toxicity probabilities (second column).
- 2. target is the target toxicity rate.
- 3. cohort is the total number of cohorts.
- 4. cohortsize is the number of patients in each cohort.
- 5. toxstop is the threshold probability to early terminate the trial if the lowest dose level is too toxic.
- 6. ntrial is the number of replications in the simulation.

As an illustration, we consider the CRM with skeleton 1 under scenario 1 in Example Code 5.3 Simulation of CRM Design.

# Example Code 5.3 Simulation of CRM Design

```
data skeleton;
input p ptrue;
datalines;
0.02 0.07
0.08 0.12
0.12 0.17
0.20 0.30
0.30 0.45
0.50 0.60;
run;
%CRMSim(skeleton,target=0.3,ncohort=10,cohortsize=3,toxstop=0.9,ntrial=10000);
```

The output table includes the following variables.

- 1. doseselect is the percentage of each dose level being selected as the MTD.
- 2. ntox is the average number of DLTs observed at each dose level.
- 3. ntrted is the average number of patients assigned to each dose level.
- 4. nstop is the percentage of early termination.

The BMACSIM macro has similar input and the same output variables as those of CRMSIM. BMACSIM requires users to create the skeletons and toxicity rates in different data sets (skeleton and truerate). In addition, users should specify the prior probability for each skeleton in the priormodel data set.

# 5.4 Fractional Continual Reassessment Method

Most of the dose-finding methods, including the aforementioned CRM and BMA-CRM, require the DLT outcomes to be ascertainable shortly after treatment. In other words, the toxicity outcomes of all the previously treated patients must be available in order to determine the next assignment. However, it may take a long period of time to measure the DLT outcomes after treatment. In a more common scenario, if the patient accrual is fast and the enrollment, missing toxicity data arise as the outcomes of the patients under treatment may still be unavailable. For example, in radiotherapy trials, DLTs occur long after the treatment. In a phase I trial with pancreatic cancer, the goal was to determine the MTD of oxaliplatin with combination with gemcitabine and the concurrent radiotherapy therapy [7]. The accrual rate was one patient every two weeks, whereas, took on average nine weeks to assess the toxicity outcome of each patient after the treatment. Consequently, at the moment of dose assignment for a newly arrived patient, the patients under treatment might not have completed the full assessment period and, thus, the non-toxicity outcomes could be considered missing or censored at that time.

To accommodate late-onset toxicity, we can model the underlying times to toxicity through the well-known Kaplan-Meier estimator [8,9]. An alternative approach to handling censored data is to redistribute the mass of each censored observation to the right [10,11]. Typically, there is a specified toxicity evaluation window to determine the DLT outcome. Within the evaluation window [0, $\tau]$, if a patient experiences the DLT, we take $y=1$; if a patient still has not experienced the DLT after time $\tau$, we take $y=0$. Due to continual decision making on dose assignment for each new cohort, patients currently under treatment may not have experienced the DLT when a new cohort of patients is ready for treatment. Under such circumstances, the toxicity event can be viewed as censored data, and we adopt the redistribution to the right approach by splitting the mass of 1 between the censoring point and beyond $\tau$ (anywhere that is larger than $\tau$). In the construction of the likelihood function, only the weight assigned to the censoring point within [0, $\tau$ makes the contribution, and the weight assigned beyond $\tau$ does not contribute to the likelihood. Thus, due to censoring, we can only count on fractional information of the censored data. In contrast, the naive method may simply discard the missing data as if no toxicity would ever occur in the future, which inevitably loses useful information for decision making.

To fully utilize the follow-up time information, more data need to be collected during the trial conduct, for example, patients' enrollment dates, decision-making dates (censoring times), and the dates of toxicity occurrences.
