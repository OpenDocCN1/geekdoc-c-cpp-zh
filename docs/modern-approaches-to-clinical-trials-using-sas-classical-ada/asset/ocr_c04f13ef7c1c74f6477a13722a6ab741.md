$p_C = 0.745 (placebo)$, implying $p_E - p_C = 0.172$. With these numbers, the target allocation proportion (unknown to the investigator at the outset) the AZT treatment is 0.525 for the RSIHR design and 0.607 for the compound optimal design. We use the total sample $n=477$ to match the real trial setup in [66]. In the simulation of two RAR designs, the first four patients are assigned to the AZT or placebo using the PBD with block size 4; afterwards the allocation is performed using the ERADE procedure with the corresponding target allocation. In our simulation, we assume each patient's outcome is observed before the next patient is enrolled in the study, and treatment randomization probabilities are updated sequentially by RAR algorithms. In practice, the outcome data from some patients may not be available yet at the time of the current patient's entry (i.e. responses are delayed), in which case one would just use the data accrued far thus to update parameter estimates and randomization probabilities. However, this scenario is not explored here.

Table 10.5 (supplemental program ch10.7.1.sas) shows the simulated operating characteristics of the four designs. All four designs achieved their target allocation and are very similar in terms of quality of estimation. The RSIHR design is more variable than CRD (empirical standard deviation of the allocation proportion for RSIHR, compound optimal, and CRD is 0.006, 0.033, and 0.023, respectively). On average, the RSIHR design assigns 12 more patients to the AZT treatment, and the compound optimal design assigns 53 more patients to the AZT treatment compared to the balanced randomization designs (CRD and PBD). Also, on average the RSIHR design results in 2 fewer treatment failures, and the compound optimal design results in 8 fewer treatment failures than the balanced designs. Therefore, in this example, the ethical gains of the RSIHR design are rather modest, whereas the compound optimal design provides the best balance between individual and collective ethics.
