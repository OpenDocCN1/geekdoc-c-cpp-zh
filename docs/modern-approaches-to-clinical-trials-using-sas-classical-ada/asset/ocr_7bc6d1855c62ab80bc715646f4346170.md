These include complete (simple) randomization, permuted block randomization, the maximal procedure [4], and biased coin designs [5]. On the other hand, there are a number of situations when a fixed unequal allocation may be preferred. For instance, the 2:1 allocation, where twice as many patients are assigned to the experimental treatment compared to the control, may have an ethical advantage over the 1:1 allocation if the experimental treatment turns out to be beneficial [6,7]. Unequal allocation may also be preferred for gaining more information on the safety profile of the experimental treatment, for economic efficiency, or for accounting for possible differential drop-outs of study patients [8]. Randomization procedures to implement fixed unequal allocation include, for example, permuted block designs and brick randomization [9].

In some trials, the choice of an allocation ratio is not straightforward. For instance, if variability of the outcome is different across the treatment arms, equal allocation may not be an optimal choice. In a two-arm trial, the allocation minimizing variance of the estimated treatment difference (Neyman allocation) is proportional to the standard deviations of the outcome are frequently unknown at the outset, how should one go about designing a trial in this case? On the other hand, at the trial design stage there may be some prior evidence (albeit definitive) suggesting that the experimental treatment will be an improvement over standard of care. In this case, the true equipo no longer exists and an investigator may want to consider a design with, say, 2:1 or 3:1 allocation ratio favoring experimental treatment. However, why a ratio of 2:1 or 3:1? Such choices are based on the investigator's expectation of the treatment effect which is a judgment call. These examples of clinical trials where statistical efficiency and ethics are of central concern highlight that equal or unequal allocation designs may lack flexibility. This motivates the introduction of response-adaptive randomization into the trial design.
