# 10.1.1 Randomized Clinical Trials

The most common goal of a randomized clinical trial (RCT) is to compare the effects of one or more experimental treatments versus control treatment (standard of care) in the target patient population. Randomization plays a fundamental role in the RCT design. By introducing a deliberate element of chance into the assignment of treatments, one increases the likelihood that important patient characteristics (known and unknown) that may affect the outcome of interest are similarly distributed across the treatment groups, and therefore, any observed treatment difference should be attributed to the treatment effects, not the effects of study patients [1]. Importantly, randomization by itself is not sufficient to achieve the goals of a RCT and it should be complemented by other techniques such as masking, allocation concealment, stratification or adjustment of randomization for covariates (when appropriate), covariate-adjusted analysis and the careful conduct of the trial [2].

When designing an RCT, it is essential to formulate the primary scientific question to be answered in the trial. This requires careful consideration of the disease, the target patient population, the choice of experimental and control treatments, and the primary outcome variable. This primary question is frequently questioned in the form of testing a hypothesis of the difference of treatment effects. The trial should have sufficient sample size to provide a reliable answer to the primary question [3]. One can also include additional research questions that are less important but still within the scope of the trial) as secondary objectives.

Once the trial objectives are formalized, there are several important statistical considerations, including but not limited to: 1) the choice of the target allocation ratio; 2) the choice of the randomization procedure; 3) the choice of the study sample size; 4) the choice of statistical methodology for data analysis. Let us consider the first two items in more detail. Many RCTs employ equal allocation designs, such as 1:1 allocation for a two-arm study. Equal allocation is frequently recommended in practice because it often maximizes the power of statistical tests and it is also consistent with the view of clinical equipoise at the beginning of the trial [3, pg. 98].
