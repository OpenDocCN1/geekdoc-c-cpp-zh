# 9.5.2 Randomized Play-the-Winner Rule

The original play-the-winner rule as discussed above lacks randomness because each treatment assignment is fully determined by the outcome of the previous patient, regardless of the outcomes of other patients in the trial. Such a deterministic rule would increase the potential for selection bias. As a remedy, Wei and Durham introduced the randomized version of the play-the-winner rule; that is, to switch to the other treatment with a higher probability in the case of observing a failure outcome, instead of a definitive switch [11]. As a well-known example, the Michigan extracorporeal membrane oxygenator (ECMO) trial utilized such a scheme to allocate infants to conventional therapy or the ECMO [12].

The randomized play-the-winner rule can be illustrated with an urn model (Figure 9.3 Urn Model Under Randomized Play-the-Winner Rule). Suppose that there are two types of balls, marked as A or B, in the urn. Starting with $m$ balls of each type, a ball is drawn randomly with replacement to determine the treatment for a new patient. If the ball of type A, the subject is assigned to arm A, and if the response is a success, additional $\alpha$ balls of type A and $eta$ balls of type B are added to the urn, with $\alpha \ge eta \ge 0$. Therefore, the next ball to be drawn would have a higher probability

$Pr(\text{Treatment A}) = \frac{\alpha+m}{\alpha+\beta+2m} \ge 0.5$,

to be a ball of type A, and the next patient would be more likely to be assigned to arm A. On the other hand, if the response of the subject that is assigned to arm A is a failure, additional $eta$ balls of type A and $\alpha$ balls of type B are added to the urn, such that the next ball to be drawn would have a lower probability of

$Pr(\text{Treatment A}) = \frac{\beta+m}{\alpha+\beta+2m} \le 0.5$,

to be a ball of type A, and so is the probability of the next patient assigned to arm A.
