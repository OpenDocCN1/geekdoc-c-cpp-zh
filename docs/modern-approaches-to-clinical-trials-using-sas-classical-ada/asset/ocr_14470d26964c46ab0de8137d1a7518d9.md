For example, in case of a normal end point with an unknown variance, the blinded SSR procedure will estimate only the variance using interim data. The originally hypothesized mean treatment difference along with the updated variance estimate will be used to calculate the adjusted sample size. In contrast, unblinded SSR will look at the patient-level unblinded data at the time of the interim analysis and calculate both the treatment effect and variability estimates, using both in the adjusted sample size calculations.

It is worthwhile to note that the unblinded SSR methodology is closely related to that of group sequential designs (GSDs) discussed in Chapter 2. In fact, unblinded SSR was originally developed as an extension of GSDs. Because of this relationship, these two classes of adaptive design are often compared and contrasted.

Even though GSDs satisfy the definition of adaptive design from FDA guidance described earlier, many people prefer to refer to them as classical rather than adaptive because their methodological development preceded that of unblinded SSR. There are important similarities and differences between GSD and unblinded SSR: the methodology for the latter builds upon the same distribution theory that was developed for GSDs (Chapter 2), allowing sponsors to adjust sample size upwards as well as downwards. In contrast, GSD only allows sample size reduction through early stopping. For that reason, the unblinded SSR methods are particularly appealing to developers with limited initial budgets. In such cases, the study can be initially small and additional sample size invested only if the interim results look promising [3]. Even if a larger budget is available upfront, more and more sponsors are exploring SSR options (both blinded and unblinded) in order to use their drug development resources efficiently.
