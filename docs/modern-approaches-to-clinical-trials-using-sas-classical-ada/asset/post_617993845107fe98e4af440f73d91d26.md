
## 6.2.3 Determining the Dose Range to Study

In non-adaptive design practice, usually the first dose ranging study covers a wide dose range, and then the next dose ranging study will be designed with a narrower dose range, filtering out the target doses to be assessed in phase III. Dose range in a clinical trial is defined as the ratio of the highest dose divided by the lowest dose included in the design. For example, if the doses in trial A are placebo, 20 mg, 40 mg, and 80 mg, then the dose range is 4 (= 80/20). If the doses in trial B are placebo, 0.1 mg, 1 mg and 10 mg, then the dose range is 100 (= 10/0.1). Although the doses used in trial A are higher than doses used in trial B, in fact trial B has a much wider dose range – 25 times wider than trial A. It is critical to cover a wide dose range in the first dose ranging study. Inappropriate selection of dose range may result in a potential waste of costs and resources and delay the time to market launch.

The key lesson learned from the above particular development program is that a very low dose was not explored in early phase II. In other words, if a dose at or below 10 mg was explored in the first study, a narrower dose range could have been established using the second study. Then the phase III studies could be designed with the appropriate dose range.

In general, the most difficult challenge in designing the first dose ranging trial in developing a new intervention is the selection of doses to be included. Generally, the study is designed with two very important assumptions:

- 1. The MTD was correctly estimated from earlier trials
- 2. Efficacy response is monotonic to ascending doses.

However, it is unclear what range of doses the efficacy activities would be in. At this stage, the best guidance available is the data obtained from pre-clinical experiments or from phase I using biomarkers. Even under the assumptions that the MTD was correctly estimated, and there is a monotonic efficacy dose-response relationship, there can still be many possible shapes of dose-response curves.

Given the discussion in the previous sections about MinED, it is critical to realize that in the first dose-ranging design, a wide dose range needs to be studied. The high dose is typically set at MTD or a dose below MTD to avoid any unacceptable toxicity. The real design challenge is to choose the lowest dose for this dose-ranging trial. If there is an interest in identifying a dose that is below MinED, then the difficult question would be “how low would be low enough?” Once the low dose is selected for the design, a dose range can be calculated as the ratio of the highest dose divided by the lowest dose.

## 6.2.4 Was the Concept Proven?

Sometimes it is desirable to combine the PoC and the dose-ranging studies into single study with clearly defined objectives, hypotheses, and decision criteria. The advantage of such a design is to first make a “Go/No Go” decision on PoC. If the decision is “Go”, then the same study would sequentially provide dose-ranging information to identify the dose that could achieve the target treatment effect and help design the next study. When well designed and analyzed, the combined saves development time by providing a range of efficacious doses going forward. The disadvantage is that more investment is at risk before the concept is proven.

When a simple PoC is examined under a single hypothesis test, there is not much discussion about PoC study design with regard to statistical design principles. In contrast, there are a variety of design and analysis strategies for dose-ranging trials. There exist three major categories of dose-ranging methods: multiple comparison procedures (MCP); modeling approaches; and MCP-Mod, which combines the first two [13,14].

The MCP method takes dose as a discrete factor and generally makes few assumptions about the underlying dose-response relationship. When there is no assumed ordering imposed on the testing of multiple doses, multiplicity adjustment is necessary to protect the family-wise-error rate (FWER). Popular procedures such as the Bonferroni, Dunnett, Holm and Hochberg require α splitting, which results in an increased sample size. The modeling approach treats dose as a continuous variable and assumes a functional relationship between the response and the dose. Sometimes the multiplicity is avoided as a result of the assumed relationship (e.g., non-decreasing dose-response relationship). The problem is that potential bias can be caused by model misspecification. A third approach, the MCP-Mod, addresses this issue by first selecting the dose-response model using MCP to protect the FWER; then the selected model is used to estimate the dose-response relationship. This approach is flexible in the sense of model selection; however, it pays the price of α at the model selection step.

## 6.2.5 How to Space the Test Dosages

Hamlett et al. proposed to use a binary dose spacing (BDS) for dose allocation, since this method of dose allocation is based on dividing dose intervals into halves [15]. Over the years, BDS has been applied successfully in many dose ranging designs [12,16]. In order to determine the doses using the BDS approach, it is assumed that the MTD is $\tau$, thus the dose space is $[0, \tau]$. Without loss of generality, $\tau$ can be taken as 1. It is assumed that the dose-response relationship is monotonic. It is further assumed that the number of doses is known. Given this setting, doses are chosen from the interval $[0,1]$. Suppose we want a design with three treatment groups, including placebo, a low dose and a high dose. The placebo dose is taken to be zero. The challenge then is to select a low dose and a high dose, keeping in mind that we may not want a high dose close to 1 (the assumed MTT). An intuitive approach might be to split this interval into two $[0,1/2]$ and $(1/2,1]$ and select a dose in each of these intervals. A natural choice is to select the midpoint of each interval (split each interval in half), giving the doses $x_1=1/4$ and $x_2=3/4$. Note that if we choose doses in the upper interval (greater than 1/2 and approaching 1) then these doses will be toxic and are generally not of interest in the dose-selection process. On the other hand, activities of the lower doses are very important information for drug development. Hence, the basic idea for BDS is to search for the lower end of the dose range.

In another case, suppose we want to consider a design with four treatment groups including a placebo, low, medium and high doses. In order to avoid selecting too high a dose, which can be toxic, we may want to keep $3/2$ as the high dose; that is, leave the interval $(1/2,2]$ unchanged. Since we want to use some low doses to help identify the MinED, we can divide the lower interval $[0,1/2]$ and $[1/2,1]$ in half, giving the midpoint of these two intervals and split the two intervals into halves — giving the three doses $x_1=1/8, x_2=3/2^3$ and $x_3=3/22$.
