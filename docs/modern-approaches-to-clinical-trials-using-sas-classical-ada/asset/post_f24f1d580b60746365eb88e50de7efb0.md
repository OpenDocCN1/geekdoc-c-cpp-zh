### 1.1 Introduction

Clinical testing of a drug to support its authorization is often characterized by four phases. Here, we use the word "drug" broadly to include biologic. Three of the phases are before the drug is marketed (pre-marketing) and one is afterwards (post-marketing). During the first phase (phase I), researchers investigate the drug’s absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion. Investigation is typically conducted in healthy volunteers, except for cytotoxic drugs, phase I is often conducted in patients with therapeutic options due to anticipated toxicity and uncertainty about a drug. When a drug is designed to induce a certain response, phase I can sometimes investigate what the drug does. Phase I usually consists of single-dose and multi-dose escalations to understand common reactions and dose-limiting toxicities. If the drug’s safety profile is judged to be acceptable at this stage, the development will progress to the second stage (phase II). The number of volunteers included in phase I ranges between 20 and 80, but could be higher if phase I includes an assessment of the drug’s mechanism of action.

The second phase focuses on a drug’s efficacy in patients with the targeted disorder. Clinical trials at this stage are also designed to determine dose(s) whose benefit-risk profile warrants further investigation. Multiple doses within the dose range identified from phase I are typically studied during this phase. Occasionally, a sponsor may have to conduct more than one study if the doses chosen in the initial dose-response study are not adequate to estimate dose-response relationship. This could occur if the doses selected initially are too high (e.g., near the dose-response curve). To reduce the chance of having to repeat a dose-response study, it is generally recommended to include 4-7 doses in a wide dose range.

Phase II is typically the time when researchers first learn about the beneficial effect of a drug. It also has the highest attrition rate among the three pre-marketing phases. Therefore, if a drug is not a viable candidate, it is best to recognize that as soon as possible. fewer regulatory requirements at this stage offer opportunities for flexible thinking. For example, some developers have divided Phase II into two stages. The first stage tests the proof of concept (POC) of the drug, using a high dose (e.g., the maximum tolerated dose identified in phase I) to investigate a drug's efficacy. If the drug does not demonstrate a clinically meaningful efficacy compared to the control in the POC study, there will be no need to conduct a dose-response study. Otherwise, the drug will be further tested in a dose-ranging study. This two-step process is often referred to as phase Ia and phase Ib. To streamline work that is required to initiate studies and obtain approvals from multiple boards, some sponsors combine POC and dose-response studies in one protocol with an unblinded interim analysis at the end of the POC stage. The sponsor will review results from the POC stage but use only data from the second stage to estimate the dose-response relationship. This strategy has the potential to reduce the so-called “white space” between phase Ia and phase Ib where the POC would be fully evaluated first and then dose-response study would be planned.

Depending on the target disorders, phase II traditionally consists of 100-300 patients. Despite strong advocacy by researchers [2] to use a modeling approach to analyzing dose-response data, some sponsors continue to rely on pairwise comparisons to design and analyze dose-response studies. There has been renewed emphasis that the selection of dose(s) is an estimation problem, and this problem could be addressed more efficiently by using a modeling approach [3]. In addition, Pinheiro et al. have shown that 300 patients in a dose-ranging study may not be enough to adequately identify the optimal dose based on a pre-criterion [4].

If a drug meets the efficacy requirement and passes the initial benefit-risk assessment, it will be further tested to confirm its efficacy. This is the final stage of clinical testing before most drugs receive regulatory approval for marketing. This phase (phase III) enrolls a greater number of patients who are more heterogeneous in their demographic and baseline disease status. It is also at this stage that the majority of pre-marketing safety data are collected. Since a major objective of phase III is to confirm a drug's effect, analyses focus on testing pre-specified hypotheses with adequate control for the chance of making an erroneous claim of a positive drug effect. Operations at this stage require protecting a trial's integrity so that trial results could be interpreted with confidence. The number of patients included at this stage typically ranges between 1,000 and 5,000. Drugs for orphan diseases will enroll much fewer patients than drugs that are designed to reduce the risk of a clinical endpoint which may require thousands, if not tens of thousands, of patients. In addition, more patients will be needed if the drug is developed for multiple disorders simultaneously. An example for developing multiple indications simultaneously is antibiotics.

After a drug's effect is confirmed and benefit-risk assessment supports its use in the target population, the manufacturer of the drug will file a marketing application with regulatory agencies, typically in multiple countries. Nearly all applications are for the adult population initially.
