
### 3.1.2 Overview of Considerations and Issues

When considering a trial design involving SSR, one has to be aware of potential issues (statistical, regulatory, and implementation) that are associated with such designs. The specifics of these issues differ quite a bit between blinded and unblinded SSR procedures; therefore, we will discuss them in detail within their respective sections. In general, the core statistical issue for all methods is type error control from multiple looks (interim and final) at the same data. The unblinded SSR procedure is more prone to type error inflation than blinded SSR. Regulatory issues revolve primarily around classification of adaptive designs by FDA guidance [1] into “well-understood” (blinded SSR) and “less well-understood” (unblinded SSR). This classification is based on the maturity of the methodology and consequent regulatory experience with submissions involving each type of SSR. This classification is also closely related to implementation challenges: with unblinded SSR, there is always the risk of an accidental interim result leak or treatment effect reverse-engineering that results in compromised study integrity. Reverse-engineering refers to the ability of someone familiar with the interim decision algorithm to make guesses about the observed treatment effect based on the interim decision that was made. The blinded SSR procedure is less affected by such concerns. Because unblinded SSR methods are still considered “less well-understood”, additional planning time for regulatory consultation should also be factored-in when considering such studies.

Other than budgetary considerations discussed in [3], Chuang-Stein et al. [4] describe several scientific reasons why SSR may be desirable. These include (1) lack of information on how a new product candidate may affect a long-term clinical endpoint in the target population; (2) a newly approved product have become part of a treatment regimen to which a product candidate under development will be added; (3) emerging new medical or diagnostic practice that renders information from historical trials out of date; (4) newly issued regulatory guidance that departs from previous endpoint selection and statistical approaches. In addition, confirmatory trials are increasingly designed to be global trials to support global product registration. Depending on where phase II trials were conducted, a sponsor may face a situation where data from some geographic regions will contribute substantially to the registration trials, but no prior information is available on how patients in these regions respond to the product candidate.

For the above reasons, it is often prudent to use interim data to check the validity of the assumptions made at the design stage. If the assumptions appear to be erroneous, one should consider making a midcourse sample size adjustment to improve the chance that the trial will reach a definitive conclusion.

The main focus of this chapter will be unblind SSR methodology, though we will give a brief overview of blinded methods in 3.2 Blinded SSR Methods, in order to compare and contrast with unblinded SSR methods in 3.3 Unblinded SSR Methods. We conclude with summary and discussion in Section 3.4.

## 3.2 Blinded SSR Methods

Blinded SSR does not require knowledge of the treatment assignment at the individual patient level. It is designed to modify sample size due to inaccurate assumptions of the variability associated with a continuous endpoint or an assumed background event rate in the control arm for trials with a binary endpoint. Even though the treatment assignment information is very helpful in assessing such parameters, methods have been developed to allow their estimation without the actual treatment assignment information.

Because blinded SSR does not involve treatment unblinding and can be conducted without worrying about introducing bias into a trial, blinded SSR is classified as a “well-understood” adaptive feature in the FDA guidance. From a practical standpoint, it is also easier to implement than unblinded SSR. For example, there is no potential risk of trial integrity loss due to accidental interim results, and no independent data monitoring committee (DMC) required. However, this simplicity comes at a cost: most blinded SSR methodologies rely on initially assumed treatment effect to value. In case of the actual treatment effect being larger than planned, these methods can mistakenly infer that the variance is larger than expected and increase the sample size unnecessarily. Alternatively, if the treatment effect is smaller than expected, the sample size increase will be insufficient, resulting in an overpowered trial, unable to reach meaningful conclusions. In other words, when nuisance parameters are assessed at the time of interim analysis in a blinded manner, it is impossible to determine for sure whether increased/decreased variability in the pooled results comes from changes in the treatment effect alone or if also. For this reason, blinded SSR methods are typically reserved for situations where there is reasonable confidence to believe that treatment effect assumptions will hold and only nuisance parameter assumptions must be reassessed at the interim analysis.

For the case of a two-treatment comparison of a normal endpoint, there are a few methods available to estimate the variance (nuisance parameter) in a blinded manner. The most popular is the one-sample variance method [5]. This method computes the overall estimate of variability from two treatment groups (active and control) essentially ignoring the different treatment assignments. For simplicity we discuss the case of equal allocation to treatment groups. For two normal samples with (unknown) means $\mu_{trl}$ and $\mu_{ctrl}$, the common variance $\sigma^2$ (nuisance parameter) can be estimated as follows:

$$\hat{\sigma}^2_{OS} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{ij} - \bar{X})^2$$

where $n$ is an interim per-group sample size and $X_{ij}$ is the observed interim data with observed $\bar{X}$. The key idea here is that the overall variability is a sum of between- and within-group variability, and the former is substantially smaller in clinical trials than the latter. Even though the above estimator of variance $\hat{\sigma}^2_{OS}$ is ignoring the between-treatment variability component (and therefore is biased upward when treatment difference is non-zero), it is still a practically useful estimate because the impact of this bias is small for typical noise-to-signal ratios encountered in clinical trials.

A natural extension of this method correcting for the bias is given in an adjusted one-sample variance method [6]:

$$\hat{\sigma}^2_{adj} = \hat{\sigma}^2_{OS} - \frac{n}{2(2-n)}\Delta^2$$

The magnitude of correction depends on the interim sample size $n$ and the value of $\Delta = \mu_{trl} - \mu_{ctrl}$, where $\Delta$ is the hypothesized treatment effect for which the study was powered. Following the interim analysis, the originally planned sample size $N=f(\alpha, 1-\beta, \sigma^2, \Delta)$ is replaced by $M=f(\alpha, 1-\beta, \sigma^2, \Delta)$ where $\sigma^2$ is calculated as $\sigma^2_{adj}$ or $\sigma^2_{OS}$ and function ($f$) refers to standard sample size calculations for a two-sample t test. Alternatively, a restriction $M<N$ is placed on the sample size adjustment requiring that the new sample size is $max(M,N)$, resulting in a restricted design. The operating characteristics (power and type error) depend very much on which version (restricted or unrestricted) is specified. It has been shown by simulation that blinded SSR trials using unrestricted design can lead to slight power losses and negligible type error inflation [5]. They suggest using the more conservative one-sample variance method or using a restricted design to prevent inflation.
