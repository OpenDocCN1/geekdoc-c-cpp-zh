In more general settings, RAR can provide more substantial improvements, both in terms of statistical efficiency and ethics, over equal allocation randomization [17-19,31]. One important topic that we have not discussed in this chapter is a combination of RAR with group-sequential designs that allow stopping the trial early for futility (if there is lack of treatment effect) or efficacy (if treatment effect is pronounced). Some authors theoretically showed advantages of group-sequential RAR designs [68-71].

It is important to note that RAR designs are operationally more complex and require more upfront planning than designs with fixed randomization schedules. RAR is applicable in trials where the recruitment period is relatively long and the outcomes can be observed quickly; the data must be of high quality to mitigate the potential for bias throughout the trial conduct. See [2,22,45] for further considerations on implementing RAR designs in practice. In conclusion, if used judiciously, optimal RAR designs can be useful in randomized comparative clinical trials with objectives primarily related to statistical efficiency and ethics.
