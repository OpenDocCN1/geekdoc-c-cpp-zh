Conversely, in an unrestricted blinded SSR, a decrease in the actual treatment difference leads to reduced variance estimate, and a potential decrease in the sample size compared to the original planned size. These changes are the opposite to those that are actually needed. This can happen because blinded estimate of variance essentially ignores the between-treatment difference in its calculation. They are efficient only when there is a reason to believe that actual treatment difference is not too far from what was assumed at the design stage. In situations where there is great uncertainty about the underlying treatment effect, the unblinded SSR procedure may be well worth the additional effort to implement.

A second reason to consider an unblinded SSR procedure is that a sample size modification may not be the only objective of an interim analysis. In other words, other types of adaptation may be of interest. In more and trials routinely implement early stopping for efficacy or futility (e.g. classical GSD), both of which would require unblinding. If unblinding is already considered in the study plan, then using an unblinded SSR is a lot more precise than a blinded SSR. In fact, many unblinded SSR methodologies evolved as an adaptation to increase sample size on top of GSD-like boundaries for early stopping, i.e., as a way to expand the list of adaptation options available at the time of interim analysis. In general, it is very hard to get an overall recommendation on when to use one adaptation versus another. Each trial should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, preferably by simulation that includes a good variety of reasonable treatment effect/variance scenarios. Based on the resulting operating characteristics and the likelihood of underlying assumptions, a final choice between blinded vs unblinded SSR procedures should be made.
