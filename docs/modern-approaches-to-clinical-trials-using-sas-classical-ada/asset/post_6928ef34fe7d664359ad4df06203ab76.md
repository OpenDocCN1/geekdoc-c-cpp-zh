# 3.5 Summary and Conclusions

Several different methods for blinded and unblinded sample size estimations were presented in this chapter for normally distributed data. Blinded methods reviewed included the one-sample variance method and the adjusted one-sample variance method. Unblinded SSR methods included Fisher’s combination test, the inverse normal method, CHW procedure, the promising zone design and conditional type I error preserving procedures (Proscan and Hansberger, Müller and Schäfer). With the exception of the promising zone and the Müller and Schäfer procedure, all the other unblinded methods can be viewed as a special form of a combination test.

These procedures can be extended to the case of a binary endpoint based on the asymptotic normal approximation of binomial distribution in situations where such approximation is appropriate. In cases where exact binomial distribution has to be used, the problem of SSR becomes more complicated and was not investigated within this chapter.

Except for the promising zone and the Proscan and Hansberger methods, unblinded SSR approaches reviewed were presented under the assumption that sample size adjustment is the only decision to be made at the interim analysis. i.e. to focus on the concept of SSR under each method). In practice, other decisions such as early stop for utility or efficacy accompany SSR. For more real-life situations, the approaches presented in this section remain generally applicable, but the type I and type II error rate calculation needs to be adjusted to reflect opportunities for early stopping.

When a design includes the opportunity for an early efficacy stop, the need to preserve the overall type I error rate is well accepted. Early stopping due to utility is more subtle. It is common knowledge that stopping for utility can reduce the false positive rate, making it possible to use a higher significance level at the final analysis while maintaining the overall type I error rate at the desired level [38]. While the latter is theoretically justified, regulators are unwilling to accept a higher significance level at the final analysis due to a planned interim futility analysis because futility rules are not binding and may not be rigorously enforced by a sponsor.

Many original manuscripts describing the unblinded SSR methodology focus solely on type I error control, because it is the most important topic to regulators (at least in a confirmatory trial setting). When considering SSR, one has to address not only type I error control but also the rules to select the new sample size and to estimate the treatment effect at the end of the trial. We did not get the opportunity to address these issues in detail within this chapter. Some general considerations are outlined below.

**Sample size.** With the exception of the promising zone design, a user is free to choose the method to re-estimate the sample size. It is commonly accepted to revise the sample size based on the desired conditional power (similar to how promising zone does). Even though in theory the sample size $M$ does not have to be restricted, in practice most trials with unblinded SSR employ a cap on the maximum allowable sample size increase. The competing forces in selecting a maximum allowable sample size (MASS) are obvious. To maximize the chance of success in a trial (purely in terms of statistical power) we would select a value for the MASS that is as large as possible. This could potentially lead to increased costs and duration and carry a regulatory risk of achieving statistical significance for a treatment difference that is not clinically relevant. On the other hand, selecting too small a value for MASS could make the SSR effort useless, because power would not be maintained in situations of early departures from the assumptions used in the original sample size planning. As statisticians, we need to ensure that the selected value of the MASS is large enough to ensure the usefulness of a proposed SSR plan, while keeping in mind the implications of a large size increase to trial operations and interpretation.

**Estimation.** In a two-stage design, naïve estimates from Stage 1 and Stage 2 are unbiased. But because the Stage 2 sample size is determined by using Stage 1 data, the naïve estimation approach from both stages yields a biased estimate [39]. Final inference following a trial with unblinded SSR remains an active area of research. For some methods analytical solutions exist and for others simulation remains the only way of quantifying the bias these reasons the authors did not attempt to give a comprehensive review of estimation methods here. With exception of the promising zone design, for most methods with a normal endpoint treatment effect estimation is usually accomplished by relying on the duality between Cls and a hypothesis test. Barnath and coauthors [19] extended the repeated confidence interval approach of [20] to flexible designs. The promising zone design stands out from the other methods because it specifies the condition under which it can be used and does not require users to modify the test statistic or rejection region in the final analysis. The latter has an appealing property of simplicity. For example, one can use the standard Wald statistic for final inference. All associated decisions that one has to make are “organically built into the promising-zone design,” including the new sample size selection rule and early utility/efficacy stop boundaries [3]. We would generally recommend the promising zone design, but with one word of caution. Because of the very feature of simplicity mentioned above, the duality property between hypothesis testing and confidence intervals is lost and treatment effect estimation at the end of such trials remains an open problem. For trials implementing the promising zone design, we suggest using simulation to carefully assess the amount of bias in treatment effect estimates at the end of trial on a case-by-case basis.
