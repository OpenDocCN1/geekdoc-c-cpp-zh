## 2.1 Introduction

One of the major advances in modern clinical trials is the invention and application of sequential analysis in experimental design. This type of design is often called the group sequential design. A group sequential design consists of multiple stages and employs a repeated significance testing strategy. It is one of the most commonly used adaptive designs. The Encyclopaedic Companion to Medical Statistics [1] defines sequential analysis as “a method allowing hypothesis tests to be conducted on a number of occasions as data accumulate through the course of a trial. A trial monitored in this is usually called a sequential trial.” For example, in a phase III randomized clinical trial that has several stages; an interim analysis is planned at each stage. An interim analysis is intended to assess the treatment effect with respect to efficacy or safety based on accumulated data at a planned time prior to the completion of a clinical trial. Based on results from the interim analysis, such a given test statistic is compared to a critical boundary, a decision can be made either to stop to reject the null hypothesis of no treatment effect, to accept the null hypothesis, or to continue on the next stage.

There are three different types of group sequential designs: (1) early efficacy stopping design permitting only early claiming of efficacy, (2) early futility stopping design permitting only claiming of futility, and (3) early efficacy or utility stopping design permitting either an efficacy or futility claim. Based on prior knowledge, if we believe that the test treatment is promising, an early efficacy stopping design should be used. If we are concerned that the test treatment may not work, an early futility stopping design should be employed.

In clinical studies, there is great interest in sequential procedures for ethical, economic, and administrative reasons and the methodology has a role to play in all phases of clinical research. Therefore, this type of trial design is attractive to clinical scientists and researchers because it provides a method to add flexibility to the analysis of ongoing trial data. The most compelling reason for monitoring trial data is, ethically, it is desirable to terminate or modify a trial when evidence has emerged concerning the particular hypothesis of interest. Armitage [2] argued that ethical considerations demand a trial to be stopped as soon as there is clear evidence that one of the treatments is to be preferred and that this leads to the use of sequential trial. For a trial with a positive result, early stopping ensures that a new product can be exploited sooner. If a negative result is indicated, early stopping avoids wasting resources and does not subject patients to inefficacious treatments. Sequential methods typically lead to savings in average sample size, time, and cost compared to a classical design with a fixed sample size. This is especially true if investigators are not certain of the effect size of the new treatment. If the true effect size is in fact larger, early stopping will be desirable; if the true effect size is smaller than expected, the trial may continue or even stop for early futility.

On the other hand, there are situations when a fixed sample design may be preferred to a group sequential design. In practice, if we have good knowledge regarding the effect size, a classical design with a fixed sample size may be more efficient. In this case, it does not require periodic monitoring and can lighten the administrative burden. Also, in some trials, if safety of the test treatment was not adequately assessed in previous trials, using the group sequential design to stop early for efficacy may lead to insufficient exploration of potential safety concerns. In this case, not only is the safety pool too small to answer safety questions, but if secondary outcomes are also important, early stopping for the primary efficacy outcome may not give adequate power for testing these secondary hypotheses. Also, the success of using group sequential methods necessitates that responses on the outcomes be readily measured for decision-making regarding trial termination before the recruitment of the next group of subjects, and so delayed responses may present a challenge to trial monitoring. Last, if an unbiased estimator of the effect size is one of the objectives, group sequential methods will be inadequate to achieve this goal by shifting sampling distribution across stages. Therefore, it is important to consider these factors carefully before deciding on adopting the sequential testing approach.

If the group sequential testing strategy is appropriate, it can be executed in two phases - the design phase and the interim monitoring phase. At the design stage, the user specifies the following:

- statistical process generating the data,
- the null and alternative hypotheses being tested,
- the desired type I error,
- the power of the sequential testing procedure,
- the shape parameters for the spending functions of stopping boundaries,
- the planned number of interim looks,
- and the timing of the interim looks.

To facilitate the design, the maximum number of interim looks at the data is pre-specified. At the interim monitoring phase, the interim looks or interim analyses can be conducted at precisely equally spaced intervals or when outcomes of equal number of subjects are ascertained. During the course of a trial, many factors may influence the frequency and timing of interim analyses. If the frequency or timing of the analyses is to be substantially altered from the planned schedule then the designs described above will become inappropriate. There are two distinct approaches to monitoring: continuous and discrete. As an abstract concept, continuous monitoring refers to the test statistic on a continuous scale as a function of the continuous information fraction. In the context of clinical trial, continuous monitoring means monitoring after observing the outcome of every single subject. In practice, this is seldom performed. Discrete monitoring, however, refers to monitoring after observing a group of subjects. This type of sequential monitoring is the focus of this chapter.

During the actual conduct of the group sequential trial, one may want to allow for flexibility in the analysis, while maintaining the integrity of the trial as bias may be introduced during interim examinations of accrued data. The (DSMB) data and safety monitoring board should not be understated. For instance, it may be necessary to set the dates of the interim monitoring looks so as to accommodate the schedule of the DSMB.
