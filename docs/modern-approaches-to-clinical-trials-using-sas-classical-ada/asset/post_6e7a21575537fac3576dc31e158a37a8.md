For example, Jenkins et al. [48] proposed an adaptive seamless phase II/III design with a subpopulation selection that can be more flexible. It allows the trial to select the appropriate subgroup at the interim analysis or continue in all patients with both the subgroup and the full population. With this method, a combination of test statistics for the final endpoint each stage is used for hypothesis testing. The decision to extend to the second stage is based on an intermediate or surrogate endpoint correlated to the final endpoint. This method is presented in the context of oncology with the progression free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) as interim and final endpoints, though it can be extended to other indications. In this design, having a co-primary endpoint recognizes the uncertainty at the interim look on the best possible endpoint and population. The additional flexibility is in the fact that the testing can be still done on the overall population, subgroup population, or the co-primary endpoint at the final analysis and also stopping the trial at the interim analysis for futility. This mitigates the risk of missing a promising subgroup early in the trial which could be detrimental both from an ethical and commercial perspective. The proposed design uses PFS as the intermediate endpoint and overall survival as has been accepted as a confirmatory endpoint in many cancer types. The design can be viewed as a special case or extension of the existing seamless design methods which combines the population and endpoints of both stages in support of the licensing claims. This is done by combining the test statistic testing clinically meaningful hypothesis and the two endpoints are combined without any additional bias. Suppose we have a randomized, two parallel arm trial. We partition trial into a learning stage and a confirmatory stage like any seamless phase II/III trial. After collecting data (~50%) on the interim outcome (say PFS) on the first stage subjects, an interim analysis is conducted where both the overall population (Op) and the pre-specified subgroup population (Sp) is examined for efficacy in the new treatment. The initial subgroup should be clearly defined and should be the only subgroup to be examined for efficacy. There is a null and alternative hypothesis associated with each of the populations (Op and Sp). The null hypotheses (H0Op and H0Sp) of no difference between the experimental treatment and the control are for the final endpoint (overall survival in this case), while the alternate hypotheses (H1Op and H1Sp) indicate that there is increased efficacy in the new treatment compared to the control. At the interim look, there can be five decisions for overwhelming efficacy that can be made:

- (1) stop the trial for overwhelming efficacy in the Op;
- (2) continue the trial in Op, if the efficacy is moderate in both Op and Sp;
- (3) continue the trial in Sp if the efficacy is slightly better in than Op with the co-primary subgroup hypothesis at the end of the trial;
- (4) enrich to include only Sp at the final analysis if the efficacy is much better than the Op;
- (5) stop the trial for utility if the treatment is not going any better than the control. Each of the possible presspecified decisions will differ in the sample size and in the follow-up post-interim analysis. The patients who start in the first stage will remain in the study and will be monitored for their long term (final) endpoint.

Wang et al. [45] proposed a similar design but only for a single normally distributed endpoint. We present the % subpower macro for design formulations.

The % subpower macro (Example Code 11.9 Subset Power Macro) calculates the subset power given:

- 1. f: the sample size ratio
- 2. alpha_a: the significance level for testing the overall population
- 3. beta_a: type II error of the overall trial
- 4. es_ratio: effect size ratio, es_ratio = delta_g +delta
- 5. alpha_g: the significance level for testing the subgroup population.

## Example Code 11.9 Subset Power Macro

```sas
%macro subpower(f,alpha_a, beta_a, es_ratio, alpha_g);
  data result;
    f=&f;
    alpha_a=\&alpha_a;
    beta_a=\&beta_a;
    es_ratio=&es_ratio;
    za=quantile('Normal', 1-alpha_a);
    zb=quantile('Normal', 1-beta_a);
    zag=quantile('Normal', 1-alpha_g);
    d=sqrt(&f)*es_ratio*(za+zb)-zag;
    sub_power=normCDF('Normal', d);
  label es_ratio="effect size ratio";
  label f="sample size ratio";
run;

proc noobs label;
  var f alpha_a beta_a es_sub_power;
  run;
%mend subpower;
%subpower(0.5, 0.04, 0.2, 2, 0.01)
```

For example, assuming 50% prevalence of a biomarker group, an alpha split of 0.04 versus 0.01 for the overall population and the subgroup, 80% power for the overall population, effect size ratio of 2, the power to detect treatment effect in the biomarker based population is 91%.

With multiple looks and subgroups there are several multiplicity methods [38, 42, 50] that can be employed to control the family-wise error rate at a nominal level, i.e., the probability of rejecting at least one true null hypothesis. A closed testing procedure is used to control multiplicity where all the possible intersection hypotheses ($H_0^{Cp}, H_0^{Sp}, H_0^{CpSp}$) is considered for rejecting any initial hypothesis ($H_0$). The stage-wise p-values for testing $H_0^{Cp}$ and $H_0^{Sp}$ are denoted by $Cp_p$ and $Sp_p$ for each stage $i\in\{1,2\}$, while $CpSp_{p_i}$ is a function of $Cp_{p_i}$ and $Sp_{p_i}$ for accounting multiplicity multiplicity can be calculated using the Hochberg correction [51] or the Dunnett methods [52]. The final analysis is done on the overall population ($C_P$) using the inverse normal p-value combination test [53-54]. For each stage weights $w_1$ and $w_2$, where $w_1^2+w_2^2=1$, are specified to combine the p-values. The null hypothesis at the final analysis is rejected if $C(p_1, p_2)=w_1^{-1}(1-p_1)+w_2^{-1}(1-p_2) \geq c$.

## 11.4.3 Bayesian Methods

### 11.4.3.1 Bayesian Adaptive Randomization (BAR) Enrichment Design

An important feature of an adaptive clinical trial is to continually update the design by using the accumulated information from interim data, which fits naturally into the Bayesian paradigm. The Bayesian approach was actually developed specifically to deal with new data as come in by updating prior beliefs to provide direct probability statements about the treatment effect. Moreover, in contrast to frequentist adaptive designs that require the type I error rate to be controlled, Bayesian inference is not affected by the number or timing of the interim analyses [55], allowing multiple analyses of the accumulating study data that update the probabilities under investigation. As a result, Bayesian designs are appealing to adaptive enrichment designs where adaptations in enrollment, randomization ratio, and subgroup selection are carried out based on emerging data, particularly in exploratory studies to screen biomarkers.

In contrast to the equal (or fixed ratio) randomization approach in a frequentist design, this type of Bayesian design [56-57] implements an adaptive randomization method that allows the evaluation of treatments and biomarkers simultaneously, while providing more patients with potentially more effective treatments according to the patients’ marker profiles. The Bayesian design is usually based on a hierarchical model for the endpoint of interests. The model parameters are also random variables modeling the random effects regarding the association of treatments and biomarkers with the outcomes of interest.
