Also, if disease outcome is severe or life threatening, then even modest reduction in treatment failures in the trial is desirable and can be viewed as a significant contribution from an ethical standpoint. RAR is aligned with the principle of individual ethics, it can be an an attractive research design in trials for rare diseases and trials with grave outcomes.

The ethical objective is one reason for using RAR. Another important reason is the pursuit of statistical efficiency. By applying RAR in trials with heteroscedastic outcomes, one can target allocations that lead to more accurate estimates of the treatment effects or more powerful tests compared to equal randomization [17-19]. In the draft the FDA provides the following view on RAR: “...more recently the approach has been revised to suit the objective of dose-response evaluation” and it is “...particularly valuable for exploratory studies” [20]. Indeed, RAR can be used in phase II dose-ranging studies to increase allocation frequency at dose levels that carry the most information on the shape of a dose-response curve or at dose levels of special interest. One good example where these ideas were applied is the ASTIN trial, an adaptive dose-ranging study in acute ischemic stroke which evaluated 15 different dose levels and the placebo, with the goal to determine the dose producing 95% of the maximum treatment effect (ED95) [21]. The ASTIN study used Bayesian RAR design where the randomization probability to any dose level set set to posterior probability that the given dose is the ED95. The dose-response curve was found to be flat and the study was terminated for futility, achieving substantial savings in the cost and patient resources.
