Additionally, they have shown that differences in samples and one-sample and adjusted one-sample SSR procedures are minimal. This finding is likely to be attributed to treatment effect sizes they explored: in most clinical trials to ratio is fairly high, i.e. between-group variability is much smaller than within-group variability. This is particularly true for non-inferiority trials (i.e. trials where small treatment effect is expected and the success of the trial really depends on how well the variability can be nailed down). Such an assumption would also hold for superiority trials powered for a minimal significantly effect size. With the landscape of clinical trials constantly changing, this assumption has to be evaluated carefully on a case-by-case basis; as budgets tighter and competition increases, many sponsors are forced into powering their trials more aggressively for larger commercially viable effect sizes. In these situations, blinded SSR methods should be applied with caution, as the key assumptions driving their performance (i.e. low signal to noise ratio) may get violated and are likely to increase sample size unnecessarily or fail to increase sample size when needed.

Additional methods for blinded SSR with normal endpoints include the EM (expectation minimization) algorithm, the block-sum estimation of variance, and the unequal allocation within blocks method [7-9]. These methods are less commonly used due to various statistical issues. In general, we will not discuss these challenges here and refer the reader to published reviews. For example, several references have a back-and-forth debate over the EM method [10-12].

For trials with a binary endpoint, several methods are also available. Gould describes a procedure for updating a sample size using the observed overall rate at the interim analysis [13,14]. A similar strategy in the context of a non-inferiority trial with binary endpoint is described in [15]. Variance estimation using blocks of unequal treatment allocation for binary data is proposed in [16].
