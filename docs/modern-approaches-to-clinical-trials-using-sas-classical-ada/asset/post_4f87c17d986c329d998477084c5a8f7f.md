Other ways of subdividing are also possible. Instead of splitting the initial interval $[0, T/2]$ and $[T/2, T]$, we may use $1/p$ cut — $[0, T/p]$ and $(T/p, T]$ — where $p \ge 2$ and then repeat the procedure described above. The BDS design is nonparametric because it does not depend on any underlying models other than the two assumptions: knowledge of MTD and a non-decreasing dose-response relationship. It provides a way to identify doses that are below the MTD and a means to potentially estimate the MinED.

In addition to BDS, other algorithms could be useful, e.g., log-dose spacing, the Fibonacci series, and the modified Fibonacci series approach can also be considered $[17,18]$. However, it is important to note that most of these methods propose dose spacing from lower doses to higher doses. In practice, dose spacing should be considered starting with the MTD, and move down to lower doses. Hence, when applying any of these algorithms, the dose allocation would be reversed (from high to low, instead of low to high).

## 6.2.6 Number of Doses and Control Groups

As discussed in the previous sections, a wide dose range is critical in phase II dose ranging studies. Once the MTD is estimated from phase I and a wide dose range is considered, the natural questions to think about how many doses should be included in the study, whether a placebo control group is sufficient, or whether an active control group is also needed.

A single dose plus placebo arm design may be able to demonstrate the proof-of-concept, but cannot, by essence, characterize the dose-response relationship. Any attempt to interpolate a dose-response relationship between the placebo and the test dose would require very strong assumptions, which oftentimes are not realistic. Therefore, a dose-ranging study typically needs several test dose levels. One very commonly used design is a four-group study with a placebo, low dose, medium dose and high dose group of the candidate under development. Some authors suggest that more doses could help; in the first-dose-ranging study, adding more doses to the study design [19]. However, the number of doses to be used might be limited by practical and logistical considerations (e.g., available formulations of test doses, dosing frequencies, convenience for outpatients to use on their own or blinding complexity, etc.). Given many treatment groups already included in a study, it may not be practical to add many test doses into the same study.

In an early phase II dose-ranging trial design, typically the total sample size is limited depending on budget, availability of patients, and difficulty in recruitment. Under this circumstance, more treatment groups in a design provide a less precise estimate of treatment effect. Hence, blinding dose groups to a study design does not necessarily make the design more likely to deliver more informative results. A well-thought-out design strategy is needed before the dose groups can be determined.

In our opinion, for the first dose-ranging study design, it is more important to cover a wide dose range, rather than simply adding more doses to a narrow range of doses. This is both practical, and scientifically sound. In practice, a trial with 4 to 5 test doses, plus a placebo control will deliver a good understanding of where the test medication is most active, if the dose range is wide enough, and if the dose spacing is reasonable. Some simulation studies suggest that among the number of treatment arms (4, 5, 6, or 7, placebo arm included), three test doses (the 4-arm design) could be insufficient in some cases because it significantly limits the space of potentially relevant models to fit to the data with clear potential impact on the ability to identify the optimal dose [16]. The performance increases when more than three doses are studied. On the other hand, six test doses (the 7-arm design) may not necessarily deliver better results than the four or five test doses (the 5-arm or 6-arm design) comparisons. When the total sample size is fixed, the 7-arm design offers a smaller sample size per group when an equal total sample size is used, and the precision would be sacrificed. It may also be a good idea to consider an unbalanced sample-size allocation, e.g., allocate more subjects to the extreme doses (i.e., higher dose(s) and a placebo arm).

Consequently, a dose ranging design including four to five test doses (in addition to a placebo) and covering a wide dose range provides a very useful and pragmatic basis in designing the dose ranging clinical trial [20].

## 6.2.7 Sample Size Considerations

### 6.2.7.1 Statistical Hypothesis for Determining PoC

Whenever sample size calculation is needed for a given study, the first point is to understand the hypothesis to be tested. In the process of designing a phase II dose ranging program, the initial statistical hypothesis is typically a proof of concept hypothesis. In the case when the study is designed with only two treatment groups – a placebo group is compared to the MTD of study product - the hypothesis can be expressed in a straight-forward fashion by comparing the response from the test treatment against the response from the placebo. If the comparison is based on means, then the hypothesis can be written as:

$H_0: \mu_T = \mu_P$ VS. $H_1: \mu_T > \mu_P$

where $\mu_T$ is the test treatment and $\mu_P$ is the placebo. However, in a dose-ranging trial including more than two groups, the PoC hypothesis is not straightforward. This is because it might not be the most efficient to just test the highest dose, but rather use a contrast. Under this scenario, several possible options could be considered. One option is to use a contrast comparing two doses, as- expressed above. Another option is to use a contrast that includes more than two groups in order to perform the PoC test.

If the second option is selected, then there could be many varieties in writing such a contrast. For example, in a four-group design with a placebo, low dose, medium dose and high dose, the PoC can be based on the high dose (let $\mu_L$ be the mean response of low dose, $\mu_M$ be the mean response for medium dose, and $\mu_H$ be the mean response of high dose):

$H_0: \mu_H = \mu_P$ VS. $H_1: \mu_H > \mu_P$

Or, the contrast can be a trend test, which is based on all doses [12, 16], assuming a monotonic dose-response relationship and equally spaced doses:

$H_0: -3\mu_P - \mu_L + \mu_M + 3\mu_H = 0$ VS. $H_1: -3\mu_P - \mu_L + \mu_M + 3\mu_H > 0$.

Another possible PoC contrast may also include equal weight to each dose (i.e., testing the weighted sum of the three doses versus placebo):

$H_0: -3\mu_P + \mu_L + \mu_M + \mu_H = 0$ VS. $H_1: -3\mu_P + \mu_L + \mu_M + \mu_H > 0$.

Assuming only the high dose is effective (i.e., testing the highest dose versus the weighted sum of the other groups):

$H_0: -\mu_P - \mu_L - \mu_M + 3\mu_H = 0$ VS. $H_1: -\mu_P - \mu_L - \mu_M + 3\mu_H > 0$.

As can be found from the above examples, if a contrast (that includes more than two treatment groups) is used for the PoC hypothesis, then there could be many possible ways of writing such a contrast, but it is really important that the project team reflect on choosing the most meaningful one for the particular compound and therapeutic area.

In data analysis of dose-ranging studies, a modeling approach, or MCP-Mod method could also be used [13]. In these settings, there could be a parameter of interest – e.g., for a linear model, the parameter could be the slope, for an exponential model, the parameter could be . In these situations, sample sizes could be calculated using simulations, or using proc power.
