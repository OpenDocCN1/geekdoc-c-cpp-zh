
## 9.6 Summary

This chapter reviews various types of adaptive-randomization designs in clinical trials. When used appropriately, adaptive designs may improve the efficiency and reduce the cost of a trial. Also, the information from the recruited patients can be maximally utilized, and the risk to the patients can be substantially reduced.

However, in practice, randomized trials are still dominated by the conventional fixed-randomization scheme due to the following concerns:

- Patients may not be balanced on certain covariates, especially those with possible time trends.
- Hypothesis tests during interim monitoring and at the trial completion are unconventional and difficult.
- Delayed outcomes may affect the practical implementation of adaptive randomization designs.

To facilitate practical use of adaptive randomization, more automatic and frequent updating systems should be built. Necessary infrastructures and cooperation between statisticians and clinicians need to be established. Further investigation on the statistical properties of various types of randomized design is warranted [1].

## 10.1 Introduction

### 10.1.1 Randomized Clinical Trials

The most common goal of a randomized clinical trial (RCT) is to compare the effects of one or more experimental treatments versus control treatment (standard of care) in the target patient population. Randomization plays a fundamental role in the RCT design. By introducing a deliberate element of chance into the assignment of treatments, one increases the likelihood that important patient characteristics (known and unknown) that may affect the outcome of interest are similarly distributed across the treatment groups, and therefore, any observed treatment difference should be attributed to the treatment effects, not the effects of study patients [1]. Importantly, randomization by itself is not sufficient to achieve the goals of a RCT and it should be complemented by other techniques such as masking, allocation concealment, stratification or adjustment of randomization for covariates (when appropriate), covariate-adjusted analysis and the careful conduct of the trial [2].

When designing an RCT, it is essential to formulate the primary scientific question to be answered in the trial. This requires careful consideration of the disease, the target patient population, the choice of experimental and control treatments, and the primary outcome variable. This primary question is frequently questioned in the form of testing a hypothesis of the difference of treatment effects. The trial should have sufficient sample size to provide a reliable answer to the primary question [3]. One can also include additional research questions that are less important but still within the scope of the trial as secondary objectives.

Once the trial objectives are formalized, there are several important statistical considerations, including but not limited to: 1) the choice of the target allocation ratio; 2) the choice of the randomization procedure; 3) the choice of the study sample size; 4) the choice of statistical methodology for data analysis. Let us consider the first two items in more detail. Many RCTs employ equal allocation designs, such as 1:1 allocation for a two-arm study. Equal allocation is frequently recommended in practice because it often maximizes the power of statistical tests and it is also consistent with the view of equipoise at the beginning of the trial [3], pg. 98. A number of

randomization procedures for implementing equal allocation are available. These include complete (simple) randomization, permuted block randomization, the maximal procedure [4], and biased coin designs [5]. On the other hand, there are a number of situations when fixed unequal allocation may be preferred. For instance, the 2:1 allocation, where twice as many patients are assigned to the experimental treatment compared to the control, may have an ethical advantage over the 1:1 allocation if the experimental treatment turns out to be beneficial [6,7]. Unequal allocation may also be preferred for gaining more information on the safety profile of the experimental treatment, for economic efficiency, or for accounting for possible drop-outs of study patients [8]. Randomization procedures to implement fixed unequal allocation include, for example, permuted block designs and biased randomization [9].
