# ### 3.1.1 Motivation. Why Sample Size Re-estimation?

Sample size re-estimation (SSR) has received a lot of attention in recent years as a result of exploding interest in adaptive clinical trial designs in general. In February 2010, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a draft guidance on Adaptive Clinical Trials for Drugs and Biologics, which defined an adaptive trial design as “a study that includes a prospectively planned opportunity for modification of one or more specified aspects of the study design and hypotheses based on analysis of data (usually interim data) from subjects in the study. Analyses of the accumulating study data are performed at prospectively planned time points within the study, can be performed in a fully blinded manner or in an unblinded manner, and can occur with or without formal statistical hypothesis testing” [1]. By definition, adaptive design can have many different types of adaptations and rules [2]. SSR focusing on modifying a trial's sample size based on interim analysis results. It can be performed in either a blinded or unblinded manner. A blinded SSR does not require the knowledge of treatment assignment at the individual patient level, while an unblinded SSR procedure uses treatment assignment knowledge.

When sample size is planned for clinical trials, it depends heavily on assumptions used in sample size calculations, as the hypothesized treatment effect size and the values of nuisance parameters. There is usually a considerable degree of uncertainty either or both of these parameters at the trial planning stage. This translates into a wide range of possibilities for the sample size needed to achieve a desired power level (e.g. 80%). Often the best guesses for effect size and nuisance parameter are utilized in power calculations in order to narrow down the choices for sample size to a single number.
Unfortunately, when these assumptions are violated, the power of the study is negatively affected, sometimes quite dramatically. If the trial does not have an interim analysis (i.e. a fixed trial design), it is impossible to detect the gap between our assumptions and reality until the study ends and all available resources are used.
