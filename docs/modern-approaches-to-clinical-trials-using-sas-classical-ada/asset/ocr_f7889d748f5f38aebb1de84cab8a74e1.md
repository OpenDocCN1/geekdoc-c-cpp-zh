The new sample size $M$ per group is selected so that $CP_{\zeta}[Z_n, M-n]=1-eta$ A not too extreme sample size increase is quantified as (1) implementing a conservative cap on the total sample size and (2) increasing sample size only if $CP_{\min} \leq CP < 1-eta$, where $1-eta$ is the targeted study power and $\zeta$ is the minimally acceptable conditional power that would still assure type I error preservation using the conventional Wald statistic at the end of the trial. The precise value of $eta$ is determined based on the ratios $N_{\max}/N$ and $n/N$ on the targeted power $1-eta$. For example, most commonly used trials, will be in a range of 30-40%. For example, a two-fold maximum sample size increase, $N_{\max}/N=2$, and one interim look midway through the trial, would be 36% for 90% overall power [3].

One important (and controversial) point is which value of treatment effect to use in calculating the conditional power in equation (3.3). Should it be the treatment effect $\Delta_0$ used to determine the original sample size or its interim estimate $\hat{\Delta}$? There is no consensus or uniformly agreed upon answer. Mehta and Pocook recommend using the observed treatment difference, which may be justifiable under somewhat restrictive requirements under which the SSR is carried out [3]. Under their method, sample size adjustment will occur only if the observed treatment effect is in the ballpark of what was hypothesized, so it is unlikely to cause problems. Posh, Bauer, and Brannath give pros and cons of both approaches, pointing out that neither is perfect in practice [221]. Other authors advise against plugging-in an estimated treatment effect into the originally planned effect size, citing lack of stability of mid-term estimates and undesirable sample size properties of the former [4].

As a good rule of thumb, neither option should be ruled out a priori, and simulations should be conducted to evaluate operating characteristics of both options. The result may vary well depending on the meaningful range of possible treatment effects. If that range is too wide (i.e. extends beyond clinically meaningful differences and the range of therapeutic value) then it is probably impossible to settle on a SSR rule that would give a uniformly good performance.
