
## 10.1.2 Response-Adaptive Randomization

With response-adaptive randomization (RAR), treatment randomization probabilities are modified according to the history of treatment assignments and responses from patients in the trial to achieve some pre-determined experimental objective (e.g. to achieve an allocation ratio that is desirable from the standpoint of statistical efficiency and ethics), while maintaining validity and integrity of the trial results. For instance, an objective may be to reduce the number of treatment failures with no loss of statistical power [10].

RAR has a long history in the biostatistics literature [11,12]. However, its use in practice has been limited, mainly due to a negative experience with a poorly designed ECMO trial in the 1980s [13]. At the same time, in the past decades research on RAR has advanced substantially—many new methodologies have been developed and statistical properties of various RAR designs have been established. The monograph by Hu and Rosenberger provides a modern theory of RAR in clinical trials [14]. Recent statistical research has focused on the development of optimal RAR procedures that can simultaneously address the objectives related to statistical efficiency and ethics. Optimal RAR procedures should be contrasted with heuristic RAR procedures such as the randomized play-the-winner rule [12] or Bayesian adaptive randomization [15]. The former procedures target explicitly the objectives of statistical efficiency and ethics, whereas the latter are primarily concerned with assigning more patients to the empirically better treatment.

It is instructive to review clinical trial scenarios where RAR can be applied with the most nest benefit. Palmer and Rosenberger in [16] provide an excellent discussion of the delicate balance between individual ethics (doing what is best for every subject in the trial) and collective ethics (doing what is best for future patients who stand to benefit from the trial results). They consider two dimensions of disease characteristics—severity and prevalence—and suggest that individual ethics should definitely outweigh collective ethics when disease is severe or life threatening and when disease is rare or very rare. Indeed, disease is (very) rare, the majority of patients with the disease will be treated within trial and very few patients will be treated outside the trial. Also, if disease outcome is severe or life threatening, then even modest reduction in treatment failures in the trial is desirable and can be viewed as a significant contribution from an ethical standpoint. RAR is aligned with the principle of individual ethics, it can be an attractive research design in trials for rare diseases and trials with grave outcomes.

The ethical objective is one reason for using RAR. Another important reason is the pursuit of statistical efficiency. By applying RAR in trials with heteroscedastic outcomes, one can target allocations that lead to more accurate estimates of the treatment effects or more powerful tests compared to equal randomization [17-19]. In the draft the FDA provides the following view on RAR: “...more recently the approach has been revised to suit the objective of dose-response evaluation” and it is “...particularly valuable for exploratory studies” [20]. Indeed, RAR can be used in phase II dose-ranging studies to increase allocation frequency at dose levels that carry the most information on the shape of a dose-response curve or at dose levels of special interest. One good example where these ideas were applied is the ASTIN trial, an adaptive dose-ranging study in acute ischemic stroke which evaluated 15 different dose levels and the placebo, with the goal to determine the dose producing 95% of the maximum treatment effect (ED95) [21]. The ASTIN study used Bayesian RAR design where the randomization probability to any dose level set set to posterior probability that the given dose is the ED95. The dose-response curve was found to be flat and the study was terminated for futility, achieving substantial savings in the cost and patient resources.

By discussing various RAR designs, it is instructive to state key assumptions for RAR trials [2,22]:

- *Statement of the obvious*: Better treatment will actually perform better in the trial.
- *The treatment with better outcome does not cause serious.*
- *Study outcomes should be observable relatively quickly, and the patient inclusion rate should be relatively low to enable adaptations in the design.*
- Data must be of excellent quality to enable accurate estimation and adaptation throughout the trial.
- The disease is stable over time (there is no drift in patient characteristics).
- The trial is carefully planned with extensive simulations run under different experimental scenarios.

Clearly, RAR designs are more complex experiments and require more upfront planning than designs with fixed-schedule randomization [2].

## 10.1.3 A Classification of Response-Adaptive Randomization Designs

### 10.1.3.1 RAR Urn Models

The FDA draft guidance on adaptive designs classifies RAR methods as "adaptive study designs whose properties are less well understood" [20]. Indeed, there is a limited number of trials which successfully implemented RAR and, to our knowledge, none of the RAR trials have been run in a confirmatory (phase III) setting. However, as the knowledge on statistical properties, merits, and limitations of RAR is increasing, it is likely that more trials using RAR will be performed in the future [2,23]. In this subsection we briefly discuss three major types of RAR designs: 1) RAR urn models, 2) Bayesian adaptive randomization, and 3) optimal RAR procedures. In terms of maturity levels, the first two can be viewed as "established methods" with some examples of real-life applications, whereas the third type includes procedures whose potential is yet to be demonstrated in practice.

Urn models provide a useful probabilistic mechanism for implementing RAR in clinical trials [24]. The most famous RAR urn model is the randomized play-the-winner rule (RPWR), developed in 1978 by Wei and Durham [12]. The method assumes a two-arm comparative trial with binary outcomes, where a patient's treatment assignment is determined by randomly drawing a ball from the urn, and the urn composition is sequentially updated based on the outcomes from previous patients in the trial such that balls representing more successful treatment are chosen more frequently. The RPWR is a heuristic appeal of assigning fewer patients to the less successful therapy—the limiting allocation proportion of study patients assigned to a given treatment is inversely proportional to the failure rate of that treatment. However, such an allocation is not optimal in terms of any statistical criterion (in particular, statistical power with this approach may be lower than with 1:1 allocation). The RPWR was used to design a pediatric trial of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), which compared the ECMO therapy versus the conventional therapy [13]. At the trial outset there was a strong ethical rationale for implementing a skewed allocation in favor of ECMO arm. In the actual trial, the RPWR stopped after 12 infants were randomized (one infant was randomized to the conventional therapy and died, and 11 infants were randomized to ECMO treatment and all survived). As such, the study failed because did not provide conclusive results about the relative merits of the two therapies. According to the modern theory of RAR, the failure of the ECMO trial can be explained by the trial's sample size, the RPWR's high variability, and the dependence on the initial composition of the urn (initially the urn contained one ball of each, and after several assignments the urn composition became extremely skewed in favor of the ECMO arm, leaving no chance for the conventional therapy to be chosen). Despite this, the ECMO trial is a good setting where RAR designs with robust statistical properties could have been used. One method that could have been beneficial in this setting is the drop-the-loser, an urn design with the same limiting allocation as RPWR, but with much lower variability and higher speed of convergence [25]. Many other urn models with established statistical properties are available; refer to §10.5 of [22], Chapter 4 of [14], and [26] for further details.

### 10.1.3.2 Bayesian Adaptive Randomization

Bayesian adaptive randomization, originally proposed by Thompson in [27], is another heuristic approach to facilitate RAR. Let $p_E$ and $p_C$ denote the success probabilities for experimental (E) and control (C) treatments, respectively. Assume that $p_E$ and $p_C$ follow independent prior distributions, and the posteriors of $p_E$ and $p_C$ are updated sequentially by accumulating patient outcome data. The current eligible patient is randomized between treatments E and C with probabilities $\lambda_E = P(p_E > p_C|data)$ and $\lambda_C = 1 - \lambda_E$. In other words, the current patient is assigned with higher probability to the treatment that is viewed more successful given current data. Thall and Waten in [15] noted that such a randomization procedure, while holding ethical promise, can be highly variable due to the random nature of $\lambda_E, \lambda_C$.
