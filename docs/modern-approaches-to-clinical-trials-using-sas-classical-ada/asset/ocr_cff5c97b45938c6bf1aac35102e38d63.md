Many original manuscripts describing the unblinded SSR methodology focus solely on type I error control, because it is the most important topic to regulators (at least in a confirmatory trial setting). When considering SSR, one has to address not only type I error control but also the rules to select the new sample size and to estimate the treatment effect at the end of the trial. We did not get the opportunity to address these issues in detail within this chapter. Some general considerations are outlined below.

**Sample size.** With the exception of the promising zone design, a user is free to choose the method to re-estimate the sample size. It is commonly accepted to revise the sample size based on the desired conditional power (similar to how promising zone does). Even though in theory the sample size $M$ does not have to be restricted, in practice most trials with unblinded SSR employ a cap on the maximum allowable sample size increase. The competing forces in selecting a maximum allowable sample size (MASS) are obvious. To maximize the chance of success in a trial (purely in terms of statistical power) we would select a value for the MASS that is as large as possible. This could potentially lead to increased costs and duration and carry a regulatory risk of achieving statistical significance for a treatment difference that is not clinically relevant. On the other hand, selecting too small a value for MASS could make the SSR effort useless, because power would not be maintained in situations of early departures from the assumptions used in the original sample size planning. As statisticians, we need to ensure that the selected value of the MASS is large enough to ensure the usefulness of a proposed SSR plan, while keeping in mind the implications of a large sample size increase to trial operations and interpretation.

**Estimation.** In a two-stage design, naïve estimates from Stage 1 and Stage 2 are unbiased. But because the Stage 2 sample size is determined by using Stage 1 data, the naïve estimation approach from both stages yields a biased estimate [39]. Final inference following a trial with unblinded SSR remains an active area of research. For some methods analytical solutions exist and for others simulation remains the only way of quantifying the bias in estimation.
