## 9.5 Response-Adaptive Randomization

### 9.5.1 Play-the-Winner Rule

From an ethical standpoint, clinical trials should utilize the evidence accumulated from the data to assign more patients to the superior arm, so that more patients will benefit from the better treatment. Response or outcome-based adaptive randomization aims to achieve this goal by skewing the number of patients to a potentially better treatment group.

The play-the-winner rule attempts to allocate more patients to the potentially better treatment: staying with the winner until a failure outcome occurs and then switching to the alternative treatment [10]. This rule is often applied to the dichotomous cases, where the outcome is either a success or failure. In a study comparing two treatments A and B, Figure 9.2 Play-the-Winner Rule with Dichotomous Outcomes with Treatments A and B: the first patient is randomized to either treatment A or B with an equal probability. If the previous patient’s response is a success, the treatment for that patient retains for the next patient; while in the case of a failure, the next patient will receive the other treatment. Success is a solid circle and failure is an open circle.

Figure 9.2 Play-the-Winner Rule with Dichotomous Outcomes with Treatments A and B

![](img/f4ec2665c3484c528370923aeb5cb99f_448_0.png)

Such alternating process continues until the total sample size is exhausted. The ratio of the numbers of subjects assigned to treatments A and B tends to $(1-p_B) / (1-p_A)$ as the number of patients in the trial goes to infinity, where $p_A$ and $p_B$ denote the response rates of treatments A and B, respectively.

### 9.5.2 Randomized Play-the-Winner Rule

The original play-the-winner rule as discussed above lacks randomness because each treatment assignment is fully determined by the outcome of the previous patient, regardless of the outcomes of other patients in the trial. Such a deterministic rule would increase the potential for selection bias. As a remedy, Wei and Durham introduced the randomized version of the play-the-winner rule; that is, to switch to the other treatment with a higher probability in the case of observing a failure outcome, instead of a definitive switch [11]. As a well-known example, the Michigan extracorporeal membrane oxygenator (ECMO) trial utilized such a scheme to allocate infants to conventional therapy or the ECMO [12].

The randomized play-the-winner rule can be illustrated with an urn model (Figure 9.3 Urn Model Under Randomized Play-the-Winner Rule). Suppose that there are two types of balls, marked as A or B, in the urn. Starting with $m$ balls of each type, a ball is drawn randomly with replacement to determine the treatment for a new patient. If the ball of type A, the subject is assigned to arm A, and if the response is a success, additional $\alpha$ balls of type A and $\beta$ balls of type B are added to the urn, with $\alpha \ge \beta \ge 0$. Therefore, the next ball to be drawn would have a higher probability

$Pr(\text{Treatment A}) = \frac{\alpha+m}{\alpha+\beta+2m} \ge 0.5$,

to be a ball of type A, and the next patient would be more likely to be assigned to arm A. On the other hand, if the response of the subject that is assigned to arm A is a failure, additional $\beta$ balls of type A and $\alpha$ balls of type B are added to the urn, such that the next ball to be drawn would have a lower probability of

$Pr(\text{Treatment A}) = \frac{\beta+m}{\alpha+\beta+2m} \le 0.5$,

to be a ball of type A, and so is the probability of the next patient assigned to arm A.

### 9.5.3 Optimal Randomization

Adaptive randomization can be optimized by minimizing the variance of the treatment difference. From an ethical perspective, let us consider two-arm trials where $p_1$ and $p_2$ be the response rates of treatments 1 and 2, respectively. If a smaller response is preferred, we may minimize the expected number of patients assigned to arm 1. The play-the-winner rule results in an allocation ratio of $(1-p_2)/(1-p_1)$, which is not constant.
