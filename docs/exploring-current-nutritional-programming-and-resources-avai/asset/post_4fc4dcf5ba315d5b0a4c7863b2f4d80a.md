## ABSTRACT

**Objective:** The objective of this scoping review was to map the current literature and resources available on nutrition and food programming for people living with HIV in Canada. This review is phase 1 of a 4-phase project, called FoodNOW (Food to eNhance Our Wellness), a community-based nutritional needs assessment of people living with HIV in Nova Scotia, Canada.

**Introduction:** People living with HIV may experience nutritional challenges, including nutritional deficiencies associated with the virus, food insecurity, and nutrition-drug interactions. Nutritional programming is often required for optimal care for people living with HIV. The literature, however, has not been sufficiently mapped to create a comprehensive picture of available programming. This review has informed the development of subsequent study phases, and will contribute towards shaping and planning food programs, as well as evaluating the need for subsequent systematic reviews.

**Inclusion criteria:** This review considered literature focused on nutrition and food programming and resources in Canada for people living with HIV. People living with HIV of any age, sex, race, gender identity, or sexual orientation, as well as pregnant and lactating people, were included as the population of interest.

**Methods:** The databases searched were MEDLINE (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO), Academic Search Premier (EBSCO), Social Services Abstracts (ProQuest), and Scopus. Sources of gray literature searched included government and organization websites, and Google searches. The database search was conducted in July 2021, and the gray literature searches were conducted in August and October 2021. Searches were limited to evidence published or translated in English. Two independent reviewers conducted title and abstract screening, and potentially relevant results were retrieved in full. Full-text screening and data extraction was conducted by 2 independent reviewers using a data extraction tool designed specifically for the scoping review objectives and research inclusion criteria, and any conflicts were resolved through discussion. Results are presented in both tabular and diagrammatic formats, with a narrative summary.

**Results:** A total of 581 results were screened (published and gray literature). A total of 64 results were included in the review. The 6 reasons for exclusion at full-text review were:

- not nutrition and food programming (n = 83)
- not Canadian (n = 37)
- duplicates (n = 22)
- not focused on people living with HIV (n = 6)
- conference abstract (n = 1)
- not in English (n = 1)

A total of 76 resources were located, as some of the 64 included sources offered more than 1 resource. The 76 resources were organized into 6 categories:

- charitable food provision (n = 21; 27.6%)
- financial aid (n = 14; 18.4%)
- nutrition care (n = 12; 15.8%)
- providing access to secondary sources (n = 10; 13.2%)
- food and nutrition expertise (n = 10; 13.2%)
- population health promotion (n = 9; 11.8%)

Recommendations for future research and programming are discussed.

**Conclusions:** This scoping review demonstrates that current programming relies heavily on charitable food provision services for people living with HIV and that there is an unequal distribution of resources across Canada. Program expansion to target diverse populations with more equal distribution across Canada may improve overall health outcomes for people living with HIV. Future research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of available programming and the needs of end users (people living with HIV and their supports). FoodNOW will build on these findings to further explore and address the needs of people living with HIV.

## Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) attacks the body’s immune system, deteriorating and weakening it, and increasing the risk of a person acquiring life-threatening infections and diseases (eg, cancer). If left untreated, HIV can progress into acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) and result in death. There were an estimated 62,050 Canadians living with HIV by the end of 2018. In 2019, the number of positive HIV cases identified in Canada was slightly lower than 2018; however, this decrease was not as great as in other countries. For instance, positive HIV cases identified among gay, bisexual, and men who have sex with men (gbMSM) have declined by 47% in the United Kingdom since 2014, whereas the cases among the same population in Canada have decreased by 26% over the past 10 years, providing rationale for a Canadian-focused scoping review.

A cornerstone of HIV treatment is medical nutrition therapy (MNT), which has been highlighted by Dietitians of Canada and the American Dietetic Association (now named the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics) in their reports. Through nutrition assessment and co-developed support, the nutrition care process and MNT aim to prevent and manage the signs, symptoms, and progression of HIV. MNT assessment includes nutrition-related problems, causes, and significance; medical, physical, social, economic, and dietary history; pharmacotherapy; and recognition/integration of social determinants of health. While a referral to a registered dietitian is part of the treatment stage of the cascade of care for individuals upon HIV diagnosis, there is no guarantee they will have access to subsequent nutrition care in their own communities.

HIV has had “a significant impact on domestic and global health, social, political, and economic outcomes.” There were 2122 new cases of HIV reported in Canada in 2019. Additionally, Dietitians of Canada and the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics reported that virus cases are highest among those who have and continue to experience barriers in accessing medical care: 30% (n = 626) were immigrants, and 56% (n = 1193) were gbMSM, while approximately 25% (n = 531) of new cases were among Indigenous populations. Important to note, Indigenous peoples made up 5% (n = 1,807,250) of the total population (approximately 37 million) in Canada in 2021.

A widely accepted definition of *food insecurity*, from Anderson is “limited or uncertain availability of nutritionally adequate and safe foods or limited or uncertain ability to acquire acceptable foods in socially acceptable ways.” In high-income, high-resource settings such as Canada and the United States, 49% to 71% (30,404 to 44,055 of 62,050) of people living with HIV (PLWH) experience food insecurity, largely due to socioeconomic status. Moreover, PLWH may experience a range of nutrient deficiencies, including anemias, antioxidant depletion, or protein-energy malnutrition, due to their pathology alone, directly impacting nutritional status and adequacy and, thus, their food security.
