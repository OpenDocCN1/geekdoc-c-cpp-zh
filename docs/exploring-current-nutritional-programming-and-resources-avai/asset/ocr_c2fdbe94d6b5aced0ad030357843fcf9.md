For example, 3% of resources identified in this review were targeted towards Indigenous people, despite this population accounting for almost 25% of new HIV cases in 2019.³ Black CAP in Toronto is an example of a targeted resource. Their programming is focused on providing basic food items that are culturally specific for low-income Black, African, and Caribbean households in Toronto.⁴⁶ Also found in Toronto, the Ontario Aboriginal HIV/AIDS Strategy (OAHAS) provides assistance in accessing food and shelter for urban-based First Nations, Inuit, Metis, and non-status community groups/organizations/agencies, schools, and individuals in Ontario.⁴⁷ OAHAS performs outreach in streets, neighborhoods, events, powwows, agencies, in-reach in correctional facilities, hospitals, medical centers, and schools. This also demonstrates that most of the targeted programming is offered in large cities.

Second, large cities, such as Vancouver (n = 11/18; 61% of the results from British Columbia) and Toronto (n = 7/14; 50% of the results from Ontario), were identified as home to most of their provinces’ resources (see Figures 2 and 3), leading to speculation that there are not as many resources offered in rural areas. Collectively, the programming identified in these regions covered a wide range of needs for PLWH, offering different resources for targeted population groups. The resources found were mostly provided by nonprofit organizations, private (for-profit) clinics, and provincial governments. For example, in British Columbia, we identified 18 results that include programming from all 6 categories, whereas in Manitoba we identified 2 results that covered 3 of the 6 categories, which were charitable food provision, nutrition care, and financial aid. The Manitoba HIV Program, operated by Nine Circles Community Health Centre (a nonprofit organization), 7th Street Access Centre (a provincially operated health clinic), and the Winnipeg Health Sciences Centre (a provincial hospital), offers both charitable food provision and nutrition care.⁴⁸ While there were not many resources identified in Manitoba, organizations such as Nine Circles offer multiple types of programming (eg, emergency food supply, nutrition education with a dietitian) that may be able to target PLWH with different needs.⁴⁸ There do remain, however, many regions that do not have any services available to them. Having fewer resources to reach large, highly impacted populations

can leave programming stretched and potentially unable to meet demand.

Third, the most common type of nutrition and food programming identified was charitable food provision services. This included supports such as food banks, grocery vouchers, and soup kitchens, which accounted for 27.6% (21/76) of all nutrition and food services identified. These supports do not appear to be different for PLWH than for the general population; however, certain food provision services are reserved for PLWH. Many of the organizations identified offer charitable food provision as their only nutrition-related resource available for PLWH. For example, ACT (formerly the AIDS Committee of Toronto) offers hot lunches to PLWH twice per week⁴⁹ and the HIV/AIDS Resources and Community Health offers monthly $10 grocery store gift cards to PLWH.⁵⁰ For many PLWH, charitable food provision services may be the only nutritional support they are receiving to manage their symptoms and medication side effects.²¹ Future research would benefit from exploring long-term solutions; however, charitable food services are needed in the interim, not as a solution, but to meet immediate needs. The second-most common type of support identified was financial aid (14/76; 18.4%), as each province and territory has its own form of governmental financial aid for PLWH under employment support and income assistance if the individual qualifies (ie, referral from a dietitian). The amount provided to each individual can consist of allowances for basic food, diet supplements, and caloric intake/special diet. The third-most common type of support identified was nutrition care (12/76; 15.8%). Nutrition care included resources such as access to dietitian services for diet supplement referrals and MNT process, which has proven beneficial for PLWH.¹ Secondary sources (ie, organization websites that do not offer nutrition and food resources but provide a list/website links of others that do) were tied for fourth-most common support identified (10/76; 13.2%). Secondary sources, however, may be less helpful to those in search of nutrition and food support. As a significant number of resources found were from service-provider websites offering links to secondary sources, this indicates a lack of available support, as some of the secondary sources provide further links to tertiary sources, making it challenging to find a tangible resource that provides nutrition or foods programming.
