An individual’s overall health and well-being are impacted by economic and social intersections, otherwise known as social determinants of health.⁹ PLWH may experience multiple prejudices simultaneously, such as race, gender, sexual orientation, and socioeconomic status, which create complex systems of oppression and privilege. Using an intersectional¹⁰ lens when conducting HIV research has proved beneficial in understanding PLWH and their challenges in accessing care.¹¹,¹² As such, an intersectional approach to HIV prevention and management would greatly benefit food programming research and planning towards inclusivity while considering the multiple social identities to which PLWH are often connected.

In attempts to meet nutritional needs of PLWH in Canada, there are different types of nutrition and food programming. For example, food banks and meal programs provide emergency food supply for those in need,⁹,¹³–¹⁷ named in this review as *charitable food provision*. MNT and other dietitian services, such as community-based cooking classes, aim to treat nutrient deficiencies, maintain lean body mass, improve quality and length of life, manage symptoms, facilitate medication therapy and avoid food–drug interactions, and address complications.¹,⁴ Community-based cooking classes have also been shown to improve health and life skills and increase the likelihood of participation in nutrition and food programming.⁹,¹³

Charitable food provision services were originally created to serve as an emergency food service for those in immediate need.¹⁸,¹⁹ Despite this, food banks and soup kitchens are commonly used as a longstanding response to address food insecurity, despite low intervention efficacy.¹⁹ Much research has been conducted to highlight why providing food in this way does not address the root cause of food insecurity.¹³,¹⁸–²² For example, although there are more than 50 free and low-cost food centers operating in the Downtown Eastside of Vancouver, British Columbia, there continue to be high rates of food insecurity reported among residents in this area.¹⁹ Research has shown that only 20% to 30% of people experiencing food insecurity utilize charitable food provisions, such as food banks and meal programs, due to their stigmatizing nature, inadequacy to meet people’s real needs, and many other limitations and negative consequences.²² Further, Miewald *et al.* and others highlight the importance of considering the intersection(s) of HIV, poverty, and gender, and how this may affect an individual’s experience in gaining proper food and nutrition by creating more challenges to navigate.¹³,¹⁸,¹⁹

The federal government in Canada distributes health care funding to provinces and territories with the aim of meeting the specific needs and priorities of each region. This model of federal funding and provincial or territorial service provision makes up the Canadian publicly funded health care system.²³ For Indigenous populations, the Non-Insured Health Benefits Program offers coverage for medically necessary goods and services.²⁴ As a result of each province having individualized funding and programming, Canada does not have a national plan for MNT or community-based nutrition for PLWH,²⁵,²⁶ despite there being evidence-based agreement on MNT for patients with chronic disease, including HIV.²⁶,²⁷ A coordinated and comprehensive approach would be beneficial towards ensuring efficient and effective sharing of knowledge and resources.²⁶,²⁷ To optimize results, organizations that work with targeted populations, including women and children, aging populations, and gbMSM, may attempt to offer specialized programming that best suits their needs. Resources often differ from region to region and, thus, tailored responses that correspond with intersecting local geographical, social, and political contexts are common, and frequently necessary, to address local needs.²⁸

In addition to the programming itself, health care workers and service providers meant to deliver programming for PLWH require specific supports to be able to provide the best care possible. Consequently, the Global Health Workforce Alliance (2010) conducted a review on the state of health care services for PLWH in which they offered recommendations, including improved support for health care workers and service workers in primary care.²⁹ The supports identified included resources, flexibility, interprofessional education opportunities, and quality communication. Several peer-reviewed original works, within and beyond Canada, have identified similar findings.

## Data analysis and presentation

The results of this review are presented in tables and figures, and accompanied by a narrative summary that describes how the results relate to the review objective and question. The focus of this review was the broad concept of nutrition and food programming available to PLWH, and as such, we reported a broad range of programming types.

## Results

### Study inclusion

A search of the peer-reviewed literature conducted on July 14, 2021, yielded 696 results from 5 databases. Gray literature searches conducted on August 24, October 5, and October 15, 2021 (see Appendix I), yielded another 143 results (see Appendix IV). A total of 839 results were recorded. For purposes of this study, identified articles (peer-reviewed) and evidence (gray literature) are referred to as results, and individual programming and resources available to PLWH or AIDS are referred to as programming and resources.

After duplicates were removed from the published literature, 439 results remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 366 results did not match the research inclusion criteria, and 2 results were unable to be retrieved, leaving 213 results for full-text-review (71 published and 143 gray literature results). After full-text screening, 64 results were included in the review (17 published and 47 gray literature results). See Figure 1 for the complete search results and study selection and inclusion process. The 6 reasons for exclusion after full-text screening were:

- i) not nutrition and food programming (n = 83),
- ii) not Canadian context (n = 37),
- iii) duplicates (n = 22),
- iv) not focused on PLWH (n = 6),
- v) conference abstract (n = 1),
- vi) not in English (n = 1).

See Appendix II for the list of excluded results. Duplicates identified among the published literature during the full-text review were due to multiple records published on the same resource/service organization. As the objective of the review was to identify the number of resources available in Canada, only 1 record per resource/service organization was eligible to be included in the scoping review.

Details of the 64 results included in the scoping review are provided in Appendix IV. Study characteristics were recorded as they relate to the research objectives and include full citations. The included results were published between 1993 and 2021. Four results had no date available.
