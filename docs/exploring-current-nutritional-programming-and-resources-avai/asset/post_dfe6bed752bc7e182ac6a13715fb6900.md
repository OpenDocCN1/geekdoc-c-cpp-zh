## Discussion

For this scoping review, we mapped the current literature and resources available on nutrition and food programming for PLWH in Canada. To this end, we recorded what types of nutrition and food resources were offered in locations across Canada. We then mapped these resources according to the 7 Partnership for Dietetic Education and Practice (PDEP) competencies that dietitians must demonstrate before becoming a registered dietitian and entering the practice.⁵¹ The 7 competencies are i) food and nutrition expertise, ii) professionalism and ethics, iii) communication and collaboration, iv) management and leadership, v) nutrition care, vi) population health promotion, and vii) food provision. We found that 3 of the 6 types of resources identified in this review reflected the competencies. These were i) food and nutrition expertise (eg, nutrient and diet guidelines from professionals; education through books, PDFs, and workshops offered by organizations), ii) nutrition care (eg, dietitian services including the nutrition care process and MNT), and iii) population health promotion (eg, cooking classes and general healthy eating tips focused on community inclusion and sociability). The 3 types of resources that did not fit within the PDEP competencies were i) charitable food provision (eg, food banks, soup kitchens, grocery vouchers), ii) financial aid (ie, provincial government subsidies), and iii) providing access to secondary sources (ie, websites that do not offer nutrition and food programming but offer links to different websites that do). As some resources fit within the PDEP competencies and some did not, this helps us to understand what falls beyond the scope of dietitians and highlights the need to train those delivering these services to PLWH.

Most resources identified in this review were charitable food provision services; therefore, it appears that long-term approaches/solutions to ensuring PLWH in Canada obtain adequate nutrition are needed. While we have noted that charitable food provision is a necessary service, it is not sustainable long-term. It may be challenging to maintain adequate nutrition for individuals who access charitable food provision services as their only source of nutrition to manage their HIV conditions. Food banks and meal programs are shown to support daily needs as emergency food supply for those in need.⁴,⁹,¹³–¹⁶ They are not shown, however, to provide adequate long-term nutrition and food security support for individuals and families, especially for PLWH with complex nutrition needs.¹³,¹⁹ Tarasuk and Eakin²⁰ report that charitable food provision services may operate with a dissociation between food assistance and client need, resulting in food assistance becoming a symbolic gesture rather than solely providing food aid. Additionally, charitable food provision may be damaging and counterproductive for those in need, as it can decrease income supports for individuals, leading them further into poverty.²⁰ More recent work by Tarasuk et al.²¹ echoes the same sentiment, stating that charitable food provision services are not able to address or assist the root causes of food insecurity, such as income disparity and homelessness.

This scoping review identified a need in available programming in that the majority of food programming offered (charitable food provision) is not meeting the complex nutritional needs of PLWH in Canada.⁴,¹³,¹⁹ Many charitable food provision programs do not have adequate supply to address these specific needs, such as fresh fruits and vegetables,¹⁷,¹⁹ or to address the food insecurity that 49% to 71% of PLWH face.⁸,⁹ Miewald et al., among others, have reported that for PLWH, “food insecurity has been linked to lower CD4 counts, reduced drug adherence (i.e., ART [antiretroviral therapy]) and effectiveness, poor viral suppression (viral suppression being the primary goal of HIV treatment), and increased risk of mortality.”³¹⁹(p.511–2)

Additionally, it is evident from the review that distribution of the resources across Canada appears to be unequal in both quantity and type of resource offered, as most of the results identified were services offered in provinces with high populations or larger cities (see Figures 2 and 3). This could indicate that provinces and territories with smaller and more remote populations may be left with little to no resources. Further, PLWH who also belong to populations that have been marginalized, (eg, Black, Indigenous, or person of color; low-income; sexual minorities such as transgender, non-binary, or gender-diverse people) may have more difficulty finding programming that is specific to their needs and cultural healing practices, especially if they reside in less-populated areas of Canada (see Figure 3). For instance, Nova Scotia has the highest population of gender-diverse people (of those aged 15 to 34 years) among the Canadian provinces,⁵² but as outlined in Figure 2, we did not identify current nutrition and food programming in Nova Scotia that is specifically targeted for this population. PLWH may also have additional chronic conditions or disability considerations, especially as they get older.

In their study on food access for women living with HIV in Vancouver, Sernick et al.¹⁷ highlighted the barriers that some populations face in finding appropriate nutrition services including immigrant, Indigenous, women, and transgender PLWH. Despite the majority of identified resources targeting the general PLWH population, there were some programs identified targeted towards a more specific population (eg, women and children, transgender, non-binary, gender-diverse people), which are better tailored to their needs. Black CAP⁴⁶ and OAHAS⁴⁷ mentioned previously are examples that offer programming for specific populations. These organizations demonstrate that specialized care for different population groups is warranted.

More specialized resources that offer an intersectional approach to nutrition and food programming could be beneficial for these population groups in providing adequate nutritional support and safe spaces.¹²,¹⁷ For example, Sernick et al.¹⁷ found that peoples’ safety based on gender (ie, gender identity safety) was a main concern for those accessing food banks at the cis and trans women-focused centers, which was a main source of food for their study population. Sernick et al.¹⁷ demonstrated why it is important for individual experience and accounts from PLWH to be a part of the dialogue in research towards nutrition and food programming in Canada. Without consulting end users, research on which programming should be developed may miss key learnings and opportunities for improving programming.

We found that the number of results identified increased throughout the years, with 40% of results published between 2010 and 2019, and 45% of results between 2020 and 2021 alone. As many of our results came from service provider websites, this large percentage of results from 2020–2021 could be explained by the number of service organization websites identified in the review that update their website information each year, resulting in a large number of sources identified for the year 2021. With 85% of review results published between 2010 and 2021, this signifies that the results reported are relatively up-to-date and, thus, an accurate depiction of the currently available programming.

## Strengths of the review

There are several strengths to be noted in the creation of this scoping review. First, we have a dynamic research team with backgrounds including, but not limited to, dietetics, applied human nutrition, nursing, anthropology, 2SLGBTQIA+ nutrition and health, and Black, Indigenous, or person of color studies. This multitude of perspectives provided the review with a degree of depth that may not have been accessible had the research team been limited to 1 field of study. Community consultation through the DJM technique also provided this review with an additional layer of feedback that came from community members and service providers who work with PLWH, allowing the review to report more thoroughly on available food and nutrition resources for PLWH in Canada.
