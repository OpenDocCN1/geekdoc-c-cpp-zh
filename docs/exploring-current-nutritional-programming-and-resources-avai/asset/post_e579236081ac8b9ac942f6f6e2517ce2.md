## *Review findings*

### Population

Most results for available nutrition and food programming targeted the general population of PLWH in Canada. Of the 64 results included in the review, some offered specific programming for more than 1 population, for a total of 69 targeted programming resources. Of these 69 results, 44 (63.8%) were broadly focused on PLWH, rather than breaking programming down to target specific diverse populations. Some programming identified in the review did target specific populations that would have distinct needs compared to the general population of PLWH (see Figure 2 for resources offering targeted programming per Canadian city). The programming and resources identified from these results were focused on women and/or children (n = 6; 8.7%); PLWH who use drugs (n = 3; 4.3%); transgender, non-binary, and gender-diverse PLWH (n = 3; 4.3%); PLWH on antiretroviral therapy (n = 3; 4.3%); African, Caribbean, and Black PLWH (n = 2; 2.9%); Indigenous PLWH (n = 2; 2.9%); gbMSM (n = 2; 2.9%); low-income PLWH (n = 1; 1.4%), French-speaking PLWH (n = 1; 1.4%); sex workers (n = 1; 1.4%); and PLWH who have been marginalized (n = 1; 1.4%).

### Context

All results included in the review were based in Canada, per the research inclusion criteria. Of the 64 results identified, 18 (28.1%) were published in British Columbia; 14 (21.9%) in Ontario; 10 (15.6%) were Canada-wide non-provincial/territory specific; 7 (10.9%) in Nova Scotia; 3 (4.7%) in Alberta; 3 (4.7%) in Saskatchewan; 2 (3.1%) in Manitoba; and 1 (1.6%) each in Quebec, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador, Yukon, Northwest Territories, and Nunavut. For context, the 2019 provincial and territorial diagnosis rates of HIV per 100,000 people were as follows: Saskatchewan, n = 16.9; Manitoba, n = 8.8; Quebec, n = 7.4; Alberta, n = 5.8; Ontario, n = 4.7; British Columbia, n = 3.5; the Atlantic provinces, n = 3.0; and the Northwest Territories, n = 1.7.²

### Concept

A total of 76 resources were located, as some of the 64 results included in the review offered more than 1 resource (see Figure 3 for distribution of resources per province and territory). We have classified these 76 resources as charitable food provision (e.g., food banks, soup kitchens, grocery vouchers; n = 21; 27.6%); financial aid (i.e., provincial government subsidies; n = 14; 18.4%); nutrition care (e.g., dietitian services including the nutrition care process and MNT; n = 12; 15.8%); providing access to secondary sources (i.e., websites that do not offer nutrition and food programming but offer links to different websites that do; n = 10; 13.2%); food and nutrition expertise (e.g., nutrient and diet guidelines from professionals, books, PDFs, and workshops offered by organizations; n = 10; 13.2%); and population health promotion (e.g., cooking classes and general healthy eating tips focused on community inclusion and sociability; n = 9; 11.8%). Through searching the website links provided by these resources, we discovered an additional 69 secondary sources of food and nutrition programming, 10 of which had already been captured through initial searches (primary results). Thus, a total of 135 resources for nutrition and food programming for PLWH were located across Canada.

## Gaps in known services and areas for future research

The results show a lack of peer-reviewed literature on the topic of available nutrition and food programming for PLWH in Canada, as most results were from gray literature (n = 47/64; 73.4%). This highlights the importance of engaging with scoping reviews, because systematic scoping reviews are considered by many policymakers to offer the strongest evidence.⁴⁵ Moreover, policymakers, such as public health departments and federal governments, most often look to peer-reviewed literature and original research when gathering policy-relevant evidence.⁴⁵ For any interventions to be made at a policy level, more peer-reviewed research on this topic is needed. The results from the gray literature indicate that while there is no standard approach to providing nutrition and food programming to PLWH by programs offered across Canada by an array of organizations, there are similarities among the provincial approaches and supports offered. Four areas were identified as areas for future research on the topic of known services: i) limited targeted programming, ii) geographical distribution of resources, iii) type of resources offered, and iv) resource/program effectiveness as it relates to individual experience.

First, a limited amount of identified programming was designed for diverse subpopulations. For example, many women and children; Indigenous people; transgender and gender-diverse people; and Black, African, and Caribbean people have different needs to be addressed. Cisgender and transgender women living with HIV, for instance, need trauma-informed and gender-safe spaces to access food services.¹⁷ Black, African, and Caribbean people, such as the clients of the Black Coalition for AIDS Prevention (Black CAP), require programming that considers social barriers such as racism, homophobia, and poverty.⁴⁶ Despite this difference in needs, only 36% of results offered targeted programming. Their programming is focused on providing basic food items that are culturally specific for low-income Black, African, and Caribbean households in Toronto.⁴⁶ Also found in Toronto, the Ontario Aboriginal HIV/AIDS Strategy (OAHAS) provides assistance in accessing food and shelter for urban-based First Nations, Inuit, Metis, and non-status community groups/organizations/agencies, schools, and individuals in Ontario.⁴⁷ OAHAS performs outreach in streets, neighborhoods, events, powwows, agencies, in-reach in correctional facilities, hospitals, medical centers, and schools. This also demonstrates that most of the targeted programming is offered in large cities.

Second, large cities, such as Vancouver (n = 11/18; 61% of the results from British Columbia) and Toronto (n = 7/14; 50% of the results from Ontario), were identified as home to most of their provinces’ resources (see Figures 2 and 3), leading to speculation that there are not as many resources offered in rural areas. Collectively, the programming identified in these regions covered a wide range of needs for PLWH, offering different resources for targeted population groups. The resources found were mostly provided by nonprofit organizations, private (for-profit) clinics, and provincial governments. For example, in British Columbia, we identified 18 results that include programming from all 6 categories, whereas in Manitoba we identified 2 results that covered 3 of the 6 categories, which were charitable food provision, nutrition care, and financial aid. The Manitoba HIV Program, operated by Nine Circles Community Health Centre (a nonprofit organization), 7th Street Access Centre (a provincially operated health clinic), and the Winnipeg Health Sciences Centre (a provincial hospital), offers both charitable food provision and nutrition care.⁴⁸ While there were not many resources identified in Manitoba, organizations such as Nine Circles offer multiple types of programming (e.g., emergency food supply, nutrition education with a dietitian) that may be able to target PLWH with different needs.⁴⁸ There do remain, however, many regions that do not have any services available to them. Having fewer resources to reach large, highly impacted populations can leave programming stretched and potentially unable to meet demand.

Third, the most common type of nutrition and food programming identified was charitable food provision services. This included supports such as food banks, grocery vouchers, and soup kitchens, which accounted for 27.6% (21/76) of all nutrition and food services identified. These supports do not appear to be different for PLWH than for the general population; however, certain food provision services are reserved for PLWH. Many of the organizations identified offer charitable food provision as their only nutrition-related resource available for PLWH. For example, ACT (formerly the AIDS Committee of Toronto) offers hot lunches to PLWH twice per week⁴⁹ and the HIV/AIDS Resources and Community Health offers monthly $10 grocery store gift cards to PLWH.⁵⁰ For many PLWH, charitable food provision services may be the only nutritional support they are receiving to manage their symptoms and medication side effects.²¹ Future research would benefit from exploring long-term solutions; however, charitable food services are needed in the interim, not as a solution, but to meet immediate needs. The second-most common type of support identified was financial aid (14/76; 18.4%), as each province and territory has its own form of governmental financial aid for PLWH under employment support and income assistance if the individual qualifies (i.e., referral from a dietitian). The amount provided to each individual can consist of allowances for basic food, diet supplements, and caloric intake/special diet. The third-most common type of support identified was nutrition care (12/76; 15.8%). Nutrition care included resources such as access to dietitian services for diet supplement referrals and MNT process, which has proven beneficial for PLWH.¹ Secondary sources (i.e., organization websites that do not offer nutrition and food resources but provide a list/website links of others that do) were tied for fourth-most common support identified (10/76; 13.2%). Secondary sources, however, may be less helpful to those in search of nutrition and food support. As a significant number of resources found were from service-provider websites offering links to secondary sources, this indicates a lack of available support, as some of the secondary sources provide further links to tertiary sources, making it challenging to find a tangible resource that provides nutrition or foods programming.
