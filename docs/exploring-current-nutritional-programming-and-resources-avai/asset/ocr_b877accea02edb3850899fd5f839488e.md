# Gaps in known services and areas for future research

The results show a lack of peer-reviewed literature on the topic of available nutrition and food programming for PLWH in Canada, as most results were from gray literature (n = 47/64; 73.4%). This highlights the importance of engaging with scoping reviews, because systematic scoping reviews are considered by many policymakers to offer the strongest evidence.⁴⁵ Moreover, policymakers, such as public health departments and federal governments, most often look to peer-reviewed literature and original research when gathering policy-relevant evidence.⁴⁵ For any interventions to be made at a policy level, more peer-reviewed research on this topic is needed. The results from the gray literature indicate that while there is no standard approach to providing nutrition and food programming to PLWH by programs offered across Canada by an array of organizations, there are similarities among the provincial approaches and supports offered. Four areas were identified as areas for future research on the topic of known services: i) limited targeted programming, ii) geographical distribution of resources, iii) type of resources offered, and iv) resource/program effectiveness as it relates to individual experience.

First, a limited amount of identified programming was designed for diverse subpopulations. For example, many women and children; Indigenous people; transgender and gender-diverse people; and Black, African, and Caribbean people have different needs to be addressed. Cisgender and transgender women living with HIV, for instance, need trauma-informed and gender-safe spaces to access food services.¹⁷ Black, African, and Caribbean people, such as the clients of the Black Coalition for AIDS Prevention (Black CAP), require programming that considers social barriers such as racism, homophobia, and poverty.⁴⁶ Despite this difference in needs, only 36% of results offered targeted programming.
