
Existing works have contributed much to PHM. However, there is a common deficiency for most of them as they are primarily driven by the equipment in its physical space, with little connection to its virtual model, which affects the efficiency and reliability of PHM. Currently, with the development of cyber–physical system (CPS), attaching importance to the virtual space and implementing the seamless cyber–physical fusion to improve PHM are inevitable trends. Therefore, some researchers have tried to introduce digital mirrors of the equipment from the virtual space to work with the real entities to better support PHM. For example, Lee et al. proposed to build a digital twin model that managed hundreds of similar physical equipment with integrated data from multiple sources, to assess the equipment condition and identify the failure mode through simulation [22,23]. Liu and Xu investigated the concept of a cyber–physical machine tool (CPMT), which integrates the machine tool with its cyber twin that has the ability to transform the data into valuable information for PHM [24]. Zinnikus et al. presented a framework for the fault analysis of a cyber–physical production system, which compares the observations of the physical equipment with the behaviors of the models for anomaly detection and fault diagnosis [25]. Penna et al. proposed a visualization tool for a cyber–physical maintenance environment, which relates the physical elements with virtual ones and makes remote access possible via a web browser [26].

Although these works have attempted to introduce the digital models of complex equipment into PHM, to implement PHM driven by both physical and virtual spaces, some common problems still exist. They include (1) how to build the models to describe the complex equipment thoroughly and accurately, (2) how to establish the interaction mechanism between the equipment and its digital mirror model to make them evolve jointly and support PHM together, and (3) how to fuse the data from both physical and virtual spaces and generate valuable information for PHM. However, few studies have been focused on these problems [27].

As DT has the potential to realize cyber–physical fusion, this chapter proposes a DT-driven PHM method. First, the DT for the complex equipment is built, including the physical equipment (PE), virtual equipment (VE), services (Ss), connection (CN), and DT data (DD). Second, the DT-driven PHM method, including the framework, the three-stage procedure, and the coevolution mechanism is explored. The framework defines the inputs, outputs, and roles of the DT in the proposed method; then the procedure is designed to deal with two types of faults (the gradual fault and abrupt fault) with different workflows; and the coevolution mechanism illustrates the evolution process for different parts in the DT during PHM. Finally, to verify the efficiency and advantages of the proposed DT modeling and DT-driven PHM methods, the yaw system and the gearbox of a wind turbine (WT) are taken as examples in the case study. Based on the DT, the above three common problems existing in the current work of PHM can be solved as follows: (1) According to the construction of the DT, a five-dimension DT for the equipment can be built, which describes the real equipment in multiple aspects and provides access to the equipment even out of physical proximity. (2) Utilizing the interaction mechanism in the DT, the difference between the physical equipment and the digital mirror can be used to detect the disturbances from the external environment, potential faults in the equipment, and defects in the virtual models. It is a coupled optimization for both the equipment and its digital mirror to better serve PHM. (3) Since the DT generates data from the equipment, the digital mirror, and the fused data, data for PHM activities (e.g., condition assessment, feature extraction, and prognostics) are enriched greatly, which can provide more comprehensive and accurate information [27].

## 7.2 DIGITAL TWIN FOR COMPLEX EQUIPMENT

### 7.2.1 Five-Dimension Digital Twin for Complex Equipment

According to the proposed five-dimension definition of the DT in Chapter 3, Five-Dimension Digital Twin Modeling and Its Key Technologies, a DT concept model for complex equipment is built as shown in Fig. 7.2 [27].

![](img/5042607b423be4a5ee3f15dae22cc5bb_147_0.png)

Figure 7.2 Five-dimension DT concept model for complex equipment [27].

The DT includes five components, that is, PE, VE, Ss, DD, and CN. PE refers to the physical equipment, operating in the real environment and completing prescribed tasks. VE represents the virtual equipment, which is the high-fidelity digital counterpart of the PE. It is composed of a set of models in multiple dimensions to describe the PE thoroughly. Ss stands for the services for both PE and VE. It optimizes the operations of the PE, and ensures the high fidelity of the VE through calibrating the VE parameters during its operation. DD is the DT data, including data from PE, VE, Ss, and domain knowledge, as well as the fused data. As the DD includes data from both physical and virtual aspects as well as their fusion, it can provide more comprehensive and accurate information. CN is the connection that ties different parts of the DT together [27].

To illustrate the models, a WT is taken as an example of complex equipment. For each dimension, some typical models, which have been studied in authors’ previous work [27], are shown in Fig. 7.3 and illustrated as follows.

- 1. **PE (i.e., physical WT).** The physical WT includes mainly the subsystems and the mounted sensors. The subsystems consist of the blade, generator, gearbox, and yaw system for transforming wind energy into mechanical and electrical energy. Sensors are deployed to collect the generator temperature, gearbox vibration, and power output.

- 2. **VE (i.e., virtual WT).** According to expression (2) in Chapter 3, Five-Dimension Digital Twin Modeling and Its Key Technologies, the virtual WT consists of a geometry model, physics model, behavior model, and rule model, expressed as $G_v$, $P_v$, $B_v$, and $R_v$, respectively. In $G_v$, the components (e.g., the blade, gearbox, and shaft) are constructed in three dimensions, then assembled to form the geometrical WT. In $P_v$, the blade deformation, gear tooth stress, bearing temperature, etc. can be simulated using the finite element method (FEM) to reflect the physical properties. $B_v$ includes the power generation, yawing, pitching, untwisting, etc. For example, the power generation can be described by the curve of the wind speed and power output. The yawing can be expressed by the integral relation between the yaw angle ($\varphi_y$) and the yaw rate ($\omega_y$), and the yaw rate ($\omega_y$) is denoted by a piecewise function of the yaw error (err) [28]. In $R_v$, the constraints for wind speed can be simulated through force analysis, and the associations of WT parameters can be mined using a neural network.
