
## CHAPTER 10
## Digital Twin and Services

Digital twin (DT) provides an effective way for the cyber-physical fusion of manufacturing. Meanwhile, services can make all the business processes and operation procedures in manufacturing realize servitization and standardization, to achieve higher efficiency. Combined with the services, the DT will be easier for usage and will generate more acceptable analysis and evaluation results on product design, manufacturing, usage, prognostics and health management (PHM), and other processes. This chapter specifies and highlights what manufacturing services are, how manufacturing services and the DT converge, and how various components of the DT are used by manufacturers in the form of services.

### 10.1 INTRODUCTION

As manufacturing evolves toward socialization and servitization, service plays an increasingly more important role in manufacturing [1]. More manufacturers are adopting service logic for their businesses to cope with intense competition and gain more revenue [2]. Services can shield the heterogeneities of resources, which are conducted by different vendors using various standards and communication protocols/interfaces, and enable interaction and integration [3]. With the characteristics of on-demand use, dynamic reconfiguration, and platform independence [4], services endow manufacturing with the advancement of large-scale sharing and collaboration [30].

In view of the concept of everything as a service (XaaS), services can fully release the potential of the digital twin (DT). Through services, each component of the DT, especially virtual models, which are not easy to create rapidly, can be shared and used in a convenient “pay-as-you-go” manner [5]. Moreover, services are an integral part in the working process of the DT, and many actions require the support of third-party services. For example, multisource data fusion requires algorithms, and computing and storage services [30]. Therefore, in this chapter, the DT and services are combined, and DT service generation, management, and application are specified.

### 10.2 SERVICES IN MANUFACTURING

#### 10.2.1 Concept of Servitization in Manufacturing

The concept of servitization in manufacturing, which has been studied by the authors in their previous work [26], is introduced in this section. The term servitization was coined by Vandermerwe et al. in 1988 to describe the phenomenon wherein more companies were adding value to their core products through services driven by customer demand [6]. In fact, servitization was conceptualized as an innovation of organizational capabilities and processes [7]. More companies have already expanded the scope of their services to enhance market competitiveness and gain more revenue [8]. In this context, service-oriented architecture (SoA) [9] provides an effective technique for servitization, and has been applied in various advanced manufacturing modes and systems, such as application service providers (ASPs) [10], manufacturing grids (MGrid) [11,12], product service systems (PSS)/industrial PSS (IPS2) [13,14], and cloud manufacturing (CMfg) [15,5]. For instance, distributed and heterogeneous manufacturing resource services in MGrid can be aggregated for collaborative operations with high efficiency [16]. Moreover, as a cloud-based, service-oriented manufacturing model embracing the concept of manufacturing as a service (MaaS) [17], CMfg can enable sharing of manufacturing resources and capabilities and cooperation in a convenient pay-as-you-go manner through services [18], to maximize the benefits of the manufacturing industry. CMfg has the characteristics of customer centricity, dynamic reconfiguration, on-demand mode, and sharing [19]. Services endow manufacturing enterprises with the advantage of coping with the challenges such as fierce global competition, increasing labor costs, and other kinds of pressures [2,20]. Because services have the characteristics of interoperability and platform independence, they pave the way for large-scale enterprise collaboration, leading to manufacturing socialization [21,22]. A complex manufacturing task can be accomplished collaboratively by many services from different companies [23,24]. In addition, many smart applications such as remote monitoring and diagnosis, predictive maintenance, operational analysis, and product usage (e.g., training, assistance, and interaction) are made possible through services [25]. Services bridge the gap between real production activities and logical collaboration [4], which can promote smart manufacturing applications.

#### 10.2.2 Framework of Service-Oriented Smart Manufacturing

As shown in Fig. 10.1, the abstract framework of service-oriented smart manufacturing (SoSM) consists of two parts, the physical space and the virtual space. The physical space comprises manufacturing resources and human resources. Manufacturing resources include materials, machine tools, machining centers, robots, products, and software resources. Human resources refer to workers on the shop-floor, managers in enterprise, and users in the product’s lifecycle. In addition, to interact with the virtual space, the physical space also includes sensor systems and actuator systems. The sensor systems are responsible for collecting various data of manufacturing resources and processes from the physical space. The actuators receive instructions from the virtual space and drive the manufacturing resources to achieve tasks according to smart decisions. All the manufacturing activities are accomplished collaboratively by humans, machines, and other entities in the physical space eventually [26].

The virtual space is composed of two important components. One of them is the virtual mapping of the physical space. Through real-time data acquisition, data integration, and analysis, it can dynamically track and monitor the status and progresses of the physical space, based on the virtual models from physical entities. Some data are generated from the interaction between the physical space and the virtual space. Besides the virtual models from physical entities, there are some mathematical models, computing models, and others. These services are encapsulated through a service-oriented approach, such as SoA. Some are resource services, which are encapsulated from the physical manufacturing resources and capabilities based on data from the physical space. Some other services are encapsulated from information elements (i.e., data as services, models as services, algorithms as services, knowledge as services, and rules as services). It should be noted that the physical resources (e.g., material, machines, products) and information elements (e.g., production data and algorithms) can be shared and used remotely by global customers because of service availability [26].

With the comprehensive analysis and processing capabilities for data and the interoperability characteristics of services in the virtual space, SoSM can cope with complex changes in the physical space through effective decision-making processes. In this process, there are four basic functions, that is, smart perception, smart analysis, autonomous decision making, and precise control and execution. First of all, various data from humans, materials, equipment, products, and systems in the physical space are accurately sensed through advanced sensing technologies. Then, the data acquired through perception are identified, processed, and analyzed quickly and accurately in the virtual space, as well as other data operations. According to the results of the analysis, decisions and choices are made autonomously in accordance with the rules and knowledge. Manufacturing resources are invoked in the form of services. Finally, on the basis of the decision making, the physical space is adjusted according to dynamic changes [26].
