# Digital Twin and Cloud, Fog, Edge Computing

Digital twin (DT) is the preferred means to achieve the interoperability and integration between the physical and virtual spaces. From a hierarchical perspective, DTs can be divided into unit level, system level, and system of systems (SoS) level. To meet the practical requirements on smart manufacturing, three complementary technologies, that is, edge computing, fog computing, and cloud computing, are introduced to combine with DTs in different levels for function enhancement. In this chapter, the three-level DTs in manufacturing are described first, then the perspectives of unit-level DT, system-level DT, and SoS-level DT respectively supported by edge computing, fog computing, and cloud computing are discussed.

## 8.1 INTRODUCTION

From the manufacturing development perspective, a fusion process between the physical and virtual spaces of manufacturing is evolving over time. First, before the advent of information technologies, manufacturing only involved the physical space. Specifically, all manufacturing activities were done by humans and/or machines. The lack of information limited manufacturing efficiency and production capacities. In the second stage, the virtual space appeared. A variety of manufacturing-related software products were developed and applied in production. However, due to technological limitations, the advantages and potential applications of virtual space had not been fully exploited. In the third stage, the physical space and the virtual space began to interact with each other. The virtual space has strengthened the management of the manufacturing process, optimizing the logistics, capital flow, and information flow. However, during this stage, the physical and virtual spaces of manufacturing were not synchronized. At present, with the continuous developments of new generation information technologies (New IT), real-time interaction and further integration between the physical and virtual spaces is becoming an inevitable trend in smart manufacturing [16].

According to the horizontal analysis on various manufacturing strategies and vertical analysis on manufacturing development, the cyber–physical fusion of manufacturing is gaining increasing importance. To this end, digital twin (DT) has become the preferred technology. The DT creates virtual models for physical objects in a digital way to simulate their behaviors, serving as a bridge between the physical and virtual spaces [1]. Through the cyber–physical closed loop, the DT could promote smartness of all manufacturing processes [16].

Smart manufacturing is a value creation process in which multiple subjects participate. Depending upon different magnitudes of the data in smart manufacturing and different time-sensitivities of smart applications, the DTs can be divided into three different levels, that is, the unit level, system level, and system of systems (SoS) level [2]. Each level has a data closed loop, including status perception, data analysis, decision making, and execution. However, as various levels have different roles and infrastructures, how to build various levels of DTs toward smart manufacturing becomes a central issue [16].

Edge computing, fog computing, and cloud computing may offer solutions to the abovementioned issues. Cloud computing enables ubiquitous, convenient, on-demand network access to the shared resources pool [3]. Because of on-demand resource sharing, high computing and storage capabilities, as well as low costs, cloud computing is a perfect technological means for the SoS-level DT. Fog computing shifts the computing, storage, and networking capabilities of cloud to the edge network [4]. By directly processing data on the network (e.g., network routers, various information systems, etc.), fog computing can help implement the system-level DT. Edge computing allows data processing to be performed at closer proximity to the data sources [5]. The edge can be defined as the end points with both data producers and data consumers. For the unit-level DT, edge computing will be very beneficial [16].

In this chapter, edge computing, fog computing, and cloud computing are introduced for implementing the unit-level, system-level, and SoS-level DTs, respectively, toward smart manufacturing. The three-level DTs in manufacturing are described first, then edge computing, fog computing and cloud computing are briefly reviewed. Finally, a framework of the three-level DTs based on these technologies is discussed.

## 8.2 THREE-LEVEL DIGITAL TWINS IN MANUFACTURING

By building a DT system that integrates data from different objects, activities, and processes, the innovation and efficiency of product design, production planning, and implementation can be enhanced effectively. The DT is an important supporting technology for smart manufacturing. To better understand the DT, a systematic and hierarchical view should be established. For smart manufacturing, both small objects such as a piece of equipment or a production line, and large objects such as a shop-floor or an entire factory, can be considered as DTs. Therefore, the DTs need to be divided into different levels, including unit level, system level, and SoS level, to adapt to objects in different granularity [6]. The DT in each level is a systematic model that is comprised of a previous level or levels. The system-level DT can be considered as an integration of multiple unit-level DTs that cooperate with each other. The multiple unit-level DTs or multiple system-level DTs constitute the SoS-level DT, that is, a complex system including complicated relations, associations, and combinations. As shown in Fig. 8.1, the unit-level DT mainly serves for a single object (e.g., a piece of equipment), which is the smallest unit participating in manufacturing activities. The optimization of manufacturing can be achieved through the adjustment of the equipment. With respect to the system-level DT, a smart production line comprised of machine tools, robot arms, automated guided vehicles (AGVs), etc. can be considered as a system with specific production capacities. For the unit-level and system-level DTs, the virtual models are ultrahigh-fidelity mappings through digital descriptions of the equipment and production line from the perspectives of geometric shape, function, and operating status. Basic attributes, real-time status, and other data are transmitted to the virtual models to drive simulation and prediction. Meanwhile, parameters of the virtual models are fed back to optimize the physical entities. By the closed-loop interaction process, the physical entities and virtual models evolve simultaneously. For the SoS-level (e.g., a shop-floor), accurate shop-floor management and reliable operations, which are inseparable from services, are especially crucial. As shown in Fig. 8.1, (1) physical entities are a set of objective entities that have specific functions to complete manufacturing tasks according to inputs and outputs; (2) virtual models are the digital images of the physical entities and can completely and truly reflect the lifecycle of the physical counterparts; (3) services integrate various functions such as control, optimization, and prediction to provide application services according to the requirements; (4) data is the core driver of the DT, including the data from the physical entities, virtual models, services, as well as their fusion data; and (5) connections among them place the above four parts in pairs, ensuring real-time interaction and iterative optimization. Based on the five-dimension structure of the DT, the digital twin shop-floor (DTS) provides a new way to practice smart manufacturing [6].
