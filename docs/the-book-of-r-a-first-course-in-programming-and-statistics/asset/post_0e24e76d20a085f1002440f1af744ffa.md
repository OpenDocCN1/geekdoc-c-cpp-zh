
The CI given by (19.37, 23.99) is therefore interpreted as follows: you are 95 percent confident that the true mean maximum temperature in Dunedin lies somewhere between 19.37 and 23.99 degrees Celsius.

With this result, you've combined knowledge of the estimate of the mean itself with the inherent variability of a sample to define an interval of values in which you're sure the true mean will lie. As you know, the true mean in this is 22, which is indeed included in the calculated CI.

From this, it's easy to change the confidence levels, which as always, must define α/2 in each tail. For example, 80 percent CI (α = 0.2) and a 99 percent CI (α = 0.01) for the same example value here can be found with two lines, respectively:

```
R> temp.mean+c(-1,1)*qt(p=0.9,df=4)*temp.se
[1] 20.40372 22.95788
R> temp.mean+c(-1,1)*qt(p=0.995,df=4)*temp.se
[1] 17.84593 25.51557
```

Note here the use of multiplication by the vector c(-1,1) so that the lower and upper limits can be obtained at once and the result returned as a vector of 2. Usually, the qt function is used with respect to a complete lower-tail area, so p is set at 1-α/2.

These most recent intervals highlight the natural consequence of going to a higher confidence level for a given CI. A higher probability in the central area translates to a more extreme critical value, resulting in a wider interval. This makes sense—in order to be “more confident” about the true parameter value, you'd need to take into account a larger range of possible values.

## 17.2.2 An Interval for a Proportion

Establishing a CI for a sample proportion follows the same rules as for the mean. With knowledge of the sampling distribution as per Section 17.1.2, you obtain critical values from the standard normal distribution, and for an estimate p from a sample of size n, the interval itself is constructed with the standard error √(p(1-p)/n).

Let's return to the example from Section 17.1.2, where 80 of 118 individuals said that they knew how they were going to vote in the next US presidential election. Recall we have the following:

```
R> p.hat <- 80/118
R> p.hat
[1] 0.6779661
R> p.se <- sqrt(p.hat*(1-p.hat)/118)
R> p.se
[1] 0.04301439
```

To construct a 90 percent CI (α=0.1), the appropriate critical value from the standardized sampling distribution of interest is as follows, implying Pr(-1.644854 < Z < 1.644854) = 0.9 for Z ~ N(0,1):

```
R> qnorm(0.95)
[1] 1.644854
```

Now you again follow Equation (17.2):

```
R> p.hat+c(-1,1)*qnorm(0.95)*p.se
[1] 0.6072137 0.7478185
```

You conclude that you're 90 percent confident that the true proportion of voters who know how they will vote in the next election lies somewhere between 0.61 and 0.75 (rounded to two decimal places).

## 17.23 Other Intervals

The simple situations presented in Sections 17.2.1 and 17.2.2 serve to highlight the importance of associating any point estimate (in other words, a sample statistic) with the idea of its variability. Confidence intervals can be constructed for other quantities, and over the following sections (as part of testing hypotheses), I will expand on the discussion of confidence intervals to investigate differences between means and proportions, as well as ratios of categorical counts. These more complicated statistics come with their standard error formulas, though the corresponding sampling distributions are all symmetric via the normal and t-curves (if, again, some standard assumptions are met), which means that the now familiar formulation of Equation (17.2) still applies.

Generally, a confidence interval serves to mark off a central area of 1-α from the sampling distribution of interest, including those that are asymmetric. In these cases, however, it doesn't make sense to have a symmetric CI based on a single, standardized critical value as in Equation (17.2). Similarly, you might not know the functional, parametric form of the sampling distribution and so may not be willing to make any. In these cases, you can take an alternative path based on the raw quantiles (or estimated quantiles; see Section 17.2.3) of the supposed asymmetric sampling distribution. Using specific quantiles to mark off α/2 upper- and lower-tail areas is a valid method that remains sensitive to the shape of the sampling distribution, still allowing you to construct a useful interval that describes true parameter values.

## 17.2.4 Comments on Interpretation of a CI

The typical statement about the interpretation of any CI references a degree of confidence in where the true parameter value lies, but a more formally correct interpretation should consider and clarify the probabilistic nature of the construction. Technically, given a 100(1-α) percent confidence level, the more accurate interpretation is as follows: many samples of the same size and from the same population with a CI, of the same confidence level, is constructed with respect to the same statistic from each sample, you would expect the true corresponding parameter value to fall within the limits of 100(1-α) percent of such intervals.

This comes from the fact that the theory of a sampling distribution describes the variability in multiple samples, not just the sample that has been taken. At first glance it may be difficult to fully appreciate the difference between this and the colloquially used "confidence statement", but is important to remain aware of the technically correct definition, particularly given that a CI is typically estimated based on only one sample.
