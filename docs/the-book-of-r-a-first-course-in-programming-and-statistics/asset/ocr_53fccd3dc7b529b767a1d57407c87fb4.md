The problem is here that by ignoring one of the variables (or, more generally, statistical relationships) that occur at a finer level. For example, the wool type alone seems to have no remarkable impact on the mean of warp breaks, you can not tell whether this would be the case if you looked at wool types at a particular level of tension.

Instead, you investigate this kind of question using two-way ANOVA. The following executes a two-way ANOVA for the warp breaks based only on the main effects of the two grouping variables:

Take a look at the formula. Specifying wool+tension to the right of the outcome variable and "allows" you to take both grouping variables into account at the same time. The results reveal a small drop in the size of the p-values for wool is 0.073, approaching the conventional cutoff significance of a = 0.05. To interpret the results, you hold one grouping variable constant—if you focus on just one type of wool, there is still significant evidence to suggest a difference in the mean number of warp breaks between the different tension levels. If you focus on just one level of tension, the evidence of a difference considering the two wool types has increased a little but is still statistically significant (assuming the aforementioned a = 0.05).

There's a limitation with considering only means. While the previous analysis shows that's variation in the outcome between the two categorical variables, it doesn't address the possibility that a difference in the mean number of warp breaks might vary when holding the other variable constant. This relatively subtle yet important consideration is known as an interaction. Specifically, if there is an interactive effect between tension and wool with respect to warp breaks, then this would imply that the magnitude and/or direction of the difference in the mean number of warp breaks is *not the same* at different levels of the two grouping factors.
