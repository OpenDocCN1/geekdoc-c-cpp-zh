A rule of thumb to check the condition on $n$ and $\pi$ simply involves checking that $\hat{n}$ and $n(1 - \hat{ho})$ are both greater than 5, where $\hat{ho}$ is the sample estimate of $\pi$.

It's worth noting that the standard error in the case of tests involving proportions is itself dependent upon $\pi$. This is important—remember that any hypothesis test assumes satisfaction of $H_0$ in the relevant calculations. When dealing with proportions, that means when computing the test statistic, the standard error must use of the null value $\pi$ rather than the estimated sample proportion $\hat{ho}$.

I'll clarify this in an example. Suppose an individual of a particular fast-food chain notices that he has to have an upset stomach within a certain amount of time after having his lunch. He comes across the website of a blogger who believes that the chance of getting an upset stomach shortly after eating that particular food is 20 percent. The individual is curious to determine whether true rate of stomach upset $\pi$ is any different from the blogger's quoted value and, over time, visits these fast-food outlets for lunch on $n=29$ separate occasions, recording the success (TRUE) or failure (FALSE) of experiencing an upset stomach. This suggests the following pair of hypotheses:

$H_0: \pi = 0.2$\nH_A: \pi \neq 0.2$

These may be tested according to the general rules discussed in the following sections.

# Calculation: One-Sample Z-Test

In testing for the true value of some proportion of success, $\pi$, let $\hat{ho}$ be the sample proportion over $n$ trials, and the null value be denoted with $\pi_0$. You find the test statistic with the following:

```
$Z = \frac{\hat{ho} - \pi_0}{\sqrt{\pi_0(1-\pi_0)/n}}$
```

Provided the aforementioned conditions on the size of $n$ and the value of $\pi$ can be assumed, $Z \sim N(0,1).
The denominator of Equation (18.8), the standard error of the proportion, is calculated with respect to the null value $\pi_0$, not $\hat{ho}$. As mentioned moment ago, this is to satisfy the assumption of "truth" of $H_0$ as the test is carried out, so it allows interpretation of the resulting $p$-value as usual. The standard normal distribution is used to find the p-value with respect to $Z$; the direction underneath this curve is governed by the nature of $H_A$ just as before.
