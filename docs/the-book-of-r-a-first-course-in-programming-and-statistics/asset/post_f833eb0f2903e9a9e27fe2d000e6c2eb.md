
based on the type of wool (p-value 0.108). If you ignore wool, however, there is evidence to suggest a difference in warp breaks according to tension only.

The problem is here that by ignoring one of the variables (or, more generally, statistical relationships) that occur at a finer level. For example, the wool type alone seems to have no remarkable impact on the mean of warp breaks, you can not tell whether this would be the case if you looked at wool types at a particular level of tension.

Instead, you investigate this kind of question using two-way ANOVA. The following executes a two-way ANOVA for the warp breaks based only on the main effects of the two grouping variables:

```
R> summary(aov(breaks~wool+tension,data=warpbreaks))
```

Take a look at the formula. Specifying wool+tension to the right of the outcome variable allows you to take both grouping variables into account at the same time. The results reveal a small drop in the size of the p-values for wool is 0.073, approaching the conventional cutoff significance of a = 0.05. To interpret the results, you hold one grouping variable constant—if you focus on just one type of wool, there is still significant evidence to suggest a difference in the mean number of warp breaks between the different tension levels. If you focus on just one level of tension, the evidence of a difference considering the two wool types has increased a little but is still statistically significant (assuming the aforementioned a = 0.05).

There's a limitation with considering only means. While the previous analysis shows that's variation in the outcome between the two categorical variables, it doesn't address the possibility that a difference in the mean number of warp breaks might vary when holding the other variable constant. This relatively subtle yet important consideration is known as an interaction. Specifically, if there is an interactive effect between tension and wool with respect to warp breaks, then this would imply that the magnitude and/or direction of the difference in the mean number of warp breaks is *not the same* at different levels of the two grouping factors.

To account for this, you make a slight adjustment to the two-way ANOVA model code.

```
R> summary(aov(breaks~wool+tension+wool:tension,data=warpbreaks))
```

You can explicitly specify the interaction as the main effects formula plus the notation wool:tension, where the two grouping variables are separated by a: (Note, in this setting: : operator has nothing to do with the shortcut for an integer sequence first discussed in Section 2.3.2).

You can see the ANOVA table output, statistically, there is evidence of an interactive effect; that, the very nature of the difference in the means is dependent upon the factor levels, even though that evidence is relatively weak. Of course, the p-value of 0.021 tells you only that, there might be an interaction but not the precise features of an interaction.

To help with this, you can interpret such a two-way effect in detail with an interaction plot, in R with interaction.plot.

```
R> interaction.plot(x.factor=wb.means,[1,2], trace.factor=wb.means,[1], response=wb.means$x,trace.label="wool", xlabel="tension","ylab="mean warp breaks")
```

When interaction.plot is called, the outcome means should be supplied to the argument response, and the vectors providing the corresponding levels of each of the two factors should be supplied to the arguments x.factor (for the variable on the horizontal axis that refers to levels from the left) and trace.factor (each level of which will produce a different line, in an automatically produced legend; the title of this legend is trace.label). It doesn't matter which grouping variable is; the appearance of the plot will change accurately, but your interpretations (will!) be the. The result is shown in Figure 19-2.

The two-way interaction plot displays the outcome variable on the vertical axis and splits the recorded means by the levels of the two grouping variables. This allows you to inspect the potential effect that varying the levels of the grouping variables has on the outcome. In general, when the lines (or segments thereof) are not parallel, it suggests an interaction could be present. Vertical separations between the plotted locations indicate the main effects of the grouping variables.

It turns out that the columns returned by a call to aggregate are actually perfectly suited to interaction.plot. As usual, you can specify the common graphical parameters, like those you initially encountered in Section 7.2, to control specific features of the plot and axis annotation. For Figure 19-2,

you've specified that x.factor should be the second column of the wb.means matrix, meaning that the tension levels vary horizontally. The trace.factor is the type of wool, so there are only two distinct lines corresponding to the two levels A and B. The response is that third of wb.means, extracted using $x (take a look at the wb.means object; you'll see the column containing results of interest is labeled x by default after a call to aggregate).

Considering the actual appearance of the plot in Figure 19-2, it does indeed appear that the mean number of warp breaks for wool type A is higher if tension is low, but the nature of the difference changes if you move to a medium tension, where B has a higher point estimate than A. Interactions are certainly not a concept unique to multiple-factor ANOVA; they form an important consideration in many different types of statistical models. For the moment, it's good just to gain a basic appreciation of interactions.

## 19.3 Kruskal-Wallis Test

When comparing multiple means, there may be situations when you're unwilling to assume normality or have even found the assumption of normality invalid in diagnostic checks. In this case, you can use the Kruskal-Wallis test, an alternative to the one-way ANOVA that relaxes the dependence

on the necessity of normality. This method tests for "equality of distributions" of the measurements in each level of the grouping factor. If you make the usual assumption of equal variances across these groups, you can therefore think of this test as one compares multiple medians rather than

The hypotheses governing the test alter accordingly.

$H_0$: Group medians are all equal.
$H_1$: Group medians are not all equal
(alternatively, at least one median differs).

The Kruskal-Wallis test is a nonparametric approach since it does rely on quantiles of a standardized parametric distribution (in other words, or any of its functions). In the way that the ANOVA is a generalization of the two-sample-t test, the KruskalWallis ANOVA is a generalization of the Mann-Whitney test for two medians. It's also referred to as the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, and you use the chi-squared distribution to calculate the p-value.

Turn your attention to the data frame survey, located in the survey package. These data record particular characteristics of 237 first-year undergraduate students collected from a class at the University of Adelaide, South Australia. Load the required package first with a call to library("survey") and then enter ?survey at the prompt. You can read the file to understand which variables are present in the data frame.

Suppose you're interested in whether the age of the students, Age, tends to differ with respect to four smoking categories reported in Smoke. Inspection of the relevant side-by-side boxplots and a QQ plot of the residuals (mean-centered observations with respect to each group) suggests a straightforward one-way ANOVA isn't necessarily a good idea. The following code (which mimics the steps you saw in Section 19.1.1) produces the two images in Figure 19-3, which show normality is:

```
R: boxplot(~Smoke,data=survey)
R: age.means <- tapply(survey$Age,survey$smoke,mean)
R: age.mean <- survey$Age.means[as.numeric(survey$Smoke)]
R: qqnorm(age.mean,main="Normal QQ plot of residuals")
R: qline(age.mean)
```

With this violation of normality, you could therefore apply the Kruskal-Wallis test instead of the parametric ANOVA. A quick check for equality of variances further supports this, the ratio of the largest to the smallest group standard deviations clearly being less than 2.
