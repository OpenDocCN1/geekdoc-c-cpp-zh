The following are the keys points to remember:

+   * The entities, features, or events being counted independently in a well-defined interval at a constant rate.
* X can take only non-negative integers: $0, 1, \dots$
* $\lambda_p$ can be interpreted as the "mean number of occurrences" and must be finite and strictly positive; that is, $0 < \lambda_p < \infty$.

The mean and variance are as follows:

$$\mu_X = \lambda_p \quad \text{and} \quad \sigma_X^2 = \lambda_p$$

Like the binomial variable, the values taken by a Poisson random variable are discrete, non-negative integers. Unlike the binomial, there's typically no upper limit on a Poisson count. While implies that an "infinite count" is allowed to occur, it's a distinct feature of the Poisson distribution that the probability mass associated with some value $x$ goes to zero as $x$ itself goes to infinity.

As noted in Equation (16.4), any Poisson distribution depends upon the specification of a single parameter, denoted here with $\lambda_p$. This parameter describes the mean number of occurrences, which impacts the overall shape of the mass function, as shown in Figure 16-2.

Again, it's worth noting that the total probability mass over all possible outcomes is 1, no matter the value of $\lambda_p$ is regardless of the fact that possible outcomes can, technically, range from 0 to infinity.

By definition, it is easy to explain why the mean of $X$, $\mu_X$, is equal to $\lambda_p$; in turns out that the variance of a Poisson distributed random variable is also equal to $\lambda_p$.
