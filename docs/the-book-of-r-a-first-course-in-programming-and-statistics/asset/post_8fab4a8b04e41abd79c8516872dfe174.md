## 22.2.5 Other Selection Algorithms

Any model selection algorithm will always aim to quantitatively define parsimony and suggest a model that optimizes in light of available data. There are alternatives to AIC, such as the corrected AIC (BIC), or the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), of which impose heavier penalties on complexity than the default AIC in (22.3).

Sometimes it's tempting to simply monitor $R^2$, the coefficient of determination, for a series of models. However, as mentioned in Section 22.2.1, this is inadequate for choosing between models since it will always increase as you continue to add predictors, which have a statistically significant impact or not. The adjusted $R^2$ statistic, denoted $R^2_{adj}$ and reported as Adjusted R-squared in summary, is a simple transformation of the original $R^2$ that incorporates a penalty for complexity relative to the sample size $n$; calculated as

$$R^2_{adj} = 1 - \frac{(1-R^2)(n-1)}{n}$$

where $p$ is the number of predictor terms (excluding the intercept). The adjusted criteria are always preferable (since interpretation of $R^2$ can be difficult), but monitoring $R^2$ can be useful as a quick check between models—higher value to a preferred model.

For further reading, Chapter 8 of Faraway (2005) provides excellent commentary on the nature of selection procedures. Regardless of which approach you employ, always remember that any final model reached using these algorithms should be subject to scrutiny.

## 22.3 Residual Diagnostics

In previous chapters, you examined the practical aspects of multiple regression, such as fitting and interpreting, dummy coding, and so on, but havent yet looked at methods essential for determining the validity of your model. final part of this chapter will introduce you to model diagnostics, the primary goal of which is to ensure that your regression model is valid and accurately represents the relationships in your data. For this, I will return to the theoretical assumptions underpinning the linear regression model that were noted early in Section 22.1.1. As a refresher, in general when fitting these models, remember to keep these four things in mind:

- **Errors**: The error $\epsilon$, which defines the departure of any observation from the fitted mean, is assumed to be normally distributed with a mean of zero and constant variance denoted by $\sigma^2$. The error associated with a given observation is also assumed to be independent of the error of any other observation.
- **Linearity**: It is critical to assume that the mean response as a function is in terms of regression parameters $\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots \beta_p$. Though transformations of individual variables relax the trends, any suggestion that a relationship is nonlinear (and not captured by the fitted model at hand) must be investigated.
- **Extreme or unusual observations**: Always inspect data points that strongly influence the fitted model—points recorded incorrectly should be removed from the analysis.
- **Collinearity**: Predictors highly correlated with one another can adversely affect every model, meaning it can be easy to misinterpret the effects of included predictors. This should be avoided in regression.

Any violation of these assumptions diminishes the reliability of your model, sometimes severed. There are statistical tests you can perform to diagnose the model, but commonly a diagnostic inspection boils down to interpretation of results. Interpreting these plots can be quite difficult and only becomes easier with experience. Here, I'll provide an overview of these tools in R and describe some things to look for. For a more detailed discussion, look to dedicated texts on regression such as Chatterjee et al. (2000), Faraway (2005), or Montgomery et al. (2012).

### 22.3.1 Inspecting and Interpreting Residuals

If you look back at the plot in Figure 22-2, youll see a good demonstration of the importance of interpreting the results by $\hat{y}$ as mean response. Under the assumed model, any deviation of the raw observations from the fitted line is deemed to be the result of the (normally distributed) errors defined by the $\epsilon$ term in Equation (20.1). Of course, in practice, you't have the error values because you dont know the true model of your data. For the ith response observation $y_i$ and its fitted value from the model, $\hat{y}_i$, you would typically assess diagnostic plots using the estimated residuals $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$. A call to summary encourages a post-fit analysis of the residuals by providing you with a five-number summary of the $e_i$ above the table of estimated coefficients. This allows you to take a look at their values and do a preliminary assessment of the symmetry of their distribution (as required by the assumption of normality—see Section 22.3.2).

As well as a diagnostic inspection of the raw residuals $e_i$, some diagnostic checks can also be done using thestandardized values. The standardized residuals rescale the residuals $e_i$ to ensure they have the same variance, which is important if you need to directly compare them to one another. Formally, this is achieved with the calculation $e_i/(\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1-h_{ii}})$, where $\hat{\sigma}$ is the estimate of the standard error and $h_{ii}$ is the leverage of the ith observation (youll learn about in Section 22.4).

Arguably the most common graphical tool for residuals is a simple scatterplot of "observed-minus-fitted" residuals on the vertical axis against their corresponding fitted-model values of the regression. If the assumptions concerning $e$ are valid, then the $e_i$ should appear randomly scattered around zero (since the errors arent't assumed to be related in any way to the value of the response). Any systematic pattern in the plot suggests the residuals dont agree with the assumptions—this could be because of nonlinear relationships in your data or the presence of dependent observations (in other words, your data points are correlated and therefore not independent of one another). The plot can also be used to detect *heteroscedasticity*-a nonconstant variance in the residuals—commonly seen as a “fanning out” of the residuals around zero as the fitted values increase.

Note that more than these theoretical assumptions are valid because they affect the validity of the regression coefficients and the reliability of their standard errors (and so statistical significance)—in other words, the correctness of your interpretation of impact on the response.

To give you a better idea of this, consider the three images in Figure 22. These provide residuals versus fitted values for three hypothetical scenarios.

The plot on left, is more or less, what you're looking for—residuals appear randomly scattered around zero, and their spread around zero appears constant (homoscedasticity). In the middle plot, however, you can see systematic behavior in the residuals. The variability remains constant throughout the range of fitted values, the apparent trend suggests the current model isnt't explaining some of the relationship between response and predictor. On the right, the residuals seem to be scattered randomly about zero again. However, the variability they exhibit isn't constant. Among other things, this kind of heteroscedasticity will affect the reliability of your confidence and prediction intervals.
