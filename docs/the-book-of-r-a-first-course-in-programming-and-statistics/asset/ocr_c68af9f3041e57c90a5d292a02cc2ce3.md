# # 15.2 Random Variables and Probability Distributions

A random variable is a variable whose specific outcomes are assumed to arise by chance or according to some random or stochastic mechanism.

You've already encountered *variables*-characteristics that describe an individual entity based on data you've observed (Section 13.1). When you're considering random variables, however, you have not yet met an observation. The chances of observing a specific value, or within a specific interval, for random variable has associated with it a probability. It makes sense to think of random variables as being tied to a function that defines these probabilities, which is referred to as a *probability distribution*. In this section, you'll look at some elementary ways in how random variables are summarized and how their corresponding probability distributions are dealt with statistically.

# ## 15.2.1 Realizations

So, the concept of a random variable revolves around the consideration of the possible outcomes of a variable in a probabilistic fashion. When you've actually made observations of a random variable, these are referred to as *realizations*.

Consider the following—suppose you roll your beloved die. Define the random variable $Y$ to be the result. The possible realizations are $Y=1, Y=2, Y=3, Y=4, Y=5$, and $Y=6$.

Now, let's say you're planning to go on a picnic and monitor the daily temperature at your preferred spot. Let the random variable $W$ be the temperature in degrees Fahrenheit you observe. Technically, you might say that the possible realizations of $W$ in the interval $-\infty < W < \infty$.

These examples serve to illustrate two types of random variables. $Y$ is a *discrete random variable*; $W$ is a *continuous random variable*. Whether any random variable is discrete or continuous has consequences for the way in which you think about, and may utilize, the probabilities associated with making realizations.
