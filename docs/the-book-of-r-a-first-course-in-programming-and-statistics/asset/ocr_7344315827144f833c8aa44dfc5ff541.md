Returning to the die example, what is the probability on a single
toss you roll an even number and it's a 4 or more? Using the fact that
Pr(A|B) = $\frac{1}{2}$ and that Pr(B) = $\frac{1}{3}$, it is easy to compute Pr(A $\cap$ B) = $\frac{1}{3} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{6}$
and confirm this in R if you really want to.

```
R> (2/3)+(1/2)
[1] 0.3333333
```

You can see that the events are not mutually exclusive because
Pr(A $\cap$ B) $
eq$ 0. This makes sense—it's perfectly possible in a die roll to
observe a number that's both even and at least 4.

# 15.1.4 Union

The union of events is written as Pr(A $\cup$ B) and is read as "the
probability that A or B occurs." Here is the representation of a union as a Venn
diagram:

![](bbox=[0.313, 0.396, 0.715, 0.532])

Theoretically, you have this:

> Pr(A $\cup$ B) = Pr(A) + Pr(B) - Pr(A $\cap$ B)  (15.2)

The reason you need to subtract the intersection in this diagram is that
summing Pr(A) and Pr(B) alone, you'd be incorrectly counting Pr(A $\cap$ B)
tice. Note, though, if the two events are mutually exclusive, then Equa-
tion (15.2) simplifies to Pr(A $\cup$ B) = Pr(A) + Pr(B).

So, rolling in the die, what is the probability that you observe an
en number or one that's at least 4? Using (15.2), it's easy to find
Pr(A $\cup$ B) = $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{6}$. The following confirms this in R:

```
R> (1/2)+(1/2)-(1/3)
[1] 0.6666667
```
