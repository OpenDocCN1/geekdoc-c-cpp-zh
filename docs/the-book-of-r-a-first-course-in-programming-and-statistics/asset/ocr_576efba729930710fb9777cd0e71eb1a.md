you typically use the ". notation, in which the dots refer to the definition of the model in the first argument. Specifically, the dots stand for "what is already there.\ In other words, through scope you're telling that the fullest model you'd consider cost as the response, an intercept, and main effects of all other predictors in the nuclear data frame (I restrict the full model to maintain effects only for ease of demonstration). You don't need to supply the data frame as an argument since those data are contained within the model object in the first argument. Lastly, you tell which to perform. There are a handful of variants available (see ?add1), but here you'll stick with "F" for partial F-tests.

Now, on the output that's provided directly after the execution

# Single term additions

Signif. codes: 0 *** '0.001 ** '0.01 * '0.05 ' 1

The output comprises a series of rows, with <none> (doing nothing to the current model). You receive the Sum of Sq and RSS values, related to calculating the test statistic. The differences in degrees of freedom are also reported. Another measure of parsimony, AIC, is also provided (you'll look at that in more detail in Section 22.2.4).

Most relevant are the outcomes; with "F", each row corresponds to an independent partial F-test comparing the model in the first argument, with the model from having added that row term only as yred_full. Usually, therefore, you would update your model by adding only the term with the largest (and most significant) improvement.

Here, you should be able to see that adding date as a predictor offers largest significant improvement to modeling cost. Let's update nuc.0 to include that term with the code.
