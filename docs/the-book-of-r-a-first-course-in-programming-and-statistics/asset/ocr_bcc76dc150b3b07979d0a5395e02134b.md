# 18.5 Errors and Power

In discussing all these forms of statistical hypothesis testing, there has one common thread: the interpretation of a p-value and what it tells you about your problem in terms of the hypotheses. Frequentist statistical hypothesis testing is ubiquitous in many fields of research, so it is important to at least briefly related concepts.

# 18.5.1 Hypothesis Test Errors

Hypothesis testing is performed with the objective of obtaining a p-value to quantify evidence against the null statement $H_0$. This is rejected in favor of the alternative, $H_A$, if the p-value is itself less than a predefined significance level $\alpha$, which is conventionally 0.05 or 0.01. As Touched upon, this approach is justifiably criticized since the choice of $\alpha$ is essentially arbitrary; a decision to reject or retain $H_0$ can change depending solely on the $\alpha$ value.

Consider for the moment, given a specific test, what the correct outcome is. If $H_0$ is really true, then you want to retain it. If $H_A$ is really true, you want to reject the null. "truth," one way or another, is impossible to know in practice. Being said, it's useful to consider in a theoretical sense how good (or bad) a given hypothesis test is at yielding a result leads to the correct conclusion.

To test the validity of your rejection or retention of the null hypothesis, you must be able to identify two kinds of errors:

+   * A Type I error occurs when you incorrectly reject a true $H_0$. In any given hypothesis test, the probability of a Type I error is equivalent to the significance level $\alpha$.
