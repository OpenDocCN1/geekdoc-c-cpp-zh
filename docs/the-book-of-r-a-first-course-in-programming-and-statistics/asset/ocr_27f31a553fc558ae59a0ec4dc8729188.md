The hypotheses governing the test alter accordingly.

$H_0$: Group medians are all equal.
$H_1$: Group medians are not all equal
(alternatively, at least one median differs).

The Kruskal-Wallis test is a nonparametric approach since it does rely on quantiles of a standardized parametric distribution (in other words, or any of its functions). In the way that the ANOVA is a generalization of the two-sample-t test, the KruskalWallis ANOVA is a generalization of the Mann-Whitney test for two medians. It's also referred to as the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, and you use the chi-squared distribution to calculate the p-value.

Turn your attention to the data frame survey, located in the survey package. These data record particular characteristics of 237 first-year undergraduate students collected from a class at the University of Adelaide, South Australia. Load the required package first with a call to library("survey") and then enter ?survey at the prompt. You can read the file to understand which variables are present in the data frame.

Suppose you're interested in whether the age of the students, Age, tends to differ with respect to four smoking categories reported in Smoke. Inspection of the relevant side-by-side boxplots and a QQ plot of the residuals (mean-centered observations with respect to each group) suggests a straightforward one-way ANOVA isn't necessarily a good idea. The following code (which mimics the steps you saw in Section 19.1.1) produces the two images in Figure 19-3, which show normality is:

```
R: boxplot(~Smoke,data=survey)
R: age.means <- tapply(survey$Age,survey$smoke,mean)
R: age.mean <- survey$Age.means[as.numeric(survey$Smoke)]
R: qqnorm(age.mean,main="Normal QQ plot of residuals")
R: qline(age.mean)
```
