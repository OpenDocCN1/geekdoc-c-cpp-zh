The different types of trend lines fitted to the mileage and displacement clearly show different interpretations of the relationship. Actually, you could reasonably argue that the simple linear fit is inadequate at modeling the relationship between response and predictor, but it's harder to come to a clear conclusion when choosing between the order 2 and 3 versions. The order 2 fit captures the curve that tapers off as $disp$ increases; the order 3 fit allows for a bump (in technical terms a saddle or inflection), followed by a steeper downward trend in the same domain.

So, which model is “best?” In this case, the statistical significance of the parameters suggests that the order 3 model should be preferred. Having said that, there are other things to consider when choosing different models, you'll think about more carefully in Section 22.2.

# Pitfalls of Polynomials

One particular drawback associated with polynomial terms in linear regression models is the instability of the fitted trend when trying to perform any kind of extrapolation. The right plot in Figure 21-4 shows the three fitted models (MPG by displacement), but this time with a much wider scale for displacement. As you can see, the validity of these models is questionable-although the order 2 and 3 models fit acceptably within the range of the observed data, if you move even slightly outside the maximum threshold of observed displacement values, the predictions of the mean mileage go wildly off. The order 2 model in particular becomes nonsensical, suggesting a rapid improvement in MPG once the engine displacement over 500 cubic inches. You must keep this mathematical behavior of polynomial functions in mind if you're considering higher-order terms in your regression models.

As such, any transformation applied to individual variables doesn't affect the linearity of the function with respect to the coefficients themselves.
