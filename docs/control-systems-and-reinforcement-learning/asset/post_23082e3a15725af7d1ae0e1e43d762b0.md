## 4.5.4 A Brief Tour of QSA Theory

While QSA theory is far simpler than stability of its stochastic ancestor, the technicalities are best left to the end of the chapter – see Section 4.9 for details. Contained here is an overview and some guidelines for algorithm design.

Our interest is not in convergence of QSA, but convergence rates, and regarding the rate of convergence is $1/t^\varrho$ if

$\limsup_{t\to\infty} t^\varrho ||\tilde{\Theta}_t|| = \begin{cases} \infty & \varrho > \varrho_0 \\ 0 & \varrho < \varrho_0 \end{cases} \quad (4.68)$

where $\Theta_t \overset{\approx}{=} \Theta_t - \theta^*$ is the estimation error. By careful design, we can achieve $\varrho_0 = 1$, which is optimal in most cases. Exercise 4.18 shows that convergence can be much faster.

## QSA-ODE Solidarity

The apparent noise plays a crucial role in the analysis:

$\tilde{\Xi}_t = f(\Theta_t, \Xi_t) - \tilde{f}(\Theta_t),$

so that

$\tilde{\mathfrak{f}}_t = a_t[\tilde{f}(\Theta_t) + \tilde{\Xi}_t].$

While this is similar to the ODE (4.42), an apparent discrepancy is that the gain $a$ is absent. In the continuous theory, it is simplest to obtain a gain for the purposes of comparison:

$\tilde{\mathfrak{f}}_t = a_t\tilde{f}(\Theta_t), \quad t \geq t_0, \quad \Theta_t = \Theta_{t_0},$

where $t_0$ depends on stability of the associated ODE (4.42) with constant gain. We are left with two steps:

- (1) Understand the relationship between the solution to the original ODE (4.42), and the solution to (4.71).
- (2) Obtain bounds on error between solutions to the QSA ODE (4.70) and the ODE (4.71) when noise is removed. In this step, we consider scaled error:

$Z_t = \frac{1}{a_t}(\Theta_t - \Theta_{t_0}), \quad t \geq t_0.$

It is shown that this is a bounded function of time under mild assumptions. Most of 4.9 is devoted to step 2. Step 1 is addressed early through the following change of variables:

$\tau = \int_{0}^{t} a_r dr, \quad t \geq 0.$

**Lemma 4.14** $\{\Theta_\tau : \tau \geq \tau_0\}$ denote the solution to (4.42) initialized at time $\tau = \tau_0$, with $\Theta_{\tau_0} = \Theta_{t_0}$. The solution to (4.71) is then given by

$\bar{\Theta}_t = \Theta_\tau, \quad t \geq t_0.$

## Gain Selection

Consider the standard choice of gain $a_t = g/(1+t)^\rho$, in which $g > 0$ and $0 < \rho \leq 1$ are fixed. The scaling reveals a significant difference between $\rho < 1$ and $\rho = 1$:

$\tau = \begin{cases} g \log(1+t) & \rho = 1 \\ g \frac{1}{1-\rho}(1+t)^{1-\rho} & 0 < \rho < 1 \end{cases}$

ODE Methods for Algorithm Design It is here that we arrive at a conflict of choice of gain. To make this clear, suppose that the ODE (4.42) satisfies the following form of exponential asymptotic stability: there exists $\rho_0 > 0, B_0 < \infty$ for any solution to (4.42), and any $t \geq 0$

$||\Theta_t - \Theta^0|| \leq B_0 ||\Theta_t - \Theta^0|| \exp(-\rho_0 t) \quad (4.76)$

From the identity $||\Theta_t - \Theta^0|| = ||\vartheta_t - \vartheta^0||$, we come to very different conclusions, depending on $\rho$:

$\rho < 1$: $\{\Theta_t\}$ converges to $\theta^0$ very quickly. However, in this case boundedness of $\{Z_t\}$ implies a suboptimal rate

$|\Theta_t| \leq B_z \frac{1}{(1+t)^\rho} \quad (4.77)$

$\rho = 1$: The bound (4.77) is ideal, but the rate of convergence of $\{\Theta_t\}$ may not be: an application of (4.75) gives

$||\Theta_t - \Theta^0|| \leq B_0 ||\Theta_0 - \Theta^0|| \frac{1}{(1+t)^\rho}$

To obtain the optimal $1/T$ convergence rate for QSA requires $g \geq 1$. This high gain may lead to other problems, such as large transients. It is here that the averaging technique of Polyak, Juditsky, and Rupp (PJR) averaging comes to the rescue. We use $\rho < 1$ to exploit the fast convergence of $\{\Theta_t\}$ to $\theta^0$, and then reduce volatility by simply averaging some fraction of the iterates:

$\Theta_T^M \coloneqq \frac{1}{T-T_0} \int_{T_0}^{T} \Theta_t dt. \quad (4.78)$

For example, $T_0 = T-T/5$ shows that we average the final 20%$. This approach will achieve the optimal $1/T$ convergence rate under very mild assumptions. Section 4.5.5 provides an introduction to the theory for a special case.

## Basic Assumptions and Conclusions

It is simplest to adopt a ‘Markovian’ setting where the probing signal is itself the state for a dynamical system:

$\frac{d}{dt} \xi = H(\xi) \quad (4.79)$

where $\Omega: \Omega \to \Omega$ is continuous, with $\Omega$ a bounded subset of Euclidean space. A canonical is the $K$-dimensional torus: $\Omega = [x \in \mathbb{C}^K : |x_i| = 1, 1 \leq i \leq K]$, and $\xi$ defined to allow of excitation with a mixture of mixtures:

$\xi_t = [\exp(\omega_1 t), \dots, \exp(\omega_k t)] \quad (4.80)$

with frequencies $\omega_1 < \omega_2 < \dots < \omega_k$. This is to keep notation consistent.
