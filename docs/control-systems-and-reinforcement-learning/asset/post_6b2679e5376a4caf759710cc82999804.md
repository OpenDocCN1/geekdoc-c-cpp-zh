### 4.7.1 Mountain Car

We return to the example introduced in Section 2.7.2, and the simple policy (2.59). This cannot be optimal unless it is clear the state remains near $z^{min}$ far longer than necessary from initial suggestions. A more sensible policy will avoid this “wandering.” Here is one suggestion, based on a threshold $\theta$ in the interval $[z^{min}, z^{goal}]$.

With $z(k) = x_1(k)$ and $v(k) = x_2(k)$ denoting position and velocity at step $k$, consider

```
$u(k) = \phi^{\theta}(x(k)) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } z(k) \leq 0 \\ -\text{sign}(v(k)) & \text{else} \end{cases}$ own```

The policy “panics” and accelerates the car towards the goal whenever $z(k)$ is at or below the threshold.

The range of acceptable $\theta$ can be estimated by examining the graph of potential energy in Figure 2.11, in the case of static $u(k) \equiv 1$. Its minimum is at $z \approx -0.48$. Denoting $v(1) = F_2(x,u)$, with definition of $\theta$:

```
$\frac{0}{dt}(z^{\theta}) = \max \sin(z^{\theta}) - \kappa - v(1), \quad u=1, x=(z^{\theta},0)^T$.
```

That is, $v(0) = 0$ implies that the mountain car has stalled: $x(1) = x(0)$. We cannot therefore allow a policy with $\phi(x) = 1$, which means that the policy $\phi^{\theta}$ is not acceptable if $\theta > 0$. We don't observe infinite cost in experiments with that policy because we artificially bound the value function, as explained.

Figure 4.1 shows trajectories of position as a function of time from three initial conditions, with $v(0) = 0$, and with two instances of this policy: $\theta = -0.8$, and $\theta = -0.2$. The former is a better choice from initial position $z(0) = -0.6$. We see that the time to reach the goal is nearly twice as long when using $\theta = -0.2$ compared to $\theta = -0.8$.

Let's see how to adapt qSGD #1 to find the optimal of $\theta$. A discrete-time approximation of the recurrence (4.96)

```
$\Theta_{n+1} = \Theta_n - \alpha_n \varepsilon \mathcal{G}_{n+1} \Gamma(\Psi_{n+1}), \Psi_{n+1} = \Theta_n + \varepsilon \mathcal{E}_{n+1}.$
```

The question is, how to define $\Gamma$?

## 4.7 Quasi Policy Gradient Algorithms

The total cost in this example coincides with the time to reach the goal. For initial condition $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$, we estimate the minimum of the corresponding total cost $J_{\theta}(x_0)$ over $\theta$. A natural approach is episodic: at stage $n$ of the algorithm, we initialize the car at state $x_n$, and run the policy $\phi^{\theta_n} = \Theta_n + \varepsilon\Theta_{n+1}$. On reaching the goal state, we have a measure of $J_{\theta}(x_0)$ for this value of $\theta$.

We make two modifications to this objective function. First, because we don't know if the policy is stabilizing for all $\theta$, we maximize over our choosing. Second, we are interested in more than one initial condition. Let $v$ denote a pdf on $X$, and define our objective function

```
$$\Gamma(\theta) = \sum \nu(x) \min\{J^{max}, J_{\theta}(x)\}$$
```

where the sum is over the support of $v$. If $v$ has $K$ points of support, then $K$ experiments are required to obtain the benefit $\Gamma(\Theta_{n+1})$ at stage $n+1$ of the algorithm.
The value $J^{max} = 5000$ is used in all of the numerical experiments that follow.

A second option is to use a constant probing signal to generate a quasirandom sequence of initial conditions $\{x_n^s : n \geq 0\}$ that induce an ergodic average: For any subset $S \subset X$,

```
$$\nu(S) = \lim_{N\to\infty} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}\{x_n^s \in S\}$$
```

In this case, we don't require that $v$ has finite support, so our objective requires the general notation,

```
$$\Gamma(\theta) = \int \min\{J^{max}, J_{\theta}(x)\} \nu(dx). \quad (4.103)$$
```

In this case, the recursion (4.102) is modified as follows:

```
$$\Gamma_{n+1} = \Theta_n - \alpha_{n+1}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Gamma_n + \varepsilon\min\{J^{max}, J_{\theta}(x_{n+1})\}$$
```

The experiments that follow are based on (4.104) option 2, though the first option (using $K$ experiments per iteration) is most feasible in terms of accelerating convergence. The sequence $\{x_n^s\}_{s=1}^K$ was chosen to cover the state space uniformly. This was achieved by introducing a second probing signal $(\xi_n, \xi_n^v)$

```
$x_n^s = \text{frac}(nr_s), \quad \xi_n^v = \text{frac}(nr_v)$
```

where "frac" denotes the fractional part of a real number, $r_s, r_v$ are irrational, and $r_v$ were chosen in all experiments. Then we define

```
$z_0^s = \text{frac}(2\pi z_0^s - 1), \quad z_0^s = z^{goal} + z^{max}^s \quad (4.105)$
```

Figure 4.12 shows one using the constant-step $\alpha_n = 0.05, \xi_n = \sin(n)$. The large step-size we chose simply to explore the nonlinear dynamics that emerge from this algorithm. It would seem that the algorithm has failed, since the estimates oscillate between -0.1 and -0.3 in time, while the actual optimal is $\theta^* \approx -0.8$. The dashed line shows the average of $\{\Theta_n\}$ over the final $20\%$ of estimates. This average is near optimal, since the objective function is nearly flat for $\theta$ near the optimizer.
