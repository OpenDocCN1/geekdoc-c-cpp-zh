# 2.2 What to Do about It?

The vast literature on control solutions is built upon a measure of input–output behavior that is used to design the policy $\phi$. Modeling and control design are each art form, with possible solutions from vast statistics and control tools.

When we say, a sequence of mappings from inputs to outputs:

```
$y(k) = G_{\phi}(u(0), u(1), \dots, u(k)), \quad k \ge 0. \quad (2.2)$
```

The functions of $G_{\phi}$ may also depend on exogenous variables (outside our control), such as the weather and traffic conditions. And here we come to one of the most vital principles of control design: the model must capture essential properties of the system to be controlled, and simultaneously simple enough to be useful.

For example, aerospace engineers will create absurd models for the design of flight controls and from this a policy $\phi$ designed to work for the model. Of course, they do not. The next step is to create entirely new model for validation and simulate a range of scenarios to answer a range of questions: What happens when the plane is full, empty, or flying through a thunderstorm? How does the control system perform when an engine detaches from a wing? If one of these tests fails, then the control engineer gains better to improve the model, the policy, or improve the airplane. That’s right, we may require additional sensors to measure pitch angles, or more powerful motors to control ailerons, flaps, or elevators.

I am writing without any knowledge of aerospace engineering. I am describing general principles for anyone interested in control design:

+   (1) Create a model for control.
(2) Design the policy $\phi$ based on the model.
(3) Simulate based on a high-fidelity model, and then revisit steps 1 and 2.
