Obtain $M \gg 1$ more batches of data $\{z^m : 1 \le m \le M\}$, and compute $f_N^m(\theta_N)$ for each $m$,

You need to increase $N$, if there is large variability in $\{f_N^m(\theta_N) : 1 \le m \le M\}$.

Is the Output of Your Algorithm Predictive of What Really Matters? This will take some work, but it is truly essential. With $M \gg 1$ batches of data, estimate the performance you obtain with the output of your algorithm. This means that for $m=1,\dots,M$, you must:

+   (i) Obtain an estimate $\theta^*$ using your algorithm.
(ii) Obtain $\phi^{**} = \arg\min_{\phi} Q^{\phi}(x, u)$.
(iii) Run experiments to estimate the performance. For the total cost problems considered here, choose a $\phi v$ with finite support. For each initial condition $x^i$ satisfying $\nu(x^i) > 0$, run a simulation to estimate $J(x^i)$ under policy $\phi^{**}$, and then obtain $\Gamma_m = \sum \nu(x^i)f_m^i$. Look at the sample mean and variance of $\{\Gamma_m : 1 \le m \le M\}$. High variance means you need a longer run.

Or, you might decide to look more closely at those policies for which $\Gamma_m$ is smallest—maybe you get lucky! To know sure, you need a deeper investigation of performance, data independent of what was used for training.
