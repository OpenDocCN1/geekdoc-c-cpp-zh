Alternatively, we can adapt the QSA model (4.44) to this example, with

$f(0, \xi_t) \stackrel{	ext{dist}}{=} y(\xi_t) - 	heta.$

The mean vector is given by

$f(0) = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T f(0, \xi_t)dt = \int_0^1 y(\xi_t)dt - 	heta,$

so that $	heta^* = \int_0^1 y(\xi_t)dt$ is the unique root of $f$. The QSA ODE (4.44) gives

$\frac{d}{dt}\Theta_t = a_t|y(\xi_t) - \Theta_t.$

The Monte Carlo approach (4.51) can be transformed into resembling (4.53). Derivatives of each of (4.51), can be estimated using the product rule of differentiation, and the fundamental theorem of calculus,

$\frac{d}{dt}\Theta_t =-\frac{1}{t} \int_0^t y(\xi_r)dr + \frac{1}{t}y(\xi_t) = \frac{1}{t}[y(\xi_t) - \Theta_t].$

This is precisely (4.53) with $a_t = 1/t$ (not a great choice for an ODE design, since it is bounded as $t \downarrow$).

The numerical results that follow are based on $y(\theta) = e^{at}\sin(\theta)$, whose mean is $	heta^* \approx -0.5$. The differential equation (4.53) was approximated using a standard Euler scheme with sampling interval $10^{-3}$. Several variations were simulated, differentiated by the gain $a_t = g/(1+t)$. Figure 4.3 shows typical sample paths of the resulting estimates for a range of gains, with initialization $\Theta_0 = 10$. In each case, the estimates converge to the true mean $	heta^* \approx -0.5$, but convergence is very slow for $g > 0$ significantly less than one.

Independent trials were conducted to obtain variance estimates. In $10^4$ independent runs, the initial condition was drawn randomly, and the estimate was calculated at time $T=100$. Figure 4.4 shows histograms of estimates for standard Monte Carlo (4.50) and QSA using gains $g=1$ and $2$. An alert reader might wonder: Why is the variance reduced by four orders of magnitude when the gain is increased from 1 to 2?
