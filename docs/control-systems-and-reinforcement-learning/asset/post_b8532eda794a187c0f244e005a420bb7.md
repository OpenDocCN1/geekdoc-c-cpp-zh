## 9.11.4 Convex Q-Learning

Extensions of the convex Q algorithms of Section 5.5 are described in [246, 247]. The main challenge in algorithm design is that the constraints involve a conditional expectation. For example, (5.63) would be modified as follows:

```
$$\max_{\theta} \langle\mu, H^\theta\rangle \ns.t. H^\theta(x,u) \le c(x,u) + E[H^\theta(\Phi(k+1)) | \Phi(k) = (x,u)], \quad x \in X, u \in U(x).$$
(9.106)
```

The conditional expectation can be approximated to obtain an algorithm, as discussed in Section 9.2. The use of **experience replay** is another approach based on empirical distributions (see [338, ch. 16] and [211]). Theory for convex Q-learning remains immature, best left to a sequel or second edition.

The recent paper [218] is based on a variant of the convex program (9.106) in the tabular setting, and [224] contains an algorithm very similar to convex Q-learning (see [224, (6)]). The more recent RL survey [269] has a version of convex Q-learning, and explains the importance of regularization. Also related to (9.106) is the *logistic Q-learning* algorithm of [28], which may represent an opening for more practical algorithms, as well as more elegant theory.

The geometry developed in this chapter requires a linearly parameterized function class. Since this is not an option, we might allow RKHS for this purpose. a $d$-dimensional basis $\psi: Z \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$ is chosen, and any approximation is expressed $H^\theta = \theta\psi$ with $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$.

The interpretation of $Q$ in (10.1), the approximation $H^\theta$ is known as the projection of $Q$ onto the subspace $\{H^\theta : \theta \in \mathbb{R}\}$, and can be expressed

$$H^\theta(\Phi(k)) = E[Q(\Phi(k))]$$

with $Y = \psi(\Phi(k))$ (recall 9.1 for definitions). For any two functions $g,h: Z \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, the inner product is defined by

$$\langle g,h \rangle \triangleq E[g(\Phi(k))h(k)] = \sum_{z \in Z} g(z)h(z)$$

so that

$$\|H^\theta - Q\|_\omega = \langle H^\theta - Q \rangle_\omega.$$

Recall that we write $g \in L_2(\omega)$ whenever $\|g\| < \infty$. This comes for free since we assume in this chapter that $X$ and $U$ are finite.

**Proposition 10.1** Suppose that $\{\psi_i\}$ are linearly independent in $L_2(\omega)$; $\|\theta\psi\| = 0$ implies that $\theta=0$. Then, for any function $G \in L_2(\omega)$, the projection exists, is unique, and is given by $G = \theta^*\psi^*$ with $\theta^* = R(0)^{-1}\psi^*$, where $\psi^{*G \in \mathbb{R}$ and the $d \times d$ matrix defined by

$$\psi_i^G = \langle \psi_i, G \rangle_{\omega}, \quad R_{i,j}^{G} = \langle \psi_i, \psi_j \rangle_{\omega}, \quad 1 \leq i, j \leq d. \quad (10.2)$$

Some interest comes when $G$ is the Q-function. For the discounted-cost criterion, the vector $\psi^Q \in \mathbb{R}^d$ can be expressed

where the second equation follows from the definition (9.6b) together with the smoothing property of conditional expectation. Estimation of the right-hand side using Monte Carlo methods is challenging because it involves the state-input trajectory over the infinite future. ## 10.1 Linear Operators and Adjoints

Let $T: L_2(\varrho) \rightarrow L_2(\varrho)$ be a linear operator. For any $g, h \in L_2(\varrho)$ and scalars $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$.

$$T(\alpha g + \beta h) = \alpha T(g) + \beta T(h).$$

It is customary to write $Tg$ rather than $T(g)$ when there is no risk of confusion. For TD-learning with discounted-cost deconstruction, the linear operator of interest is defined by

$$T_g(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k E[g(\Phi(k)) | \Phi(0) = z], \quad \text{for any } g \in L_2(\varrho) \text{ and } z \in Z, \eqno(10.4)$$

so that $Q = T_c$. Basic theory of linear operators provides tools to efficiently estimate the vector $\psi^Q$ appearing in (10.2) with $G = Q$. The main idea is the adjoint of a linear operator $T$, denoted $T^{\dagger}$. This is defined by the simple identity,

$$\langle Tg, h \varrho\rangle = \langle g, T^{\dagger}h \varrho\rangle, \quad \text{for all } h \in L_2(\varrho). \eqno(10.5)$$

In this finite there is a simple formula, based on expressing $T$ as a matrix:

$$T(z) = \sum_{z' \in Z} T(z, z')g(z').$$

**Lemma 10.2** The adjoint of $T$ is equal to its transpose, followed by a similarity transformation:

$$T^{\dagger}(z, z') = \frac{1}{\varrho(z)}T(z', z)\varrho(z'), \quad z, z' \in Z.$$

Consequently, for any $h \in L_2(\varrho)$,

$$T^{\dagger}h(z') = \sum_{z \in Z} T^{\dagger}(z, z')h(z) = \sum_{z \in Z} \sum_{z' \in Z} T(z, z')g(z)h(z)\frac{\varrho(z)}{\varrho(z')}, \quad z' \in Z. \eqno(10.6)$$
