
## 10.1 The Stage, Projection, and Adjoints

### 10.1.2 Adjoints and Eligibility Vectors

The probabilistic representation of $T_\gamma$ provides a more useful representation of its adjoint.

**Proposition 10.3** The adjoint of $T$ admits the representation, for any $h \in L_2(\omega)$ and $z \in Z$.

$$
T_\gamma^H h(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k E[h(\Phi(-k)) \mid \Phi(0) = z],
$$

Where $\Phi$ is the stationary process on the two-sided time interval.

Proof Based on (10.4), we have

$$
\langle T_{\gamma}g, h\rangle_\omega = \sum_{z\in Z} \alpha(z)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k E[g(\Phi(k)) \mid \Phi(0) = z])h(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k E_\omega[h(\Phi(0))g(\Phi(k))].
$$

We have stationary $E_\omega[h(\Phi(k))g(\Phi(0))] = E_\omega[h(\Phi(-k))g(\Phi(0))]$, so that

$$
\langle T_{\gamma}g, h\rangle_\omega = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k E_\omega[h(\Phi(-k))g(\Phi(0))].
$$

The proof is completed on the smoothing property:

$$
E_\omega[h(\Phi(-k))g(\Phi(0))] = E_\omega[E[h(\Phi(-k))g(\Phi(0))]]\sum_{z\in Z} E_\omega[h(\Phi(-k)) \mid \Phi(0) = z]g(z)(\omega).
$$

**Theorem 9.11 (ii) Revisited** This result follows from Proposition 10.1 with only notational changes.

The vector $\psi_i^Q$ in (10.3) has formula: For any $n$,

$$
\psi_i^Q = E_\omega[\psi_i(\Phi(0))Q(\Phi(0))] = \langle c_i, T\psi_i\rangle_\omega = \langle c_i, T_i^H\psi_i\rangle_\omega, \quad 1 \le i \le d. \quad (10.7)
$$

Proposition 10.3 provides a more familiar formula:

$$
\tilde{\psi}_n^Q = E_\omega[c_n\zeta_n], \quad c_n = c(\Phi(n)) \quad \text{and} \quad \zeta_n = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k \psi(\Phi(n-k)), \quad (10.8)
$$

with $\Phi$ stationary and defined on the two-sided axis. The sequence $\{\zeta_n : n \in Z\}$ is the stationary version of the eligibility vectors for the TD(1) algorithm, the formula $\theta^* = R(0)^{-1}\tilde{\psi}_w^Q$ given in Proposition 10.1 converges to the equilibrium condition for TD(1):

$$
0 = f(\theta^*) = -R^\theta^\theta^* + E_\omega[c_n\zeta_n].
$$

### 10.1.3 Weighted Norms and Eligibility

The use of weighting was introduced in Section 6.7.5 as a means to reduce variance in algorithms based on Monte Carlo methods. Let $w : Z \to \mathbb{R}_+$ denote the weighting function used to define the norm

$$
||H||_{\alpha, \omega}^2 = \mathbb{E}[H(\Phi(n))^2 w(\Phi(n))]. \ (10.9)
$$

$H \in L_2(\alpha, w)$ if this is finite (a vacuous assumption when finite, as assumed in the theoretical development). The inner product is redefined consistently:

$$
\langle G, H\rangle_{\alpha, \omega} = \mathbb{E}[G(\Phi(n))H(\Phi(n))w(\Phi(n))], \quad G, H \in L_2(\alpha, w).
$$

The adaptation of (6.49) to the current setting is minimization of the weighted mean-square error:

$$
||H^0 - Q||_{\alpha, \omega}^2 = \mathbb{E}[(H^0(\Phi(n)))^2 w(\Phi(n))]. \ (10.10)
$$

The Galerkin approach to on-policy $(\lambda$-learning) is modified as follows:

$$
0 = \mathbb{E}[-H^0(\Phi(n)) + \zeta_n + \gamma H^0(\Phi(n+1))w(\Phi(n))\zeta_n], \ (10.11)
$$

with $\zeta_n = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \lambda^k \psi^k(\Phi(n-k))$, and with $\psi$ stationary on $\mathbb{Z}$.

The development of Section 10.1 carries over to the new vector space $L_2(\alpha, w)$, beginning with the following:

**Proposition 10.4** The following hold for any function $G \in L_2(\alpha, w)$, with the following notation:

$$
\psi_i^G = \langle\psi_i, G\rangle_{\alpha, w} \quad 1 \le i \le d,
$$
$$
R_{i,j}^G = \langle\psi_i, \psi_j\rangle_{\alpha, w} \quad 1 \le i, j \le d. \ (10.12)
$$

- (i) The projection $G$ exists, with $G = \theta^* \psi$, with $\theta^* \in \mathbb{R}^d$ solution to the linear equation $R\theta^* = \psi^G$.
- (ii) Suppose $\{\psi_i\}$ are linearly independent in $L_2(\alpha, w)$: $\|\theta \psi\|_{\alpha, w} = 0$ only when $\theta = 0$. Then $\theta^* = R^{-1}\psi^G$ is unique.

When applied to minimize the objective (10.10), we still have $Q = T_{\gamma}c$, and

$$
\psi_i^Q = \langle\psi_i, T_{\gamma}c\rangle_{\alpha, w} \quad 1 \le i \le d.
$$

However, the definition of adjoint depends on the choice of norm:

**Lemma 10.5** We have for all $g, h \in L_2(\alpha, w)$,

$$
\langle T_{\gamma}h, g\rangle_{\alpha, w} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k \mathbb{E}[w(\Phi(0))h(\Phi(-k))g(\Phi(k))] = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k \langle h, g\rangle_{\omega} .
$$

Consequently, the adjoint of $T_{\gamma}$ in $L_2(\alpha, w)$ admits the following representation:

$$
T_{\gamma}^T h(z) = \frac{1}{w(z)} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k \mathbb{E}[w(\Phi(-k))h(\Phi(-k)) \mid \Phi(0) = z], \quad z \in \mathbb{Z}.
$$

### 10.2 Advantage and Innovation

#### LSTD(1) with Weighting (On-Policy)

With initialization $\zeta_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d, \Sigma_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (positive definite), and time horizon $N$:

$$
\theta_N = \Sigma_N^{-1} \psi_N \quad (10.13a)
$$
$$
\Sigma_N = \frac{1}{N} \left(\Sigma_0 + \sum_{n=1}^N \psi(n)\psi(n)^T\right) \quad (10.13b)
$$
$$
\psi_N^Q = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N c_n \zeta_n, \quad (10.13c)
$$
$$
\zeta_n = \gamma \zeta_{n-1} + w \psi(n), \quad 1 \leq n \leq N. \ (10.13d)
$$

The Law of Large Numbers gives the following proposition:

**Proposition 10.6** Under the linear independence assumption of Proposition 10.4, the LSTD(1) algorithm (10.13) is consistent: $\lim_{N\to\infty}\theta_N = \theta^* = R(0)\psi^Q$ with probability one, where $R(0) = R^Q$ and $\psi^Q$ are defined in (10.12) with $G = Q$ equal to the fixed policy $Q$-function. Consequently, $\theta^*$ minimizes the $L_2$ objective (10.10).

Recall from Section 9.1.3 one motivation for the advantage function: In policy iteration, we don't need a precise estimate of $Q$, since we are only interested in controlling its minimum over $u$ for each $x$. Instead of $Q$, we seek to estimate the difference $Q-G$, where $G: X \to \mathbb{R}$ (the function $G$ does not depend upon $u$). Proposition 9.14 tells us that the best choice is $G = Q = h$.

By "best", we mean that $h$ is the minimal MSE estimate of $Q$ over all functions on $X$. In statistics, the error $V = Q-h$ is orthogonal to any function on $X$. It is common to call $V$ the innovations associated with the approximation of $Q$ by $h$. This interpretation of the advantage function is tremendously useful for application of both $Q$ and $V$, as it inspires better approximation architectures.

Throughout this section, we remain in the discounted-cost setting, so that $h$ and $Q$ are defined via (9.47). Extension of the theory and algorithms to average cost can be granted for the remainder of the chapter.

### 10.2.1 Projection of Advantage and Value

Let step back and reconsider estimation of $Q$ within a function class defined by a basis $\psi: X \times U \to \mathbb{R}^d$, expanding the function class in one of two:

$$
\mathcal{H} = \{\theta^T\psi + \xi^T\psi : 0, \xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\} \quad \text{or} \quad \mathcal{H}^X = \{\theta^T g : 0, g \in \mathbb{R}^d, g: X \to \mathbb{R}\}, \ (10.14)
$$

where $\psi$ and $\psi$ were introduced in (9.66), followed by the function class $\mathcal{H}$. We have $\mathcal{H} \subset \mathcal{H}^X$, where the latter contains every function that depends only on $x \in X$. In particular, $h \in \mathcal{H}^X$.

Here we also require the $d$-dimensional function classes

$$
\mathcal{H}=\{\tilde{\psi} : 0 \in \mathbb{R}^d\} \text{ and } \hat{\mathcal{H}}=\=\{\tilde{\varphi} : 0 \in \mathbb{R}^d\}.
$$

The projections of $\mathcal{H}$ and $\hat{\mathcal{H}}$, denoted respectively, $Q^-$ and $Q^{\sim}$, imply $Q^-$ and $Q^{\sim}$ are orthogonal.

- (i) $G \in \mathcal{H}$ is orthogonal to any function on $X$.
- (ii) For each $g \in \mathcal{H}$, $\|\theta \psi\|_{\alpha, w} = 0$ only when $\theta = 0$.
- (iii) $G \in \mathcal{H}$ is.
- (iv) $Q^X$ is the projection of $Q$ onto $\mathcal{H}^X$.

### 10.2.2 Weighted Norm

If we seek an approximation in a weighted norm, then a convenient choice for $G$ is the solution to the $L_2$ optimization problem,

$$
G^w=\min_G ||Q-G||^2_{\omega}=\min_G \mathbb{E}[\Q(\Phi(n))-G(X(n))\}^2\Phi(n)],
$$

where the minimum is over all $G: X \to \mathbb{R}$.

$$
\langle Q^w,G\rangle_{\omega}=0 \quad \text{for all } G:X \to \mathbb{R}.
$$

The proof of Proposition 10.9 is obtained on setting $G=G^+$, where $G'(x)=1\{x=x^i\}$ for each $i$, where $\{x^i\}$ is an enumeration of the state space X.

**Proposition 10.9** The optimizer is given by

$$
G^w(x)=\frac{1}{\kappa(x)}\sum_u \Phi(u|x)Q(x,u)w(x,u), \quad \kappa(x)=\sum_u \Phi(u|x)w(x,u), \quad x \in X.
$$

If $w$ does not depend upon $u$, this gives

$$
G^w(x)=\widetilde{Q}^{\sim{}}=\sum_u \Phi(u|x)Q(x,u), \quad x \in X.
$$

Hence, in this case, $G^w=\widetilde{Q}^{\sim{}}=h$, so that $V=Q-h$ exactly as in (9.65a).
