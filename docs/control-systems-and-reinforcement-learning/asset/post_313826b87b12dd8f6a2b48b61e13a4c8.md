
The primitive functions $h^\alpha$ are dense in $\mathcal{H}$. That is, for each $\varepsilon > 0$, there is $h^\alpha$ of this form (for some integer $n$, scalars $\{\alpha_i\}$, $\{z_i\} \subset \mathbb{Z}$, all depending on $\varepsilon$), satisfying $\|h - h^\alpha\|_{\mathcal{H}} \le \varepsilon$.

For any two $h^\alpha, h^\beta$ of this form, $n$ an inner product is introduced that is consistent with the norm:

$$\langle h^\alpha, h^\beta \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} = \sum_{i,j=1}^n \alpha_i \beta_j \langle z_i, z_j \rangle, \quad (5.15a)$$

$$\|h^\alpha\|_{\mathcal{H}} = \sqrt{(h^\alpha, h^\alpha)_{\mathcal{H}}}, \quad (5.15b)$$

The positivity assumption $(5.14)$ ensures that $(h^\alpha, h^\alpha)_{\mathcal{H}}$ is nonnegative. The definition of the inner product and norm can be extended to the larger collection of functions $\mathcal{H}$, and endowed with this inner product it is known as a *reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)*.

Details regarding $\mathcal{H}$ are not required in applications because the Representer Theorem tells us to restrict to the primitive functions in the function approximation problems of interest to us. To present this theorem requires one more ingredient.

## Regularized Loss Function

In addition to the loss function $\Gamma$, we require a regularizer $\Gamma$ of the loss $G(||h||_{\mathcal{H}})$, where $G: \mathbb{R}_+ \to \mathbb{R}_+$ is nondecreasing. Typical choices are $G(r) = \delta r^2$ or $G(r) = \delta r$, with $\delta > 0$. Our interest is solving the following optimization problem:

$$h^* = \arg\min_h [\Gamma(h) + G(||h||_{\mathcal{H}}) : h \in \mathcal{H}]. \quad (5.16)$$

The regularizer is introduced to manage the overfitting problem illustrated in Figure 5.2.

**Theorem 5.1 Representer Theorem** Suppose that $\{z_i : 1 \le i \le N\}$ are given, along with a loss function of the form (5.2). Then any minimizer of the optimization problem (5.16) can be expressed, for some $\alpha^* \in \mathbb{R}^N$,

$$h^*(z) = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i k(z, z_i). \quad (5.17)$$

We turn to the two examples.

## Curve Fitting

Consider the quadratic loss (5.3). If the regularizer is quadratic, $G(r) = \delta r^2$, then the Representer Theorem provides an explicit solution to (5.16). We are left to obtain the optimal parameter:

Let $\mathcal{K}$ denote the $n \times n$ matrix with entries $K_{i,j} = k(z_i, z_j)$. We then have $||h^\alpha||_{\mathcal{H}}^2 = \alpha^T \mathcal{K} \alpha$, and $h^\alpha(z_i) = \sum_j \alpha_j k(z_i, z_j) = [\mathcal{K}\alpha]_i$. It may take you time to compute $\theta_N$, you are far from done. Some experiments you can perform to obtain more confidence that you have a useful solution:

## Is Your Parameterization Redundant?

Consider $Q^0 = \theta^\top\psi$, an approximate Q-function. With estimates of the best value of $\theta$, obtain the sample correlation matrix:

$$\hat{R} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \psi(z_i)\psi(z_i)^\top.$$

Look at the eigenvalues of this positive semidefinite matrix – if there is a nontrivial null space, then there may be a problem with your basis or your choice of data. If $\hat{R}^v = 0$ for some nonzero vector $v$, then $v^\top \hat{R} = 0$, meaning that $Q^0$, with $\theta = v$, is identically zero over the observed samples. And that your basis is redundant, in the sense that some $\psi_k$ is a linear combination of others: If $v_k \neq 0$, then

$$\psi_k(z_i) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{j \neq k} v_j(z_i), \quad i \le N.$$

## Value Function Approximations

There are two potential explanations: (1) your basis is truly dependent in an algebraic sense: $v^\top\psi(z) = 0$ for every $z \in Z$ or (2) *insufficient exploration*: The samples $z$ evolve in a small subset of $Z$.

## Is Your Parameter Predictive Using Fresh Data?

Obtain $M \gg 1$ batches of data $\{z^m : 1 \le m \le M\}$, and compute $f_N^m(\theta)$ for each $m$,

$$f_N^m(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N D_k(h^0(z_k), h^0(z_{k+1})), \quad m \le M.$$

You need to increase $N$, if there is large variability in $\{f_N^m(\theta) : 1 \le m \le M\}$.

Is the Output of Your Algorithm Predictive of What Matters? This will take some work, but it is truly essential. With $M \gg 1$ batches of data, estimate the performance you obtain with the output of your algorithm. This means that for $m=1,\dots,M$, you must:

- (i) Obtain an estimate $\theta^*$ using your algorithm.
- (ii) Obtain $\phi^{**} = \arg\min_{\phi} Q^{\phi}(x, u)$.
- (iii) Run experiments to estimate the performance. For the total cost problems considered here, choose a $\phi$ with finite support. For each initial condition $x^i$ satisfying $\nu(x^i) > 0$, run a simulation to estimate $J(x^i)$ under policy $\phi^{**}$, and then obtain $\Gamma_m = \sum \nu(x^i)f_m^i$. High variance means you need a longer run. Or, you might decide to look more closely at those policies for which $\Gamma_m$ is smallest—maybe you get lucky! To know sure, you need a deeper investigation of performance, data independent of what was used for training.

## A Few Words on Ergodic Averages

Assumption (A\xi) that follows from Assumption (QSA) appearing in Section 4.9, and that both concern averages. In the discrete-time setting of this chapter, we denote

$$g_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N g(\Phi(k))$$

for $g: Z \to R$ continuous, and $N \ge 1$. The main assumption is the existence of a limit, known as the ergodic mean (or ergodic average) and expectation:

$$E_{\xi}[|g(\Phi)|] = \lim_{N\to\infty} g_N. \quad (5.22)$$

The expression $E_{\xi}[|g(\Phi)|]$ may be regarded as notation, but in many cases it is represented as an integral. The *probability measure* $\mu$ has a density $\rho$, so that

$$E_{\xi}[|g(\Phi)|] = \int_{Z} g(z)\rho(z)dz.$$

Here is a simple example to illustrate the origin of a density:

**Lemma 5.2** *Consider the scalar signal $\xi(k) = \sin(2\pi k/T), k \ge 0$. Provided $T$ is an irrational number, for any continuous function $g: R \to R$, we have*

$$\lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} g(\xi(k)) = \int_{0}^{1} g(\sin(2\pi r))dr = \int_{-1}^{1} g(t)dt, \quad (5.23)$$

where $\rho(t) = [\pi\sqrt{1-|t|^2}]^{-1}$ is known as the arcsine density.

*Proof* Consider the first signal $\xi^0(k) = [k/T]_1$, where $[r]_1$ denotes the fractional part of a scalar $r \in R_+$. This signal samples points uniformly in the interval $[0,1]$, giving for continuous functions $h: R \to R$,

$$\lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} h(\xi^0(k)) = \int_{0}^{1} h(r)dr.$$
