The final term $d$ represents the error in the Taylor series approximation. After computing partial derivatives, we obtain

```
$$\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{x}_1 = \tilde{x}_2,$$
$\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{x}_2 = \alpha\tilde{x}_1 + \beta\tilde{u} + d \quad \text{with} \quad \alpha = 2\frac{\kappa (u)^2}{m(x_1)^3}, \quad \beta = -2\frac{\kappa u^2}{m(x_1)^2}$$
```

This can be represented as a linear state model with disturbance:

```
$$\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \alpha & 0 \end{bmatrix}\tilde{x} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \beta \end{bmatrix}\tilde{u} + d, \quad \tilde{y} = \tilde{x}_1. \quad (2.62)$$
```

There is a hidden approximation in (2.62), since $d$ is in a nonlinear function of $(x, u)$. In control design, this approximation is taken one step further by setting $d = 0$, to obtain the linear model (2.20). The approximate model is not very useful for simulations, but often leads to effective control solutions.

A history from the perspective of control education can be found in [385], which provides the dynamic equations with state $x = (z, \dot{z}, \theta, \dot{\theta})$, where $z$ is the horizontal position of the cart, and the angle $\theta$ is as shown in Figure 2.13.

The control design goal is: Keep $\theta=0$ while the cart is moving at some desired speed, or some desired fixed position. The aforementioned references describe successful strategies to swing the pole up to a desired position without excessive effort. A normalized model used in [385] is given by

Figure 2.13 CartPole.

```
$$\frac{d}{dt}x_1 = x_2, \quad \frac{d}{dt}x_2 = u,$$$$
$\frac{d}{dt}x_3 = x_4, \quad \frac{d}{dt}x_4 = \sin(x_3) - u\cos(x_3). \quad (2.63)$$
```

The state equations are almost linearized near the equilibrium $x^e = 0$ and $x^e = (z^e, 0, 0, 0)^T$ for any $z^e$: using the first-order Taylor series approximations $\sin(x_3) \approx 0$ and $\cos(x_3) \approx 1$, we obtain as in the derivation of (2.62)

```
$$\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{x}_1 = \tilde{x}_2, \quad \frac{d}{dt}\tilde{x}_2 = u,$$$$
$\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{x}_3 = \tilde{x}_4, \quad \frac{d}{dt}\tilde{x}_4 = \tilde{x}_3 - u + d. \quad (2.64)$$
```

## 2.7.5 Pendubot and Acrobat

Figure 2.14 shows a photograph of the Pendubot as it appeared in the robotics laboratory at the University of Illinois in the 1990s [29, 330] and a sketch indicating its parts. It is similar to Sutton’s CartPole [341], which is another example currently popular on openai.com. The control objective is similar to CartPole: starting from any initial condition, swinging the Pendubot up to a desired equilibrium without excessive energy. The value of this example is explained in the introduction of [330], where they compare to CartPole, and a variation of [337]. The balancing problem for the Pendubot may be solved by linearizing the equations of motion around an operating point and designing a linear feedback controller, very similar to the classical cartpole problem... One interesting distinction of the Pendubot over the classical cartpole system is the balancing positions. This feature of the Pendubot is pedagogically useful in several ways, to show how Taylor series linearization is operating point dependent and for teaching gain scheduling. Students can also understand physically how the linearized system becomes uncontrollable at $q_1=0$. **Figure 2.15** Description of the Pendubot: $\ell_1$ is the length of the first link, and $\ell_{c1},\ell_{c2}$ are the distances to center of mass of the respective links. The variables $q_1,q_2$ are joint angles of the respective links, and the input is the torque applied to the lower joint.

The Pendubot consists of two rigid links: Link 1 is directly coupled to the shaft of a DC motor mounted to the end of a table. Link 1 also includes the bearing housing for the second joint. Two optical encoders provide position measurements: One is attached to the elbow joint, and the other to the motor. That no motor is directly connected to link 2 - this makes virtual control of the system, as shown in the photograph, extremely difficult!

The system dynamics can be derived using the so-called Euler-Lagrange equations found in robotics textbooks [332]:

```
$d_{11}\dot{q}_1 + d_{12}\dot{q}_2 + h_1 + \phi_1 = \tau,$
$d_{21}\dot{q}_1 + d_{22}\dot{q}_2 + h_2 + \phi_2 = 0$
```

The variables can be defined from Figure 2.15. Consequently, this model may be written in state form, $\dot{x} = f(x,u)$, where $x = (q_1,q_2,\dot{q}_1,\dot{q}_2)^T$, and $f$ is defined from the preceding equations.

This model admits variety equilibrium: For example, when $x^e = (-\pi/2,0,0,0)$ is an equilibrium, shown on the right-hand side of Figure 2.14. Three other possibilities are shown in Figure 2.14b, with $\tau^e \neq 0$ and $x^e \neq 0$.

A equilibrium is obtained in the upright position, with $\tau^e = 0$ and $x^e = (+\pi/2,0,0,0)$. It is clear from the drawing on the left-hand side of Figure 2.14 that the upright equilibrium is strongly unstable in the sense that when $\tau = 0$, it is unlikely that the physical system will rest at all. Nevertheless, the velocity vector vanishes, $f(x^e,0) = 0$, so by definition the upright position is an equilibrium when $\tau = 0$.
