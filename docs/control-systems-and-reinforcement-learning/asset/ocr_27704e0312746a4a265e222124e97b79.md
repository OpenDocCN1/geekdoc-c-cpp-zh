The theory of exploration is mature only within a very special setting: multi-armed bandits. The term "bandit" refers to slot machines: you put money in the machine, pull an arm, and hope that money pops out. A rational application is in the advertisement industry, in which an "arm" is an advertisement (which costs money), and the advertiser hopes money will pop out as ads encourage sale. There is a great history of heuristics and science to create successful algorithms to maximize profit, based only on noisy observations of the performance of candidate algorithms (l216 is a great reference on the theory of bandits, and a short survey contained in Section 7.8). It is here that the "exploration/exploitation" trade-off is most clearly seen: you have to accept some loss of revenue through exploration in order to learn the best strategy, and then "exploit" as you gain confidence in your estimates.

The situation is much more complex in control applications: imagine that for each state x(k), there is a multi-armed bandit. "Pulling arm a" at time k choosing u(k)=a \in U. Concepts from bandit theory have led to heuristics to balance the exploration/exploitation trade-offs arising in RL. It is an exciting direction for future research [171, 307].

# 2.6 How Can We Ignore Noise?

It is hard to explain this to a student without a background in probability theory. If you have some exposure to stochastic processes, then you might want to skip Section 7.2: you will learn how to construct a deterministic "fluid model" or "mean-field model" based on a more detailed and complex stochastic state space model, and just justification for control design based on the simpler model.

The pragmatic answer to this question is that we have a reliable model of disturbances, so we leave them unmodeled but not ignored. There is, we attempt to create a control architecture that is not sensitive to disturbances. There is an elegant theory of robust control for this purpose, though even "robustness" is only with respect to disturbances within some uncertainty. The most successful outcomes of this literature heavily on domain contexts. For example, it is assumed that disturbances (the d shown in Figure 2.1) are largely limited to lower frequencies, and noise measurement (the w on the right-hand side of this figure) is limited to higher frequencies.

Justification for nonlinear control systems is based on Lyapunov techniques. We establish control through a Lyapunov function V as outlined in Section 2.4.3, and argue that V will continue to have "negative drift" in the form (2.31) even with error in the model F, or in the presence of the disturbance d.

Finally, the naive "disturbance-free" model obtained through physics, or through techniques surveyed in Section 7.2, provides a great deal of insight for the structure of control solutions. We might use this insight to build architectures for reinforcement learning.
