$$
\|\Theta_{\mathcal{N}_{n+1}}^{(n+1)} - \theta^*\| \leq \|\Theta_{\mathcal{N}_{n+1}}^{(n)}\| + \|\Theta_{\mathcal{N}_{n+1}}^{(n)} - \theta^*\| \leq \varepsilon.

By definition, $\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{n+1}} = \Theta_{\mathcal{N}_{n+1}}$ is the initialization for the next interval, which implies that

$$
\|\theta^{(k)} - \theta^*\| \leq \delta \quad \text{for all } k \geq n(\delta), \tau \geq \mathcal{N}_k.
$$

From (8.19), we then obtain

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty} |\theta_n - \theta^*\| = \lim_{t\to\infty} |\Theta_t - \theta^*\| \leq \delta.
$$

This establishes convergence, since $\delta > 0$ is arbitrary.

# Projection and Restart

The introduction of $n_0 \geq 0$ in the step-size sequence $\alpha_n = q/(n+n_0)^p$ is to avoid excessive gain when $n$ is small. Anyone who has experienced SA has seen parameter estimates explode in the first few iterations. Without understanding its asymptotic covariance, it is likely that a user will reduce the value of $g_n$, not realizing this might result in asymptotic covariance. Increasing $n_0$ can take the algorithm and has no impact on the asymptotic covariance.

Two alternatives are available, each based on a closed region $R \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ satisfying $\theta^* \in R$ (in practice, the choice of this set is much like the choice of $n_0$) through trial and error.

# Projection

The definition requires notion of distance: $dist(w,v) \geq 0$ for any $v \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with $dist(v,v)=0$ for any $v$. It is typical to use the standard Euclidean norm or the max norm: $dist(w,v) = \|w-v\|_{\infty} = \max |w_i - v_i|$. For any vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the vector $\theta' = \Pi_R v$ satisfies

$$
\theta' = \arg\min dist(w,v) : w \in R.
$$

Projected the basic algorithm (8.2) is defined by:

$$
\theta_{n+1} = \Pi_R(\theta_n + \alpha_n f(\theta_n)). \quad (8.20)
$$

There are two difficulties with this approach: one is the potential complexity in updating the parameter estimate, and the other is complexity in ODE analysis. How do we know that the parameter sequence doesn't become trapped on the boundary of $R$?

# Restart

Whenever $\theta_{n+1} \notin R$, we reset its value to a vector within the interior of $R$. For example, with $R = \{\theta : \|\theta\| \leq r\}$, you might choose the parameter to be $\theta'_{n+1} \in R$ by scaling:

$$
\theta'_{n+1} = \frac{r}{\|\theta_{n+1}\|}\theta_{n+1}.
$$

This procedure is far simpler than projection, and analysis is simpler.

# 8.2 Themes and Roadmaps

### 8.2.3 Choice of Step-Size

The next question is how to choose the step-size appearing in (8.2). A constant step-size is often preferred in applications, although choice of the value remains an art. Also, with a constant step-size we cannot expect convergence unless the covariance matrix defined in (8.2) is identically zero. This degenerate case is not found in the applications of interest, so we assume vanishing step-size:

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty} \alpha_n = 0.
$$

Two further assumptions are imposed; the discrete-time version of Assumption 1 of Section 4.5:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \alpha_n = \infty.
$$

This is imposed so that it is possible to reach $\theta^*$ from initial $\theta_0$. The second assumption is more strongly rooted in probability theory:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \alpha_n^2 < \infty. \quad (8.21)
$$

In particular, it is used to establish (8.18), which is required for convergence of parameter estimates in Theorem 8.1. The proposition follows makes clear why we require (8.21). The uniform bound on the second moment of $\{\Delta_k\}$ is in Proposition 8.2 (see (8.6)).

**Proposition 8.2** Suppose that $\{\Delta_k\}$ is an uncorrelated sequence with bounded covariance $\sigma_{\Delta}^2 = \sup_k E[\|\Delta_k\|^2] < \infty$. Then the second moment of $\{M_K^{(n)} : K \ge 1\}$ appearing in (8.6) admits the uniform bound

$$
E[\|M_K^{(n)}\|^2] \le \sigma_{\Delta}^2 \sum_{j=n+1}^{K} \alpha_j^2.
$$

The right-hand side vanishes as $n\to\infty$, provided (8.21) holds.

Proof: If $\{\Delta_k\}$ is an uncorrelated sequence, then the covariance of $M_K^{(n)}$ satisfies

$$
E[M_K^{(n)T M_K^{(n)}] = \sum_{i=1}^{K} (\alpha_i^2 + E[\Delta_i \Delta_i^T]),
$$

and consequently

$$
E[\|M_K^{(n)}\|^2] = \text{trace} E[M_K^{(n)T M_K^{(n)}] \le \sigma_{\Delta}^2 \sum_{j=n+1}^{K} \alpha_j^2.
$$

### 8.2.4 Multiple Time Scales

There are many examples in which it is valuable to use different step-sizes for different parameter estimates. Consider the averaging technique (8.10). This is usually presented as a time-scale recursion where $N_0=0$,

$$
\theta_{n+1}^{pr} = \theta_n^{pr} + \alpha_n[\theta_n^m - \theta_n^{pr}], \quad n \ge 0
$$

and $\alpha_n = 1/n$. A similar recursion for arbitrary $N_0 < N$, the recursion begins at $n = N_0$ with $\theta_{N_0}^0 = 0$. This is equivalently expressed (8.10c)

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty} \beta_n = \infty. \quad (8.22)
$$

This means that the estimates $\{\theta_n^*\}$ defined in (8.10a) evolve much more rapidly than $\{\theta_n^m\}$. The general two-time scale algorithm is described as:

$$
\theta_{n+1} = \theta_n + \beta_n f_1(\theta_n, \omega_n), \quad (8.23a)
$$
\omega_{n+1} = \omega_n + \alpha_n g_1(\theta_n, \omega_n). \quad (8.23b)
$$

where $\{\theta_n\}$ evolves in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and $\{\omega_n\}$ evolves in $\mathbb{R}^m$. The assumption (8.22) is maintained, which is what makes this a two-time-scale algorithm.

The ODE approximation for these recursions will not be covered in any generality here, but the main message is something every user of SA should understand.

As always, we recall the mean vector fields $f(\theta, \omega) = \mathbb{E}[f_1(\theta, \omega)]$ and $\tilde{g}(\theta, \omega) = \mathbb{E}[g_1(\theta, \omega)]$ (steady-state effects). For each $\omega \in \mathbb{R}^m$, assume there is a unique vector $\theta^*(\omega)$ solving $\tilde{g}(\theta, \omega) = 0$. Because the second parameter sequence is so slow compared to the first, it is possible to prove under general conditions that $\theta_n \approx \theta^*(\omega_n)$ for large $n$. This leads to the following ODE approximation for the second recursion:

$$
\frac{dw}{dt} = \tilde{g}(\theta^*(w), w). \quad (8.24)
$$

See Theorem 8.3 for example of convergence theory based on these concepts. The insight is valuable in algorithm design:

- (i) It is used to establish (8.18), which is required for convergence of parameter estimates in Theorem 8.1. The proposition follows makes clear why we require (8.21). The uniform bound on the second moment of $\{\Delta_k\}$ is in Proposition 8.2 (see (8.6)).
- (ii) It is critical in constrained optimization, where $\theta_n$ is an approximation of $\theta^*$.
- (iii) The elegant approximation (8.24) is possible through dual-scale methods, as seen in Section 4.5.
