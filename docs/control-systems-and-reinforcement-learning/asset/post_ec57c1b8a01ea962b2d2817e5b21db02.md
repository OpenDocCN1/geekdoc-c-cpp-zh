## 4.11 ODE Methods for Algorithm Design

The ODE (4.14a) was introduced in the economics literature, which led to the comprehensive analysis by Smale [325]. The term Newton–Raphson flow for (4.14a) was introduced in the deterministic control literature [320, 370]. The Zap Sa algorithm was introduced at the same time, and based on the same ODE [90, 110, 112]. Within the optimization literature, the term “Newton–Raphson dynamical system” is used: see [5] for history. Much more on this technique will appear in the second half of the book. See Section 8.5.2 for a variant that does not require matrix inversion,

The paper [335] sparked new appreciation for ODE methods within the optimization community (with particular interest in applications to ML). The goal was to understand the dynamics of two common optimization algorithms with “acceleration”:

- (i) Polyak’s heavy-ball method:

```
$x_{k+1} = x_k + \delta_{k+1}[x_k - x_{k-1}] - \alpha_k\nabla\Gamma(x_k). \quad (4.155)$
```

- (ii) Nesterov’s accelerated gradient algorithm:

```
$x_{k+1} = y_k - \alpha_k\nabla\Gamma(y_k), \quad y_k = x_k + \delta_k[x_k - x_{k-1}]. \quad (4.156)$
```

An ODE approximation for Polyak’s (4.155) with $\delta_k \equiv \delta$ is easily anticipated by denoting $\theta_k = x_k$, and $D_{k+1} = \theta_{k+1} - \theta_k$ [16]. For simplicity, take $\alpha = 1$ and write (4.155)

```
$D_{k+1} - D_k = -(1-\delta)D_k - \Gamma(\theta_k).
```

This is an Euler approximation of the second-order ODE:

```
$$\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -(1-\delta)\frac{d\theta}{dt} - \Gamma(\theta).$$
```

Nesterov’s algorithm (4.156) is cited in [335], for alerting me to Smale’s early, using the time-varying choice of $\delta_k$. A similar ODE approximation is established:

```
$$\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\beta_t\frac{d\theta}{dt} - \Gamma(\theta), \quad \beta_t = 3/t.$$
```

The appearance of “3” is explained by the representation $\delta_k = 1/(k+2)$. Better understanding of the dynamics of these ODEs leads to a much fuller understanding of why Polyak and Nesterov were successful [16, 198, 335]. This work was part of growing interest in ODE and stochastic differential equation (SDE) approximations for more efficient recursive algorithms [200, 294, 318, 375], for neural network approximation [84, 358], and ODE design based on concepts from robust control

### 4.11.3 QSA

Section 4.5 was initially conceived as an early introduction to stochastic approximation design – a topic explored in Chapter 8. Over the course of writing this book, it evolved to become a stand-alone toolkit for optimization and control. Much of Sections 4.5–7 is adapted from [40, 41, 87], which was inspired by prior results [24, 319]; [393] contains algorithms for gradient-free optimization with constraints. The QSA concept was first introduced in [212, 213] for applications to finance.

The theory of two-scale stochastic approximation enjoys a parallel history with the theory of singular perturbations of differential equations, which has played an important role in control theory and applications [186]. A simple example is the dynamical system described by the following equations:

```
$x_1 = f_1(x_1, x_2),$
x_2 = f_2(x_1, x_2).
```

It is observed that $\epsilon \ll 1$, so that the dynamics of $x_2$ are much faster than that of $x_1$. Suppose that this is a function of time, with $\epsilon \downarrow 0$ as $t \uparrow$. Assume moreover that there is a continuous function $\phi$ satisfying $f_2(x_1, \phi(x_1)) = 0$ for each $x_1$. Under further conditions, there is a tight approximation preceding the preceding ODE and

```
$x_1 = f_1(x_1, \phi(x_1)).
```

This is the thinking behind Zap QSA (4.91).

Polyak averaging was introduced independently by their namesakes [287, 288, 306] (note that Polyak had independent contributions prior to his collaboration with Juditskiy). This work has nothing to do with QSA, but concerns pertaining to optimizing the covariance $\Sigma\theta$ appearing in (6.40) for stochastic approximation – see the “Notes” section of Chapter 8 for more background. The application of averaging techniques for optimization in QSA appears to be new.

The gradient $\hat{g}$ in Lemma 4.37 (1) is precisely the solution to Poisson’s equation, with forcing $\hat{g} = g - a_0$, that appears in simulation of Markov processes, average-cost optimal control, and stochastic approximation [12, 39, 144, 250].

The phrase ODE method is frequently attributed to Ljung [229], though most authors use this to mean a method of analysis rather than a technique for algorithm design. Polyak in [136] credits Kushkin [357] for the realization that stochastic approximation is an invaluable ingredient in the algorithms of learning.

The ODE method (4.113) was introduced in [69] for stability verification in stochastic approximation: Proposition 4.22 is a special case of the Borkar-Meyn theorem [66, 69], which has been refined considerably in recent years [296, 297]. The use of ODE methods in the field of online optimization with application to control [96, 263] is worth looking over.
