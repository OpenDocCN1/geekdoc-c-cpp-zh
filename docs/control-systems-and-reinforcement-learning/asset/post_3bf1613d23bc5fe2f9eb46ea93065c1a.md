
## 2.5 A Glance Ahead: From Control Theory to RL

After collecting $N$ observations, we obtain the mean-square loss:

```
$\Gamma^c(J) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} [D_{k+1}(J)]^2. \quad (2.53)$
```

We are then faced with another machine learning problem: minimize this objective function over $J$ in a given class (for example, this is where neural networks frequently play a role).

If we can make (2.53) nearly zero, then we have a good estimate of a value function. Beyond its application to actor-critic methods, there are TD- and Q-learning techniques, designed to minimize (2.53) or a surrogate, that are part of a RL toolbox.

## 2.5.3 Bandits and Exploration

Suppose that our policy is not optimal in any sense, but $x(k) \to x^e$, $u(k) \to u^e$ rapidly as $k \to \infty$, where the limit satisfies $c(x^e, u^e) = 0$. We typically then have

```
$\lim_{k\to\infty} [-J(x(k+1)) + \tilde{J}(x(k)) - c(x(k), u(k))] = \tilde{J}(x^e) + \tilde{J}(x^e) - c(x^e, u^e) = 0. \quad (2.54)$
```

It follows that we aren't observing much via the temporal difference (2.52). If $N$ is large, then $\Gamma^c(\tilde{J}) \approx 0$. This essentially destroys any hope for a reliable estimate of the value function. Express another way: a good policy does not lead to sufficient exploration of the state space.

There are many ways to introduce exploration. We can, for example, adapt our criterion as follows: denote by $\Gamma^c(J; x)$ the mean-square loss obtained with $x(0)=x$. Rather than take a very long run, perform many shorter runs, from many $(M > 1)$ initial conditions. The loss function to be minimized is the average

```
$\Gamma^c(J) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{M} \Gamma^c(J; x^i). \quad (2.55)$
```

The best way to choose the samples $\{x^i\}$ is a topic of research.

Another approach is to let the input explore the exploring. The policy is modified slightly through the introduction of "noise":

```
$u(k) = \phi(x(k), \xi(k)).
```

For example, $\xi(k)$ might be a scalar value, defined as a mixture of sinusoids. The noisy policy is defined so that

- (i) $\phi(x(k), \xi(k)) \approx \phi^0(x(k))$ for "most $k$."
- (ii) The state process "explores." in the policy is designed to avoid convergence of $(x(k), u(k))$ to any limiting value.

This is a crude approach, since by changing the input process, the associated value function also changes. More sensible approaches are contained in Chapters 4 and 5, and the second part of the book: Q-learning and "off-policy SARSA" might be designed around an exploratory policy as this one, but these algorithms are carefully designed to avoid bias from exploration.

## Control Crash Course

The theory of exploration is mature only within a very special setting: multi-armed bandits. The term "bandit" refers to slot machines: you put money in the machine, pull an arm, and hope that money pops out. A rational application is in the advertisement industry, in which an "arm" is an advertisement (which costs money), and the advertiser hopes money will pop out as ads encourage sale. There is a great history of heuristics and science to create successful algorithms to maximize profit, based only on noisy observations of the performance of candidate algorithms (l216 is a great reference on the theory of bandits, and a short survey contained in Section 7.8). It is here that the "exploration/exploitation" trade-off is most clearly seen: you have to accept some loss of revenue through exploration in order to learn the best strategy, and then "exploit" as you gain confidence in your estimates.

The situation is much more complex in control applications: imagine that for each state $x(k)$, there is a multi-armed bandit. "Pulling arm a" at time $k$ choosing $u(k)=a \in U$. Concepts from bandit theory have led to heuristics to balance the exploration/exploitation trade-offs arising in RL. It is an exciting direction for future research [171, 307].

## 2.6 How Can We Ignore Noise?

It is hard to explain this to a student without a background in probability theory. If you have some exposure to stochastic processes, then you might want to skip Section 7.2: you will learn how to construct a deterministic "fluid model" or "mean-field model" based on a more detailed and complex stochastic state space model, and just justification for control design based on the simpler model.

The pragmatic answer to this question is that we have a reliable model of disturbances, so we leave them unmodeled but not ignored. There is, we attempt to create a control architecture that is not sensitive to disturbances. There is an elegant theory of robust control for this purpose, though even "robustness" is only with respect to disturbances within some uncertainty. The most successful outcomes of this literature heavily on domain contexts. For example, it is assumed that disturbances (the $d$ shown in Figure 2.1) are largely limited to lower frequencies, and noise measurement (the $w$ on the right-hand side of this figure) is limited to higher frequencies.

Justification for nonlinear control systems is based on Lyapunov techniques. We establish control through a Lyapunov function $V$ as outlined in Section 2.4.3, and argue that $V$ will continue to have "negative drift" in the form (2.31) even with error in the model $F$, or in the presence of the disturbance $d$.

Finally, the naive "disturbance-free" model obtained through physics, or through techniques surveyed in Section 7.2, provides a great deal of insight for the structure of control solutions. We might use this insight to build architectures for reinforcement learning.

## Where Is the Control Problem?

Let's consider the specific problem of stock portfolio management. The goal is to create a computer program that makes decisions second-by-second on what stocks to buy or sell. The goal is to “maximize profit,” but there is also the notion of risk, which is not easily defined without tools from probability and statistics.

Perhaps more significant is that this control problem is not of the centralized variety. Consider how Figure 2.1 is interpreted for stock trading. The process is the global economy and everything that goes along with it! The two blocks state feedback and observe the results of individual decision makers (the “agents”) who forecast future prices (and other events), and employ optimization strategies for online decision making. Trajectory generation will be local to each agent; this might represent purchase orders for new computers, new staff, or a new office closer to Wall Street.

In summary: trading is a game rather than a classical control problem, but this should not stop us. As an individual (or designing software for others), we can treat “process” as the actions of all other players as a larger process. Reinforcement learning is an appealing approach to control design because the learning (or training) does not require a detailed model (though significant data are required).

## State Feedback?

How we interpret $u(k) = \phi(x(k))$ The input $u(k)$ is easy to understand, given the preceding description of the stock management problem.

What is the $x(k)$? I don’t know, and I would not trust anyone who claims to have an answer! It is traditional to view prices as a stochastic process that evolves according to the actions of millions of citizens and hundreds of corporations. There is modeling based on martingales and changes of measure, so that theory from mathematical finance may provide intuition into how to construct a state process. A “quick reaction” might be this: $x(k) = x^0(k)$, the value of all stock prices at time $k$. Without any knowledge of finance, my gut tells me this would be a huge mistake. Here are examples of what many would add after reflection:

- (i) History of prices. It is important to visit recent performance in terms of trends and volatility.
- (ii) Forecasts of prices. You may have inside knowledge. You may realize that tweets from influential people provide insight on the decisions of others, which will then influence stock prices.
- (iii) What is the objective? Once you have a formulation of reward and risk, make sure that these essential quantities are functions of your state process.
