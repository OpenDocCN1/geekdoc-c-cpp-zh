To apply the LL and CLT for approximation, it is necessary to estimate the asymptotic variance. Unfortunately, none of the preceding expressions are useful for anything but analysis, or to inspire algorithms. The representation (6.40a) is inspiration for the batch method:

### Batch Means Method

$M$ independent trials, based on $N$ observations, to obtain $M$ estimates:

$$\eta_N^i = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} c(X^i(k)), \quad 1 \leq i \leq M.$$

Estimates of the mean and asymptotic variance are defined by

$$\eta_N = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{M} \eta_N^i, \quad \hat{\sigma}_{clr}^2 = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{M} (\eta_N^i - \eta_N)^2.$$

See [133] for estimators that are potentially more potent. The estimates in (6.41b) are used to obtain an appropriate confidence bound: For given $\delta > 0$, choose $r > 0$ such that $P(\hat{\sigma}_{clr}^2 \geq r) = \delta$. Then, for large $N$, we have confidence that $\eta$ lies in the interval $[\eta_N - r/\sqrt{N}, \eta_N + r/\sqrt{N}]$, with probability approximately $\delta$. If $r/\sqrt{N}$ is not very small, the run length $N$ should be increased.

### Care with Histograms

The procedure (6.41b) to estimate the asymptotic variance is only effective if the variance is finite, and the initial conditions in the $M$ trials are widely spaced apart.

## 6.7.2 Asymptotic Variance and Mixing Time

The “mixing time” is formally defined as the number of iterations required for the Markov chain to approximately reach its steady-state distribution. This might be formalized with the introduction of $\epsilon > 0$ to quantify “approximately,” and $T(\epsilon) > 0$ denote the minimal time for

$$|P^n(x, A) - \pi(A)| \le \epsilon, \quad \text{for all } n \ge T(\epsilon), \quad A \subset X.$$

This implies following bound for functions $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying $|f(x)| \le 1$ for all $x$:

$$E_x[f(X(n)) - \pi(f)]| \le 2\epsilon, \quad \text{for all } n \ge T(\epsilon).$$

This follows on observing that a function $f$ maximizing the left-hand side can be taken of the form $f^*(x) = 2\mathbb{1}_A-1$ for some $A \in \mathcal{B}(X)$. In the finite state space setting, we can take $A = \{y : P(x, y) \ge \pi(y)\}$.

Remaining in the finite state space setting, it is immediate from Theorem 6.2 and the surrounding discussion that the mixing time will be very large if there is an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $P$ satisfying $\lambda \neq 1$ but $|\lambda| \sim 1$. In particular, if $\lambda$ is on the unit circle, such as $|\lambda|=1$, the mixing time is infinite. This is an example of a two-state Markov chain with transition matrix $P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

## 6.7.3 Sample Complexity

What if a random interval gives you no confidence? A deterministic interval requires what is known as finite-$n$ bound,

$$P[\eta_n - \eta \ge r/\sqrt{n}] \le \mathcal{B}(n,r), \quad (6.42)$$

$$P[\eta_n - \eta \ge \varepsilon] \le \tilde{b}\exp(-nI(\varepsilon)), \quad (6.43)$$

where $\tilde{b}$ is a finite constant, and $I(\varepsilon) > 0$ for $\varepsilon > 0$. This leads to sample complexity bounds for estimating the mean—the terminology is explained in Section 8.2 in a more general setting.
