# 5.9.3 Q-Learning

First, why Q? The question of its origin was raised by Aaron Snowsos (then a graduate student at Queensland University of Technology) during the early days of the fall 2020 Simon’s program on reinforcement learning. Csaba Szesvari contacted Chris Watkins, who responded shortly after: “... which letter to choose? I realised I hadn’t used Q, that enigmatic letter, and now one could retro-fit ‘quality’...”. An enigmatic letter is fitting for this mysterious class of algorithms!

+   The Q(λ) algorithm (5.52) is not commonly seen in textbooks, except for the special case λ = 0. Q(0)-learning, and variants are commonly applied because they are the natural theory for Watkins’s Q-learning algorithm for controlled Markov chains. There is a firm theory for convergence of Q(0)-learning in the special case considered by Watkins, in part because it is assumed that the function class is “complete” contains every possible function of X × U. Convergence of Watkins’ algorithm is established by ODE methods in Section 9.6.

Major successes in DQN are described in [255–261], and [9] contains an insightful analysis. The limit theory for the algorithm (5.58) measured in Proposition 5.10 is taken from [27]. The refinement of DQN introduced in Exercise 5.10 is inspired by the convex-concave optimization of Hartman [155, 226]. Another approach to batch RL is based on empirical valuation [153, 174, 317].

The origin of convex Q-learning is [245], where a family of LP approaches is formulated in continuous time. The challenge at the time was to find ways to create a reliable online algorithm. The algorithm challenges addressed in [246, 247], which is the basis of Section 5.5.

For more RL in continuous time, consider reviewing the research program of Lewis [184, 359], the volume [360], and the recent survey [220].

From Section 3.1 Mann introduced the linear programming approach to dynamic programming in the 1960 article [237]. A significant program on linear programming approaches dynamic programming for Markovian decision processes (MDPs) began in [102–104, 314] and continues today.

The algorithms and theory in Section 5.5 are based on [247], which builds on the first version of convex Q-learning [245] (designed for deterministic systems in continuous time). Improvements made in the following decade, primarily in a stochastic control setting. For example, the theory in [218] is based on a variant of the convex program (3.36), with the inequality in (3.36b) replaced by equality: it contains substantial theory for the tabular setting (recall (5.10)). More recent results in [28] obtain efficient algorithms for the LP formulation closely related to the DPLP (3.36).
