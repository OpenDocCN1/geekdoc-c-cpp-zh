+   - Obfuscation (which is not a security boundary) can make it costly—either or in complexity—for the unwanted behavior to disable the detection mechanism. This added cost means that the unwanted behavior is more clearly identified as potentially malicious, and its complexity results in additional costs to a potential attacker. By shifting the obfuscation techniques, it means legitimate vendors are better off shifting the time to move away from legacy extension mechanisms and implement supported techniques instead, without the risk of looking like malware.

+   - Randomized and non-documentation of which specific checks the detection/prevention mechanism makes to monitor kernel integrity, and non-determination of which checks are executed, crippling the ability of attackers to ensure their exploits are reliable. It forces them to account for every possible non-deterministic variable and state that the mechanism has through static analysis, which obfuscation makes nearly impossible within the timeframe before another obfuscation shift or feature change is implemented in the mechanism.

+   - Because kernel crash dumps are automatically submitted to Microsoft, it allows the company to receive telemetry of in-the-wild unintended code, and to identify software vendors whose code is unsupported and is crashing systems, or to track the progress of malicious drivers in the wild, or even zero-day kernel-mode exploitations, and bugs that may not have been reported, but are actively exploited.

# # PatchGuard

Shortly after the release of 64-bit Windows for x64 and a rich third-party ecosystem had developed, Microsoft saw an opportunity to preserve the stability of 64-bit Windows, and to add telemetry and exploit-crippling patch detection to the system, through a technology called *Kernel Patch Protection (KPP)*, also referred to as *PatchGuard*. When Windows Mobile was released, it was featured on a 32-bit ARM processor core, the will be present in 64-bit ARM (AArch64) systems as well. Due to the existence of many legacy 32-bit drivers that use unsupported and dangerous hooking techniques, this mechanism is not enabled on systems, even on Windows 10 operating systems. Fortunately, 32-bit systems is almost coming to an end, and server versions of Windows no longer support this architecture at all.

Although both *Guard and Protection* imply that this mechanism will protect the system, it is important to realize that the only guard/protection offered is the crashing of the machine, which prevents further execution of the unwanted code. The mechanism does not *prevent* the attack in the first place, nor mitigate against it, nor undo. Think of KPP as an Internet-connected video security system, or CCTV, with a loud alarm (the crash) inside the vault (the kernel), not an impenetrable lock on the vault.

KPP has a variety of checks that it makes on protected systems, and documenting them all would be impractical (due to the difficulty of static analysis) and valuable to potential attackers (reducing their research time). However, Microsoft does document checks, which we generate in Table 7-23. When, and how KPP makes these checks, and which specific functions or data structures are affected, is outside of the scope of this analysis.
