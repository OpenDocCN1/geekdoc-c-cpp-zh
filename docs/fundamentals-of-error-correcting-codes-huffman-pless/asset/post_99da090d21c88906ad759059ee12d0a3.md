
We first define a lattice in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Let \(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\) be \(n\) linearly independent vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), where \(\mathbf{v}_i = (v_{i,1}, v_{i,2}, \ldots, v_{i,n})\). The lattice \(\Lambda\) with basis \(\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}\) is the set of all integer combinations of \(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\); these integer combinations of basis vectors are the *points* of the lattice. Thus

\(\Lambda = \{z_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + z_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + z_n \mathbf{v}_n \mid z_i \in \mathbb{Z}, 1 \le i \le n\}.\)

The matrix

\(M = \begin{bmatrix} v_{1,1} & v_{1,2} & \cdots & v_{1,n} \\ v_{2,1} & v_{2,2} & \cdots & v_{2,n} \\ \vdots & & & \\ v_{n,1} & v_{n,2} & \cdots & v_{n,n} \end{bmatrix}\)

is called the *generator matrix* of \(\Lambda\); thus \(\Lambda = \{\mathbf{z} M \mid \mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{Z}^n\}\). The \(n \times n\) matrix

\(A = M M^T\)

is called the *Gram matrix* for \(\Lambda\); its \((i, j)\)-entry is the (ordinary) inner product (over the real numbers) of \(\mathbf{v}_i\) and \(\mathbf{v}_j\). The *determinant* or *discriminant* of the lattice \(\Lambda\) is

\(\det \Lambda = \det A = (\det M)^2.\)

This determinant has a natural geometric meaning: det \(\Lambda\) is the volume of the *fundamental parallelotope* of \(\Lambda\), which is the region

\(\{a_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + a_n \mathbf{v}_n \mid 0 \le a_i < 1, a_i \in \mathbb{R} \text{ for } 1 \le i \le n\}.\)

### **Example 10.6.1**
The lattice \(\mathbb{Z}^2\) with basis \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 1)\) has generator and Gram matrices both equal to the identity matrix \(I_2\). Also \(\det \mathbb{Z}^2 = 1\). This lattice is clearly the set of all points in the plane with integer coordinates.

### **Example 10.6.2**
Let \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (\sqrt{2}, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (\sqrt{2}/2, \sqrt{6}/2)\). The lattice \(\Lambda_2\), shown in Figure 10.3, with basis \(\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}\) has generator and Gram matrices

\(M_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 0 \\ \sqrt{2}/2 & \sqrt{6}/2 \end{bmatrix} \text{ and } A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)

This lattice is often called the *planar hexagonal lattice*; its determinant is 3.

If the Gram matrix of a lattice has integer entries, then the inner product between any two lattice points is always integer valued, and the lattice is called *integral*. The \(\mathbb{Z}^2\) and planar hexagonal lattices defined in Examples 10.6.1 and 10.6.2 are integral. Note that in any lattice, we could rescale all the basis vectors by the same scalar; this does not change the “geometry” of the lattice, that is, the relative positions in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) of the lattice points, but could change other properties, such as the integrality of the lattice or the size of the fundamental region (\(\det \Lambda\)). If we multiply each basis vector of \(\Lambda\) by the scalar \(c\), then the resulting

lattice is denoted \(c\Lambda\). The generating matrix of \(c\Lambda\) is \(cM\) and the Gram matrix is \(c^2A\). We also essentially obtain the same lattice if we rotate or reflect \(\Lambda\). This leads to the concept of equivalence: the lattice \(\Lambda\) with generator matrix \(M\) is *equivalent* to the lattice \(\Lambda'\) if there is a scalar \(c\), a matrix \(U\) with integer entries and \(\det U = \pm 1\), and a matrix \(B\) with real entries and \(BB^T = I_n\) such that \(cUMB\) is a generator matrix of \(\Lambda'\). (The matrix \(B\) acts as a series of rotations and the matrix \(U\) acts as a series of reflections.) Generally, when choosing a representative lattice in an equivalence class of lattices, one attempts to choose a representative that is integral and has determinant as small as possible.

Using the ordinary inner product in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), we can define the *dual lattice* \(\Lambda^*\) of \(\Lambda\) to be the set of vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) whose inner product with every lattice point in \(\Lambda\) is an integer; that is,

\[
\Lambda^* = \{ \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ for all } \mathbf{x} \in \Lambda \}.
\]

An integral lattice \(\Lambda\) is called *unimodular* or *self-dual* if \(\Lambda = \Lambda^*\).

### **Theorem 10.6.3**
Let \(\Lambda\) be a lattice in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with basis \(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\), generator matrix \(M\), and Gram matrix \(A\). The following hold:

- (i) \(\Lambda^* = \{ \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid \mathbf{v}_i \cdot \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ for all } 1 \le i \le n \} = \{ \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid \mathbf{y} M^T \in \mathbb{Z}^n \}\).
- (ii) The generator matrix of \(\Lambda^*\) is \((M^{-1})^T\).
- (iii) The Gram matrix of \(\Lambda^*\) is \(A^{-1}\).
- (iv) \(\det \Lambda^* = 1/\det \Lambda\).
- (v) \(\Lambda\) is integral if and only if \(\Lambda \subseteq \Lambda^*\).
- (vi) If \(\Lambda\) is integral, then
  \[
  \Lambda \subseteq \Lambda^* \subseteq \frac{1}{\det \Lambda} \Lambda = (\det \Lambda^*)\Lambda.
  \]
- (vii) If \(\Lambda\) is integral, then \(\Lambda\) is unimodular if and only if \(\det \Lambda = \pm 1\).

Proof: We leave the proofs of (i) and (v) for Exercise 605. For (ii), let \(\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n\) be the rows of \((M^{-1})^T\). Then \((\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n)\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) and, as \((M^{-1})^T M^T = (MM^{-1})^T = I_n\),

\[
\mathbf{w}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_j = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = j, \\ 0 & \text{if } i \neq j. \end{cases}
\] (10.10)

In particular, \(\{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n\} \subseteq \Lambda^*\) by (i). Let \(\mathbf{w} \in \Lambda^*\). Then \(\mathbf{w} = a_1 \mathbf{w}_1 + \cdots + a_n \mathbf{w}_n\) as \(\{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n\}\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). As \(\mathbf{w} \in \Lambda^*\), \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{v}_j \in \mathbb{Z}\). But \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{v}_j = a_j\) for \(1 \le j \le n\) by (10.10). Hence \(a_j \in \mathbb{Z}\) and (ii) holds. The Gram matrix of \(\Lambda^*\) is \((M^{-1})^T M^{-1} = (MM^T)^{-1} = A^{-1}\) yielding (iii). Thus

\[
\det \Lambda^* = \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det A} = \frac{1}{\det \Lambda}.
\]

producing (iv).

For (vi) we only need to prove the second containment by parts (iv) and (v). Let \(\mathbf{y} \in \Lambda^*\). Then \(\mathbf{y} M^T \in \mathbb{Z}^n\) by (i), and hence there exists \(\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{Z}^n\) such that \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{z}(M^T)^{-1} = \mathbf{z} A^{-1} M\). But \(A^{-1} = (\det \Lambda)^{-1} \text{adj}(A)\), where \(\text{adj}(A)\) is the adjoint of \(A\). Hence

\[
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{z}(\det \Lambda)^{-1} \text{adj}(A) M = \mathbf{z}'(\det \Lambda)^{-1} M,
\]

where \(\mathbf{z}' = \mathbf{z} \text{adj}(A) \in \mathbb{Z}^n\) as \(\text{adj}(A)\) has integer entries since \(\Lambda\) is integral. Thus \(\mathbf{y} \in (\det \Lambda)^{-1} \Lambda\) verifying (vi).

Finally by (vi) if \(\Lambda\) is unimodular, then \(\det \Lambda = \det \Lambda^* = 1/(\det \Lambda)\) implying \(\det \Lambda = \pm 1\). Conversely, if \(\det \Lambda = \pm 1\) and \(\Lambda\) is integral, (vi) shows that \(\Lambda \subseteq \Lambda^* \subseteq \pm \Lambda\) and \(\Lambda = \Lambda^*\) as clearly \(\Lambda = \pm \Lambda\).

In an integral lattice \(\Lambda\), \(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{x}\) is an integer for all \(\mathbf{x} \in \Lambda\). An integral lattice is called *even* if \(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{x}\) is an even integer for all \(\mathbf{x} \in \Lambda\). In particular, the Gram matrix has even entries on its main diagonal. An integral lattice that is not even is called *odd*. A lattice has *Type II* provided it is an even unimodular lattice and *Type I* if it is an odd unimodular lattice. Type I lattices exist for any dimension \(n\); however, Type II lattices exist if and only if \(n \equiv 0 (\text{mod } 8)\). The unimodular lattices have been classified up to equivalence through dimension 25; note that Type I codes have been classified through length 30 and Type II through length 32.
