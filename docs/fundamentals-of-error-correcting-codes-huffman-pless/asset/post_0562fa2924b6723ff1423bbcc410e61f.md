### Exercise 728 Prove Lemma 12.4.2 when $r$ is even.

We now define the $\mathbb{Z}_4$-quadratic residue codes using the idempotents of Lemma 12.4.2. The definitions depend upon the value of $p$ modulo 8.

## 12.4.1 $\mathbb{Z}_4$-quadratic residue codes: $p \equiv -1 \pmod 8$

We first look at the case where $p \equiv -1 \pmod 8$. Let $p + 1 = 8r$. If $r$ is odd, define $D_1 = \langle Q(x) + 2N(x) \rangle$, $D_2 = \langle N(x) + 2Q(x) \rangle$, $C_1 = \langle 1 - N(x) + 2Q(x) \rangle$, and $C_2 = \langle 1 - Q(x) + 2N(x) \rangle$. If $r$ is even, define $D_1 = \langle -Q(x) \rangle$, $D_2 = \langle -N(x) \rangle$, $C_1 = \langle 1 + N(x) \rangle$, and $C_2 = \langle 1 + Q(x) \rangle$. These codes are called $\mathbb{Z}_4$-quadratic residue codes when $p \equiv -1 \pmod 8$.

The next result, from [279, 289], shows why these codes are called quadratic residue codes; compare this result with those in Chapter 6.

### Theorem 12.4.3 Let $p \equiv -1 \pmod 8$. The $\mathbb{Z}_4$-quadratic residue codes satisfy the following:

- (i) $D_1 \mu_a = D_1$ and $C_1 \mu_a = C_1$ for $a \in \mathbb{Q}_p$; $D_1 \mu_a = D_2$ and $C_1 \mu_a = C_2$ for $a \in N_p$; in particular $D_1$ and $D_2$ are equivalent as are $C_1$ and $C_2$.
- (ii) $D_1 \cap D_2 = \langle j(x) \rangle$ and $D_1 + D_2 = \mathfrak{R}_p$.
- (iii) $C_1 \cap C_2 = \{0\}$ and $C_1 + C_2 = \langle j(x) \rangle^\perp$.
- (iv) $D_1$ and $D_2$ have type $4^{(p+1)/2}$. $C_1$ and $C_2$ have type $4^{(p-1)/2}$.
- (v) $D_i = C_i + \langle j(x) \rangle$ for $i = 1$ and $2$.
- (vi) $C_1$ and $C_2$ are self-orthogonal and $C_i^\perp = D_i$ for $i = 1$ and $2$.

Proof: Suppose that $p + 1 = 8r$. We verify this result when $r$ is odd and leave the proof for $r$ even as an exercise.

For (i), \( a \in N_p \), then \( (Q(x) + 2N(x))\mu_a = N(x) + 2Q(x) \). Theorem 4.3.13(i) (whose proof is still valid for cyclic codes over \( \mathbb{Z}_4 \)) implies that \( \mathcal{D}_1\mu_a = \mathcal{D}_2 \). Similarly, if \( a \in Q_p \), then \( (Q(x) + 2N(x))\mu_a = Q(x) + 2N(x) \) and \( (N(x) + 2Q(x))\mu_a = N(x) + 2Q(x) \), implying \( \mathcal{D}_1\mu_a = \mathcal{D}_1 \). The parts of (i) involving \( C_i \) are similar.

Since \( p \equiv -1 \pmod 8 \), \( \bar{j}(x) = 3 \sum_{i=0}^{p-1} x^i = 3 + 3Q(x) + 3N(x) \). Thus \( (Q(x) + 2N(x))(N(x) + 2Q(x)) = (Q(x) + 2N(x))(\bar{j}(x) + 1 - (Q(x) + 2N(x))) = (Q(x) + 2N(x))(1 - Q(x) - 2N(x)) + Q(x) + 2N(x)) = (Q(x) + 2N(x))(1 - Q(x) - 2N(x)) + Q(x) + 2N(x)) = ... \) proving part (ii).

Using \( (Q(x) + 2N(x))(N(x) + 2Q(x)) = \bar{j}(x) \), for (iii) we have \( (1 - N(x) + 2Q(x)) \times (1 - Q(x) + 2N(x)) = 1 - N(x) + 2Q(x) - Q(x) + N(x)(2N(x) + N(x) + 2Q(x))(Q(x) + 2N(x)) = 1 + N(x) + Q(x) + \bar{j}(x) = 0 \) proving that \( C_1 \cap C_2 = \{0\} \). Since \( (1 - N(x) + 2Q(x))(1 - Q(x) + 2N(x)) = 0 \), \( C_1 + C_2 \) has generating idempotent \( 1 - N(x) + 2Q(x) + 1 - Q(x) + 2N(x) = 2 + N(x) + Q(x) = 1 - \bar{j}(x) = 1 - \bar{j}(x)\mu_{-1} \) as \( \bar{j}(x)\mu_{-1} = \bar{j}(x) \). By Lemma 12.3.23, \( C_1 + C_2 = \langle \bar{j}(x) \rangle^\perp \), completing (iii).

For (iv), we observe that \( \mathcal{D}_1 = \mathcal{D}_2|_1 = |\mathcal{D}_1|/|\mathcal{D}_2| \cap \mathcal{D}_2| \). By (i), \( |\mathcal{D}_1| = |\mathcal{D}_2| \), and by (ii), \( |\mathcal{D}_1| = 4^{(p+1)/2} \) and \( |\mathcal{D}_1 \cap \mathcal{D}_2| = 4 \). Thus \( \mathcal{D}_1 \) and \( \mathcal{D}_2 \) have size \( 4^{(p+1)/2} \); this is also their type as each has an idempotent generator (as opposed to a generator that is the sum of an idempotent and twice an idempotent; see Theorem 12.3.22 and Exercise 724). The remainder of (iv) is similar using (i) and (iii).

By (ii), \( \bar{j}(x) \in \mathcal{D}_2 \) implying that \( (N(x) + 2Q(x))\bar{j}(x) = \bar{j}(x) \) as \( N(x) + 2Q(x) \) is the multiplicative identity of \( \mathcal{D}_2 \). By Lemma 12.3.23, the generating idempotent for \( C_1 + \langle \bar{j}(x) \rangle \) is \( 1 - N(x) + 2Q(x) + \bar{j}(x) - (1 - N(x) + 2Q(x))\bar{j}(x) = 1 - N(x) + 2Q(x) + \bar{j}(x) - (\bar{j}(x) - \bar{j}(x)) = Q(x) + 2N(x) \), proving that \( C_1 + \langle \bar{j}(x) \rangle = \mathcal{D}_1 \). Similarly, \( C_2 + \langle \bar{j}(x) \rangle = \mathcal{D}_2 \), completing (v).

Finally, by Lemma 12.3.23, the generating idempotent for \( C_1^\perp \) is \( 1 - (1 - N(x) + 2Q(x))\mu_{-1} = N(x)\mu_{-1} + 2Q(x)\mu_{-1} \). By Lemma 6.2.4, \( -1 \in N_p \) for \( p \equiv -1 \pmod 8 \). Hence \( N(x)\mu_{-1} = Q(x) \) and \( Q(x)\mu_{-1} = N(x) \). Therefore the generating idempotent for \( C_1^\perp \) is \( Q(x) + 2N(x) \), verifying that \( C_1^\perp = \mathcal{D}_2 \). This also implies \( C_2^\perp \) is self-orthogonal as \( C_2 \subseteq \mathcal{D}_1 \) from (v), completing (vi).

## 12.4.2 $\mathbb{Z}_4$-quadratic residue codes: \( p \equiv 1 \pmod 8 \)

When \( p \equiv 1 \pmod 8 \), we simply reverse the \( C_i \)’s and \( D_i \)’s from the codes defined in the case \( p \equiv -1 \pmod 8 \). Again let \( p = 1 + 8r \). If \( r \) is odd, define \( D_1 = \langle 1 - N(x) + 2Q(x) \rangle \), \( D_2 = \langle 1 - Q(x) + 2N(x) \rangle \), \( C_1 = \langle Q(x) + 2N(x) \rangle \), and \( C_2 = \langle N(x) + 2Q(x) \rangle \). If \( r \) is even, define \( D_1 = \langle 1 + N(x) \rangle \), \( D_2 = \langle 1 + Q(x) \rangle \), \( C_1 = \langle -Q(x) \rangle \), and \( C_2 = \langle -N(x) \rangle \). These codes are called \(\mathbb{Z}_4\)-quadratic residue codes when \( p \equiv 1 \pmod 8 \). We leave the proof of the next result as an exercise. Again see [279, 289].

### Theorem 12.4.5 Let \( p \equiv 1 \pmod 8 \). The \(\mathbb{Z}_4\)-quadratic residue codes satisfy the following:
