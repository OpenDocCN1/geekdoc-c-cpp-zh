In the theorem we allow $S_i$ to be defined as $y(\alpha^i)$ even when $i > 2t$; these may not be legitimate syndromes as $c(\alpha^i)$ may not be 0 in those cases.

## Exercise 296 Prove Theorem 5.4.1.

The syndromes lead to a system of equations involving the unknown error locations and the unknown error magnitudes. Notice that from (5.5) the syndromes satisfy

$$ S_i = y(\alpha^i) = \sum_{j=1}^v e_{k_j} (\alpha^{k_j})^i = \sum_{j=1}^v e_{k_j} (\alpha^i)^{k_j}. $$

for $1 \le i \le 2t$. To simplify the notation, for $1 \le j \le v$, let $E_j = e_{k_j}$ denote the error magnitude at coordinate $k_j$ and $X_j = \alpha^{k_j}$ denote the error location number corresponding to the error location $k_j$. By Theorem 3.3.1, if $\alpha^i = \alpha^k$ for $i$ and $k$ between 0 and $n - 1$, then $i = k$. Thus knowing $X_j$ uniquely determines the error location $k_j$. With this notation (5.6) becomes

$$ S_i = \sum_{j=1}^v E_j X_j^i, \quad \text{for } 1 \le i \le 2t, $$

which in turn leads to the system of equations:

$$ \begin{aligned} S_1 &= E_1 X_1 + E_2 X_2 + \cdots + E_v X_v, \\ S_2 &= E_1 X_1^2 + E_2 X_2^2 + \cdots + E_v X_v^2, \\ S_3 &= E_1 X_1^3 + E_2 X_2^3 + \cdots + E_v X_v^3, \\ &\vdots \\ S_{2t} &= E_1 X_1^{2t} + E_2 X_2^{2t} + \cdots + E_v X_v^{2t}. \end{aligned} $$

This system is nonlinear in the $X_j$'s with unknown coefficients $E_j$. The strategy is to use (5.7) to set up a linear system, involving new variables $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_v$, that will lead directly to the error location numbers. Once these are known, we return to the system (5.8), which is then a linear system in the $E_j$s and solve for the error magnitudes.

To this end, define the *error locator polynomial* to be

$$ \sigma(x) = (1 - x X_1)(1 - x X_2) \cdots (1 - x X_v) = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^v \sigma_i x^i. $$

The roots of $\sigma(x)$ are the inverses of the error location numbers and thus

$$ \sigma(X_j^{-1}) = 1 + \sigma_1 X_j^{-1} + \sigma_2 X_j^{-2} + \cdots + \sigma_v X_j^{-v} = 0 $$

for $1 \le j \le v$. Multiplying by $E_j X_j^{t+v}$ produces

$$ E_j X_j^{t+v} + \sigma_1 E_j X_j^{t+v-1} + \cdots + \sigma_v E_j X_j^t = 0 $$

for $1 \le j \le v$. Summing this over $j$ for $1 \le j \le v$ yields

$$ \sum_{j=1}^v E_j X_j^{t+v} + \sigma_1 \sum_{j=1}^v E_j X_j^{t+v-1} + \cdots + \sigma_v \sum_{j=1}^v E_j X_j^t = 0. \quad (5.9) $$

As long as $1 \le i$ and $i + v \le 2t$, these summations are the syndromes obtained in (5.7). Because $v \le t$, (5.9) becomes

$$ S_{t+v} + \sigma_1 S_{t+v-1} + \sigma_2 S_{t+v-2} + \cdots + \sigma_v S_t = 0 $$

or

$$ \sigma_1 S_{t+v-1} + \sigma_2 S_{t+v-2} + \cdots + \sigma_v S_t = -S_{t+v} \quad (5.10) $$

valid for $1 \le i \le v$. Thus we can find the $\sigma_i$s if we solve the matrix equation

$$ \begin{bmatrix} S_1 & S_2 & S_3 & \cdots & S_{v-1} & S_v \\ S_2 & S_3 & S_4 & \cdots & S_v & S_{v+1} \\ S_3 & S_4 & S_5 & \cdots & S_{v+1} & S_{v+2} \\ \vdots & & \vdots & & & \vdots \\ S_v & S_{v+1} & S_{v+2} & \cdots & S_{2v-2} & S_{2v-1} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_v \\ \sigma_{v-1} \\ \vdots \\ \vdots \\ \sigma_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -S_{v+1} \\ -S_{v+2} \\ -S_{v+3} \\ \vdots \\ -S_{2v} \end{bmatrix} \quad (5.11) $$

that arises from (5.10). The second step of our algorithm is to solve (5.11) for $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_v$.

Once this second step has been completed, $\sigma(x)$ has been determined. However, determining $v(x)$ is complicated by the fact that we do not know $v$, and hence we do not know the size of the system involved. We are searching for the solution which has the smallest value of $v$, and this is aided by the following lemma.

## Lemma 5.4.2 Let $\mu \le t$ and let

$$ M_\mu = \begin{bmatrix} S_1 & S_2 & \cdots & S_\mu \\ S_2 & S_3 & \cdots & S_{\mu+1} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ S_\mu & S_{\mu+1} & \cdots & S_{2\mu-1} \end{bmatrix}. $$

Then $M_\mu$ is nonsingular if $\mu = v$ and singular if $\mu > v$, where $v$ is the number of errors that have occurred.

Proof: If \(\mu > v\), let \(X_{v+1} = X_{v+2} = \cdots = X_\mu = 0\) and \(E_{v+1} = E_{v+2} = \cdots = E_\mu = 0\). Exercise 297 shows that if \(A_\mu\) and \(B_\mu\) are given by

$$ A_\mu = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ X_1 & X_2 & \cdots & X_\mu \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ X_1^{\mu-1} & X_2^{\mu-1} & \cdots & X_\mu^{\mu-1} \end{bmatrix} \text{ and } B_\mu = \begin{bmatrix} E_1 X_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & E_2 X_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & E_\mu X_\mu \end{bmatrix}, $$

then \(M_\mu = A_\mu B_\mu A_\mu^T\). Therefore \(\det M_\mu = \det A_\mu \det B_\mu \det A_\mu = 0\) as \(B_\mu\) is a diagonal matrix with 0 on the diagonal. If \(\mu = v\), then \(\det B_\mu \neq 0\) as \(B_\mu\) is a diagonal matrix with only nonzero entries on the diagonal. Also \(\det A_\mu \neq 0\) by Lemma 4.5.1 because \(A_\mu\) is a Vandermonde matrix with \(X_1, \dots, X_\mu\) distinct. Hence \(M_\mu\) is nonsingular if \(\mu = v\).

## Exercise 297

The following, where the notation is given in Lemma 5.4.2: (a) Show that if \(\mu > v\), \(S_i = \sum_{j=1}^\mu E_j X_j^i\) for \(1 \le i \le 2t\). (b) Show that \(M_\mu = A_\mu B_\mu A_\mu^T\). \(\blacklozenge\)

To execute the second step of our algorithm, we attempt to guess the number \(v\) of errors. Call our guess \(\mu\) and begin with \(\mu = t\), which is the largest that \(v\) could be. The coefficient matrix of the linear system (5.11) that we are attempting to solve is \(M_\mu = M_\mu\) in Lemma 5.4.2. If \(M_\mu\) is singular, we reduce our guess \(\mu\) to \(\mu = t - 1\) and decide whether or not \(M_\mu = M_{t-1}\) is singular. As long as we obtain a singular matrix, we continue to reduce our guess \(\mu\) of the number of errors by one until some \(M_\mu\) is nonsingular. With \(v = \mu\), solve (5.11) and thereby determine \(\sigma(x)\).

The third step is then to find the roots of $\sigma(x)$ and invert them to determine the error location numbers. This is usually done by exhaustive search checking $\sigma(\alpha^i)$ for $0 \le i < n$. The fourth step is to plug these numbers into (5.8) and solve this linear system for the error magnitudes $E_j$. In fact we only need to consider the first $v$ equations in (5.8) for the following reason. The coefficient matrix of the first $v$ equations has determinant

$$ \det \begin{bmatrix} X_1 & X_2 & \cdots & X_v \\ X_1^2 & X_2^2 & \cdots & X_v^2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ X_1^v & X_2^v & \cdots & X_v^v \end{bmatrix} = X_1 X_2 \cdots X_v \det \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ X_1 & X_2 & \cdots & X_v \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ X_1^{v-1} & X_2^{v-1} & \cdots & X_v^{v-1} \end{bmatrix} . $$

The right-hand side determinant is the determinant of a Vandermonde matrix; the latter is nonzero as the $X_j$'s are distinct.

The Peterson-Gorenstein-Zierler Decoding Algorithm for BCH codes is therefore the following:

- I. Compute the syndromes \(S_i = y(\alpha^i)\) for \(1 \le i \le 2t\).
- II. In the order \(\mu = t, \mu = t - 1, \dots\) decide if \(M_\mu\) is singular, stopping at the first value of \(\mu\) where \(M_\mu\) is nonsingular. Set \(v = \mu\) and solve (5.11) to determine \(\sigma(x)\).
- III. Find the roots of \(\sigma(x)\) by computing \(\sigma(\alpha^i)\) for \(0 \le i < n\). Invert the roots to get the error location numbers \(X_j\).
- IV. Solve the first $v$ equations of (5.8) to obtain the error magnitudes $E_j$.
