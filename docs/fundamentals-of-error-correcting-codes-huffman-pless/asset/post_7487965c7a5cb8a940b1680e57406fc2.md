
## Corollary 8.6.7
Let $(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{B})$ be a projective plane of order $\mu \equiv 2 \pmod 4$ with incidence matrix $A$. Then the ovals of $(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{B})$ are precisely the supports of the codewords of weight $\mu + 2$ in $C_2(A)$.

**Proof.** By Theorem 8.6.4, $C_2(A)^\perp$ is the subcode of even weight vectors in $C_2(A)$. The result follows from Theorem 8.6.6.

We have settled the question of the dimension of $C_2(A)$ for a projective plane of order $\mu$ except when $4 \mid \mu$. If $4 \mid \mu$, $C_2(A)$ is self-orthogonal and $C_2(A)$ has minimum weight $\mu + 1$ by Theorem 8.6.3; however, we do not know the dimension of $C_2(A)$. A special case of this was considered by K. J. C. Smith [316], where he examined designs arising from certain projective geometries. The next theorem, which we present without proof, gives a part of Smith's result. The projective plane denoted PG(2, $2^r$) is the plane whose points are the 1-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{F}_q^3$, where $q = 2^r$, and whose lines are the 2-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{F}_q^3$.

## Theorem 8.6.8
If $(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{B})$ is the projective plane PG(2, $2^r$), then $C_2(A)$ has dimension $3^r + 1$.

If $\mu$ is a power of any prime, then PG(2, $\mu$) yields a projective plane in the same fashion that PG(2, $2^r$) did above. The next theorem presents a special case of a result by F. J. MacWilliams and H. B. Mann [216] that gives the dimension of certain $C_p(A)$ when $p$ is an odd prime.

## Theorem 8.6.9
If $(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{B})$ is the projective plane PG(2, $p^r$) where $p$ is an odd prime, then $C_p(A)$ has dimension

\(\binom{p+1}{2} + 1\).

Combinatorists have long been fascinated with the problem of finding values $\mu$ for which there is a projective plane of that order or proving that no such plane can exist. Since PG(2, $\mu$) is a projective plane of order $\mu$ when $\mu$ is a power of a prime, projective planes exist for all prime power orders. There are three projective planes of order 9 that are not equivalent to PG(2, 9) or each other; see [182]. However, at the present time, there is no known projective plane of any order other than a prime power. In fact it is conjectured that projective planes of nonprime power orders do not exist.

The most useful result that allows one to show that a symmetric 2-($v$, $k$, $\lambda$) design cannot exist is the Bruck-Ryser-Chowla Theorem.

## Theorem 8.6.11 (Bruck-Ryser-Chowla)
If there exists a symmetric 2-($v$, $k$, $\lambda$) design where $\mu = k - \lambda$, then either:
- (i) $v$ is even and $\mu$ is a square; or
- (ii) $v$ is odd and $z^2 = \mu x^2 + (-1)^{(v-1)/2} \lambda y^2$ has an integer solution with not all of $x$, $y$, and $z$ equal to 0.

## Theorem 8.6.12
Suppose that there exists a symmetric \(2-(v, k, \lambda)\) design with order \(\mu = k - \lambda\). Let \(p \mid \mu\) where \(p\) is an odd prime.

- (i) If \(p \mid \mu^*\) and \(p \mid \lambda^*\), then \(\mu\) is a square modulo \(p\).
- (ii) If \(p \mid \mu^*\) and \(p \nmid \lambda^*\), then \((-1)^{(v-1)/2}2^\lambda\) is a square modulo \(p\).

As a corollary, we can prove the following result about the existence of projective planes.

## Corollary 8.6.13
Suppose that there exists a projective plane of order $\mu$ where $\mu \equiv 1 (\text{mod } 4)$ or $\mu \equiv 2 (\text{mod } 4)$. If $p$ is an odd prime with $p \mid \mu^*$, then $p \equiv 1 (\text{mod } 4)$.

Proof: The number of points $v$ of the plane is $v = \mu^2 + \mu + 1$. Hence $(v-1)/2$ is odd when $\mu$ is as stated. Thus by Theorem 8.6.12(ii), $-1$ is a square modulo $p$. By Lemma 6.2.4, $p \equiv 1 (\text{mod } 4)$.

## 8.7 Cyclic projective planes

In this section we discuss cyclic projective planes and explore the relationship of difference sets to cyclic planes. We will use the theory of duadic codes to show the nonexistence of certain cyclic projective planes.

Recall that a projective plane on \(v\) points is cyclic if there is a \(v\)-cycle in its automorphism group. For example, the projective plane PG(2, \(q\)) of order \(q = \mu\) where \(q\) is a prime power, has an automorphism which is a \(v\)-cycle where \(v = \mu^2 + \mu + 1\); this is called a Singer cycle (see [4]). In general, a cyclic design is one that has a \(v\)-cycle in its automorphism group. By renaming the points, we may assume that the points are the cyclic group \(\mathbb{Z}_v\) under addition modulo \(v\) and that the cyclic automorphism is the map \(i \mapsto i + 1 \pmod v\). We make this assumption whenever we speak of cyclic designs. We begin with a theorem relating cyclic designs to duadic codes that will prove quite useful when we look at cyclic projective planes.

**Theorem 8.7.1** Let \(A\) be the incidence matrix of a cyclic symmetric 2-\((v, k, \lambda)\) design \((\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{B})\). Let \(p\) be the characteristic of \(\mathbb{F}_q\) and assume that \(p \nmid (k - \lambda)\) but \(p^2 \nmid (k - \lambda)\). Then the following hold: (i) If \(p \nmid k\), then \(C_q(A)\) is a self-orthogonal even-like duadic code of dimension \((v - 1)/2\) whose splitting is given by \(\mu_{-1}\). (ii) If \(p \nmid k\), then \(\mathcal{D}_q(A)\) is a self-orthogonal even-like duadic code of dimension \((v - 1)/2\) whose splitting is given by \(\mu_{-1}\). Also \(\mathcal{D}_q(A)^\perp = C_q(A)\).

**Proof.** As the rows of \(A\) are permuted by the map \(i \mapsto i + 1 \pmod v\), \(C_q(A)\) must be a cyclic code. As differences of rows of \(A\) are also permuted by this map, \(\mathcal{D}_q(A)\) is also a cyclic code. By Theorems 8.5.3 the dimensions and self-orthogonality of \(C_q(A)\) in (i) and \(\mathcal{D}_q(A)\) in (ii) are as claimed. The remainder of the result follows from Theorems 6.4.1 and 8.5.3.
