\(\mu_3 \in PAut(C_3(A))\) and thus \(\mu_3\) permutes the minimum weight codewords; these codewords are precisely those with supports the lines of the plane by Theorem 6.5.2 and are multiples of the binary vectors corresponding to these supports. Hence \(\mu_3 \in Aut(D)\). Because \(\mu_3\) fixes the coordinate 0 by Theorem 8.7.2, \(\mu_3\) fixes a line \(\ell\) and thus \(\mu_3\) fixes a minimum weight codeword \(e\) of \(C_3(A)\) with supp(e) = \(\ell\). Writing the codeword \(e\) as the polynomial \(c(x)\) in \(\mathcal{R}_v = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^v - 1)\), we have \(c(x) = c(x)\mu_3 = c(x)^3 = c(x)^3\) as \(\mathcal{R}_v\) has characteristic 3. Thus \(c(x)^3 = c(x)^3\) implying that \(e(x) = c(x)^2\) is an idempotent. Let \(C'\) be the cyclic code generated by \(e(x)\) and all of its cyclic shifts. As \(c(x)v(x) = c(x) = c(x)^3\), \(C'\) contains \(c(x)\), all of its cyclic shifts, and their scalar multiples. But these are all the minimum weight codewords of \(C_3(A)\) by Theorem 6.5.2, hence \(C' = C_3(A)\) implying that \(e(x)\) is the generating idempotent of \(C_3(A)\).

As \(e(x)\) and \(e(x)\mu_{-1}\) are the generating idempotents of an odd-like pair of duadic codes, by Exercise 329 \(e(x) + e(x)\mu_{-1} = 1 + \overline{J(x)}\). Since \(v \equiv 1 \pmod 3\) as \(3 \mid \mu\), \(\overline{J(x)} = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots + x^{v-1}\) and so

\(e(x) + e(x^{-1}) = 2 + x + x^2 + \cdots + x^{v+\mu}.\) (8.13)

We examine how each of the terms in \(e(x) = c(x)^2 = c(x)^2 = \sum_{m=1}^{v-1} e_m x^m\) can arise in the expansion of \(c(x)^2\). The term \(e_m x^m\) is the sum of terms of the form \(x^i x^j\) where \(i\) and \(j\) are elements of \(\ell\). Suppose that \(x^i x^j = x^{i'} x^{j'}\) with \(i \neq j'\). Then \(i - i' \equiv j' - j \pmod v\). Then \(i - i' \equiv j' - j \pmod v\) can be obtained only once because \(\ell\) is a \((v, k, 1)\) difference set by Theorem 8.7.5. Therefore \(e(x)\) has exactly \(\mu + 1\) terms \(x^{2i}\) coming from the product \(x^i x^i\) and \((\frac{\mu + 1}{2})\) terms \(2x^{i+j}\) coming from \(x^i x^j + x^j x^i\), where \(i \neq j\). Thus \(e(x)\) has exactly \(\mu + 1\) terms with coefficient 1 and \((\frac{\mu + 1}{2})\) terms with coefficient 2. The same holds for \(e(x)\mu_{-1}\). In order for (8.13) to hold, since the coefficient of \(x^0\) in both \(e(x)\) and \(e(x)\mu_{-1}\) must agree, the coefficient of \(x^0\) in both \(e(x)\) and \(e(x)\mu_{-1}\) must be 1. Furthermore, \(e(x)\) and \(e(x)\mu_{-1}\) must have exactly the same set of terms \(x^k\) with coefficients equal to 2; these terms when added in \(e(x) + e(x)\mu_{-1}\) have coefficient equal to 1. Finally, whenever the coefficient of \(x^k\) with \(k \neq 0\) in \(e(x)\) (or \(e(x)\mu_{-1}\)) is 1, then the coefficient of \(x^k\) in \(e(x)\mu_{-1}\) (respectively, \(e(x)\)) is 0. This accounts for all terms of \(e(x) + e(x)\mu_{-1}\), and so there is a total of \((\frac{\mu + 1}{2}) + 2(\mu + 1) - 1\) terms, which must therefore equal \(v = \mu^2 + \mu + 1\). Solving gives \(\mu^2 - 3\mu = 0\); thus \(\mu = 3\).
