
Proof: By Lemma 6.3.1, we may assume that \( n = p^m \), where \( p \) is an odd prime. We first show that if a splitting of \( n = p^m \) exists, then \( q \) is a square modulo \( n \). Let \( U \) be the group of units in \( \mathbb{Z}_n \). By Lemma 6.2.1 this group is cyclic of order \( \phi(p^m) = (p - 1)p^{m-1} \), which is even as \( p \) is odd. Since \( q \) is relatively prime to \( n \), \( q \in U \). Let \( R \) be the subgroup of \( U \) consisting of the squares in \( U \). We only need to show that \( q \in R \). If \( q \) generates \( U \), then \( u^2 \) generates \( R \). As \( U \) has even order, \( R \) has index 2 in \( U \). Since \( q \in U \), we define \( Q \) to be the subgroup of \( U \) generated by \( q \). Notice that if \( a \in U \), then \( aq \in U \) and hence \( U \) is a union of \( q \)-cyclotomic cosets modulo \( n \); in fact, the \( q \)-cyclotomic cosets contained in \( U \) are precisely the cosets of \( Q \) in \( U \). The number of \( q \)-cyclotomic cosets in \( U \) is then the index \( [U : Q] \) of \( Q \) in \( U \). Let \( S_1 \) and \( S_2 \) form a splitting of \( n \) given by \( \mu_q \). Each \( q \)-cyclotomic coset of \( U \) is in precisely one \( S_i \), as \( U \subseteq S_1 \cup S_2 \) and \( S_1 \cap S_2 = \emptyset \). Because \( b \) and \( n \) are relatively prime, \( b \in U \) and so \( U \mu_q = U \) implying that \( (U \cup S_1)\mu_q = U \cup S_2 \). In particular, this says that \( U \) has an even number of \( q \)-cyclotomic cosets. Thus \( [U : Q] \) is even; as \( [U : R] = 2 \) and \( U \) is cyclic, \( Q \subseteq R \). Thus \( q \in R \) as desired.

Now assume that \( q \) is a square modulo \( n \). We show how to construct a splitting of \( n \). For \( 1 \leq t \leq m \), let \( U_t \) be the group of units in \( \mathbb{Z}_{p^t} \). Let \( R_t \) be the subgroup of \( U_t \) consisting of the squares of elements in \( U_t \) and let \( Q_t \) be the subgroup of \( U_t \) generated by \( q \). As in the previous paragraph, \( U_t \) is cyclic of even order, and \( R_t \) has index 2 in \( U_t \). As \( q \) is a square modulo \( p^m \), then \( q \) is a square modulo \( p^t \) implying that \( Q_t \subseteq R_t \). Finally, \( U_t \) is a union of \( q \)-cyclotomic cosets modulo \( p^t \) and these are precisely the cosets of \( Q_t \) in \( U_t \). The nonzero elements of \( \mathbb{Z}_n \) are the set

\[
\bigcup_{t=1}^m p^{m-t} U_t. \quad (6.10)
\]

We are now ready to construct the splitting of \( n \). Since \( U_t \) is cyclic and \( Q_t \subseteq R_t \subseteq U_t \), with \( |U_t : R_t| = 2 \), there is a unique subgroup \( K_t \) of \( U_t \) containing \( Q_t \) such that \( [K_t : Q_t] = 2 \). Note that \( U_t, R_t, Q_t \), and \( K_t \) can be obtained from \( U_m, Q_m \), and \( K_m \) by reducing the latter modulo \( p^t \). Let \( b \in K_m \setminus Q_m \). Then \( K_m = Q_m \cup bQ_m \), and hence, by reducing modulo \( p^t \), \( K_t = Q_t \cup bQ_t \), for \( 1 \leq t \leq m \). Also, \( b^2 \in Q_t \) modulo \( p^t \). Let \( g_1^{(t)}, g_2^{(t)}, \dots, g_{|S_t|}^{(t)} \) be distinct coset representatives of \( K_t \) in \( U_t \). Then the \( q \)-cyclotomic cosets modulo \( p^t \) in \( U_t \) are precisely the cosets \( g_1^{(t)} Q_t, g_2^{(t)} Q_t, \dots, g_{|S_t|}^{(t)} Q_t, b g_1^{(t)} Q_t, \dots, b g_{|S_t|}^{(t)} Q_t \). Let \( S_{1,t} = g_1^{(t)} Q_t \cup g_2^{(t)} Q_t \cup \dots \cup g_{|S_t|}^{(t)} Q_t \) and \( S_{2,t} = b g_1^{(t)} Q_t \cup b g_2^{(t)} Q_t \cup \dots \cup b g_{|S_t|}^{(t)} Q_t \). Then \( S_{1,t} \mu_q = S_{2,t} \), \( S_{1,t} \cup S_{2,t} = U_t \), \( S_{1,t} \cap S_{2,t} = \emptyset \). Note that \( p^{m-t} g_i^{(t)} \) and \( p^{m-t} b g_i^{(t)} Q_t \) are \( q \)-cyclotomic cosets modulo \( p^m \). Thus by (6.10).

We can now give necessary and sufficient conditions on the length \( n \) for the existence of binary, ternary, and quaternary duadic codes. Recall from Theorem 6.13(iii) that \( n \) must be odd.

### Theorem 6.6.33
Let \( n = p_1^{\alpha_1} p_2^{\alpha_2} \dots p_r^{\alpha_r} \) where \( p_1, p_2, \dots, p_r \) are distinct odd primes. The following assertions hold:

- (i) Duadic codes of length \( n \) over \( \mathbb{F}_2 \) exist if and only if \( p_i \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8 \) for \( 1 \le i \le r \).
- (ii) Duadic codes of length \( n \) over \( \mathbb{F}_3 \) exist if and only if \( p_i \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{12} \) for \( 1 \le i \le r \).
- (iii) Duadic codes of length \( n \) over \( \mathbb{F}_4 \) exist for all (odd) \( n \).

**Proof:** Duadic codes of length \( n \) over \( \mathbb{F}_q \) exist if and only if \( q \) is a square modulo \( n \) by Theorem 6.3.2. By Lemma 6.3.1, \( q \) is a square modulo \( n \) if and only if \( q \) is a square modulo \( p_i^{r_i} \) for \( 1 \le i \le r \). By Lemma 6.2.3, \( q \) is a square modulo \( p_i^{r_i} \) if and only if \( q \) is a square modulo \( p_i \). Part (i) now follows from Lemma 6.2.5 and (ii) from Lemma 6.2.9. Finally, part (iii) follows from the simple fact that \( 4 = 2^2 \) is always a square modulo \( n \).

In the binary case, there are results that count the number of duadic codes of prime length \( n=p \). By Theorem 6.3.3, \( p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8 \). Let \( e = (p-1)/\operatorname{ord}_p(2) \). Then it can be shown [67] that the number of duadic codes of length \( p \) depends only on \( e \). Further, the number of inequivalent duadic codes also depends only on \( e \). If \( e \) is odd, the number of pairs of odd-like (or even-like) binary duadic codes is \( 2^{e-1} \). When \( e \) is even, there is a more complicated bound for this number. For example, if \( p=31 \), then \( \operatorname{ord}_p(2)=5 \) and so \( e=3 \). There are four pairs of odd-like (or even-like) duadic codes. One pair is a pair of quadratic residue codes; the other three pairs consist of six equivalent codes (see Example 6.4.8). These facts hold for any length \( p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8 \) for which \( e=3 \), such as \( p=223, 433, 439, 457, \text{and } 727 \).

## 6.4 Orthogonality of duadic codes

The multiplier \( \mu_{-1} \) plays a special role in determining the duals of duadic codes just as it does for duals of general cyclic codes; see Theorem 4.4.9. We first consider self-orthogonal codes.

### Theorem 6.4.1
Let \( \mathcal{C} \) be any \( [n, (n-1)/2] \) cyclic code over \( \mathbb{F}_q \). Then \( \mathcal{C} \) is self-orthogonal if and only if \( \mathcal{C} \) is an even-like duadic code whose splitting is given by \( \mu_{-1} \).

**Proof:** Suppose \( \mathcal{C}_1 = \mathcal{C} \) is self-orthogonal with idempotent generator \( e_1(x) \). Let \( \mathcal{C}_2 = (e_2(x)) \), where \( e_2(x) = e_1(x)\mu_{-1} \). Since \( \mathcal{C}_1 \) is self-orthogonal and \( \overline{J(x)} \) is not orthogonal to itself, \( \overline{J(x)} \notin C_1 \). Thus by Lemma 6.1.2(iii), \( \mathcal{C} \) is even-like, which implies that \( \overline{J(x)} \in C_1^\perp \). By Theorem 4.4.9, \( \overline{J(x)} \in C_1^\perp = (1 - e_1(x)\mu_{-1}) \). As \( C_1^\perp \) has dimension \( (n+1)/2 \) and \( C_1 \subset C_1^\perp \), we have \( C_1^\perp = C_1 + \overline{J(x)} \). Since \( e_1(\overline{J(x)}) = 0 \) by Lemma 6.1.2(d), it follows from Theorem 4.3.7 that \( C_1^\perp \) has generating idempotent \( e_1(x) + \overline{J(x)} = 1 - e_1(x)\mu_{-1} = 1 - e_2(x) \), giving (6.1). Since \( e_2(x) = e_1(x)\mu_{-1} \), \( e_1(x) = e_2(x)\mu_{-1}^{-1} \) yielding (6.3). Thus \( C_1 \) and \( C_2 \) are even-like duadic codes with splitting given by \( \mu_{-1} \). Conversely, let \( \mathcal{C} = C_1 = \langle e_1(x) \rangle \) be an even-like duadic code with splitting given by \( \mu_{-1} \). Then \( C_2 = \langle e_1(x)\mu_{-1} \rangle \) and \( C_1 \subset \mathcal{D}_1 = (1 - e_1(x)\mu_{-1}) = C_1^\perp \) by Theorems 6.1.3(vi) and 4.4.9 and (6.4).
