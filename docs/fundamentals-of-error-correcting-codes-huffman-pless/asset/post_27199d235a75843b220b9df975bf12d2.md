The number \(\lambda_1\) of blocks containing a given point is called the *replication number* and is sometimes denoted \(r\). A \(2-(v, k, \lambda)\) design is sometimes called a *balanced incomplete block design* or a \((b, v, r, k, \lambda)\) design. The values for \(b\) and \(r\) are determined from \(v, k\), and \(\lambda\) using Theorem 8.1.6 with \(t = 2\).

The fact that each \(\lambda_1\) is an integer implies certain constraints on the parameters \(t, v, k\), and \(\lambda\) in order for a \(t-(v, k, \lambda)\) design to exist. A main problem in the study of designs is to determine whether numbers \(t, v, k\), and \(\lambda\), for which the \(\lambda_i\) are all integers, are actually the parameters of a \(t-(v, k, \lambda)\) design. A secondary problem is to classify all designs with given parameters up to equivalence when such designs exist.

## 8.2 Intersection numbers

There are other integers associated with a $t$-design $(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{B})$ that describe certain intersection properties of the design. Let $I$ and $J$ be subsets of $\mathcal{P}$ where $I \cap J = \emptyset$. Suppose that $|I| = i$ and $|J| = j$. Denote the number of blocks in $\mathcal{B}$ that contain $I$ and are disjoint from $J$ by $\lambda_j^i$. The next theorem shows that $\lambda_j^i$ is independent of the choice of $I$ and $J$ provided $i + j \le t$. We will also see in this theorem that these numbers satisfy a recursion reminiscent of the one satisfied by binomial coefficients. These numbers are called *intersection numbers* for the design. Certain of the intersection numbers have specific meaning:

- $\lambda_0^j$ is the number $b$ of blocks,
- $\lambda^0 = \lambda_i$, and
- $\lambda_j^t$ is the number of blocks not intersecting a given set of points of size $j$.

### Theorem 8.2.1

Let $(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{B})$ be a $t-(v, k, \lambda)$ design. Let $I$ and $J$ be disjoint subsets of $\mathcal{P}$ of size $i$ and $j$, respectively. If $i + j \le t$, $\lambda_j^i$ is independent of $I$ and $J$, and for $j \ge 1$,

$\lambda_j^i = \lambda_j^{i-1} - \lambda_{j+1}^{i-1}$. (8.1)

Also

$\lambda_j^t = \lambda \frac{\binom{v-i-j}{k-i}}{\binom{v-t}{k-t}}$. (8.2)

### Proof:

We give an indication of the proof and ask the reader to give a formal proof in Exercise 440. Referring to Figure 8.1, the right-hand “edge” gives the entries $\lambda_0^i$, which are $\lambda_i$ by definition. By Theorem 8.1.3, $\lambda_i$ is independent of $I$ for all $i \le t$ and so the right-hand “edge” of the figure is uniquely determined.

Figure 8.1 Pascal triangle of a $t-(v, k, \lambda)$ design.

Let \( I = \{p_1\} \). All \( \lambda_0^0 \) blocks either contain \( p_1 \), or they do not. The number containing \( p_1 \) is \( \lambda_1^0 \); thus the number not containing \( p_1 \) is \( \lambda_0^0 - \lambda_1^0 \). Hence \( \lambda_1^0 = \lambda_0^0 - \lambda_1^0 \) is independent of \( I \). So the second row of the triangle in Figure 8.1 is uniquely determined. Now consider the third row. Let \( I = \{p_1\} \) and \( J = \{q_1\} \) with \( p_1 \neq q_1 \). The intersection number \( \lambda_1^0 \) is the number of blocks containing \( p_1 \). These blocks fall into two categories: those that contain \( q_1 \) and those that do not. The number containing \( q_1 \) is \( \lambda_2^0 \), hence the number not containing \( q_1 \) is \( \lambda_1^0 - \lambda_2^0 \). Thus the entry \( \lambda_1^1 \) is \( \lambda_0^0 - \lambda_2^0 \), which is therefore independent of both \( I \) and \( J \). Now let \( J = \{q_1, q_2\} \). There are \( \lambda_1^0 \) blocks that do not contain \( q_2 \); as this entry is in row 2, it is independent of \( q_2 \). Again these blocks either contain \( q_1 \) or they do not. There are \( \lambda_1^1 \) that do, independent of \( q_1 \), and again \( \lambda_0^0 \) must be \( \lambda_0^0 - \lambda_1^1 \) independent of \( J \). Thus three rows of Figure 8.1 is uniquely determined and has the values claimed. The formal proof of (8.1) follows inductively along similar lines.

Using the recurrence relation (8.1), we can determine all \( \lambda_i^t \) with \( i + j \le t \) from the \( \lambda_i^0 \) with \( 0 \le i \le t \). For example,

\(\lambda_2^0 = \lambda_1^0 - \lambda_1^1 = \lambda_0^0 - \lambda_1^0 - (\lambda_0^0 - \lambda_2^0) = \lambda_0^0 - 2\lambda_1^0 + \lambda_2^0 = \lambda_0 - 2\lambda_1 + \lambda_2.\)

Since the value for \( \lambda_0^0 \) given in (8.2) agrees with the value for \( \lambda_i = \lambda_i^0 \) given in Theorem 8.1.3, and since the values of \( \lambda_i^t \) given in (8.2) satisfy the recurrence relation (8.1), as Exercise 439 shows, the theorem follows.

As mentioned previously, (8.1) is reminiscent of the recurrence relation that defines the Pascal triangle of binomial coefficients: we call the family of intersection numbers \( \{\lambda_i^t \mid i + j \le t\} \) the *Pascal triangle* of \( (\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{B}) \). Figure 8.1 gives a visual representation of this triangle. Notice that the triangle is determined completely from either "edge", that is, from the values \( \lambda_0^t = \lambda_i \) for \( 0 \le i \le t \) or from the values \( \lambda_0^t \) for \( 0 \le j \le t \). By iterating (8.1), we obtain an explicit formula for the entries in the triangle from the values along the "right edge."
