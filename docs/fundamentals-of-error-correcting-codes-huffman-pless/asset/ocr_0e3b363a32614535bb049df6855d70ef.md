from each of the previous rows gives a matrix $A_2$ with

\( A_2 = \left[ \begin{array}{ccc} & & 0 \\ & H & \vdots \\ & & 0 \\ a_1 & \cdots & a_{v-1} & k \end{array} \right] \)

where \( H \) is a \( (v-1) \times (v-1) \) integer matrix and each \( a_i \) is an integer. Furthermore adding a row, or column, of a matrix to another row, or column, does not affect the determinant. Hence \( \det(A) = k \det(H) \). By the theory of invariant factors of integer matrices (see for example, [187, Appendix C]), there are \( (v-1) \times (v-1) \) integer matrices \( U \) and \( V \) of determinant \( \pm 1 \) such that \( UHV = \text{diag}(h_1, \dots, h_{v-1}) \), where \( h_1, \dots, h_{v-1} \) are integers satisfying \( h_i | h_{i+1} \) for \( 1 \le i < v-1 \). Hence,

\( \det(A) = k \det(H) = k h_1 h_2 \cdots h_{v-1} = \pm (k - \lambda)^{(v-1)/2} \).

Furthermore, the rank \( r \) of \( A \) over \( \mathbb{F}_p \), which is the dimension of \( \mathcal{C}_q(A) \), is the number of values among \( (k, h_1, \dots, h_{v-1}) \) that are not zero modulo \( p \). As \( p | (k - \lambda) \) but \( p^2 \nmid (k - \lambda) \), at most \( (v-1)/2 \) of the \( h_i \)s are \( 0 \) modulo \( p \). Hence the dimension of \( \mathcal{C}_q(A) \) is at least \( (v-1)/2 \) if \( p | k \) and at least \( (v-1)/2 + 1 = (v+1)/2 \) if \( p \nmid k \). Thus if \( p | k \), by Theorem 8.5.2(iii), \( \mathcal{C}_q(A) \) has dimension \( (v-1)/2 \) as \( v \) is odd, giving (i). If \( p \nmid k \), \( \mathcal{D}_q(A) \) has codimension 1 in \( \mathcal{C}_q(A) \) and dimension less than \( v/2 \) by Theorem 8.5.2(iv), implying that \( \mathcal{C}_q(A) \) must have dimension \( (v+1)/2 \). This gives part (ii), with the rest now following from Theorem 8.5.2.

The condition that \( p | (k - \lambda) \) but \( p^2 \nmid (k - \lambda) \) is crucial to the theorem, as can be seen in the proof. When \( p^2 | (k - \lambda) \), the code \( \mathcal{C}_q(A) \) may have smaller dimension, as we will discover in the next section.
