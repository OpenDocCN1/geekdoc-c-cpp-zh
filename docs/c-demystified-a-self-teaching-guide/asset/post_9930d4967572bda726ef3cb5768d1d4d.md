You may legitimately say why 65365 in decimal as an unsigned short data type requires only four bytes of storage. Or when words, if you specify instead of short the data type, four bytes of storage will be reserved, even if you could store the number in less bytes. The reason is that it is not known, when memory is reserved, what value will be stored there. Additionally, the value could change. Accordingly, enough bytes of storage are reserved for the maximum possible value of that data type.

## Why Use a Smaller Size Type?

Given an int can store a wider range of numbers than a short, you may also be wondering why you would use a short rather than an int. The answer is that the wider range of int comes at a price; it requires twice as much RAM as—four instead of two bytes.

However, computers these days come with megabytes of RAM, megabyte being 1,048,576 bytes; you still may wonder why you should care about nearly extra bytes. If it was just 2 extra bytes, you wouldn't care. However, if you are writing a program for an insurance company that has one million customers, you won't be talking about 2 extra bytes, but 2 million extra bytes. Therefore, you should not justively choose the largest data type.

All this said, as a general rule, of the six number data types, you most often will use int. However, it is good to know the other choices.

## Floating-Point Data Types

I was nearsighted early life until I had lasik surgery on my eyes. In this surgery, the eye surgeon programs the laser to remove my eyeball by shaving off thin slices of cornea, measuring only thousands of an inch, in certain areas of my eyeball, leaving untouched areas, again only thousands of an inch away.

Can imagine my reaction if the eye surgeon had told his philosophy was "close enough for work," he was using only numbers, ignoring any values to the right of the decimal point? You would next have seen your silhouette through the wall after I ran to escape. (Since I still go to eye surgeon, who, by the way, earned his way through as a computer programmer, and it is not in my best interest to get on his bad side, let me admit that he was very precise and the surgery was successful.)

Whole numbers work for certain information fractions don't apply. For example, who would say they have 2 % children? Whole numbers also work for certain information where fractions apply but are not important. For you be sufficient normally to say the location is 98 miles away; precision such as 98.177 miles usually is not necessary.

However, other times fractions, expressed as numbers to the right of the decimal point, are very important. My Lasik surgery is an extreme example, but there are many other common ones. If you had a 3.9 GPA, you would probably not want the school to just forget about the .9 and say your GPA was 3. Similarly, a bank that keeps track of dollars not but cents and deposits would, with millions of transactions now, have inaccurate information as to how much money it has, and depositors have.

Accordingly, there are floating-point data types that you can use when a value to the right of the decimal point is important. The term floating point comes from the fact that there are no fixed digits before and after the decimal point; that is, the decimal point can float. Floating-point numbers are also referred to as reals.

## Scientific and E Notations

In scientific notation, the number before the multiplication operator, called the mantissa, is expressed as having a single digit to the left of the decimal point, and as many digits as the right of the decimal point. The number after the multiplication operator is a power of 10, which may be positive for large numbers or negative for very small.

E notation is very similar to scientific notation. The only difference is the multiplication operator, followed by 10 and an exponent, is replaced by an E.

### Table 2-4: Scientific and E Representations of Point Values

| Decimal Notation | Scientific Notation | E Notation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 123.45 | 1.2345 x 10² | 1.2345E2 |
| 0.0051 | 5.1 x 10⁻³ | 5.1E-3 |
| 1,200,000 | 1.2 x 10⁶ | 1.2E6 |

## Storage of Floating-Point Numbers

Since only ones and zeros can be stored in memory, complex numbers, well beyond the scope of this book, are required to store floating-point numbers. Even with complex codes, a computer can only approximate floating-point values. Indeed, in certain programs the programmer has to take care to ensure that such discrepancies in each do not accumulate to the point where the final result is wrong.

## Text Data Types

There are two data types. The first is char, which stands for character. It is usually 1 byte, and can represent any single character, including a letter, a digit, a punctuation mark, or a space.

The second data type is string. The string data type may store a number of characters, including this sentence, paragraph, or page. The number of bytes required depends on the number of characters.
