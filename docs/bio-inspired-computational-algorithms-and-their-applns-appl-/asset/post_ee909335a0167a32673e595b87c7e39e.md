## 2. Methodology

The forecasting process consists of calculating or predicting the value of some event that is going to happen in the future. To adequately realize this process, it is necessary to analyze the event data in question and build a model that allows the incorporation of the behavior patterns that have occurred in the past under the assumption that they can happen again in the future. It is important to note that there is no interest in explaining how the mechanism that produces the events works, but to predict their behavior.

The TS models are used for studying the behavior of data that varies with time. The data can be interpreted as measurements of some value (observable variable) of a phenomenon, realized at equal and consecutive time intervals. There are several methods to construct TS models and an overview of the most important can be found in (Weigend & Gershenfeld, 1994). In (Palit & Popovic, 2005) an overview is shown of the methodologies most used in the area of computational intelligence. One of the most used methods is based on considering the TS as a realization of a stochastic process. This approach is the basis of statistical treatment of TS that can be found in (Box & Jenkins, 1976) and (Guerrero, 2003). Nowadays the construction of models for TS is an area of great development as evidenced by the articles of the Journal of Time Series Analysis (http://www.wiley.com/bw/journal.asp?ref=0143-9782&site=1) in addition to the papers presented in international competitions on time series modelling such as NN3. Nevertheless, the existence of GA papers in which TS are used (Alberto et al., 2010; Battaglia & Protopapas, 2011; Chiogna & Gaetan & Masarotto, 2008; Hansen et al., 1999; Mateo & Sović & Gadea, 2010; Szpiro, 1997; Yadavalli et al., 1999), it is important to note that no reference was found to the use of SAGA for this purpose.

The data will be represented by $\{Z_t\}$ with the implicit assumption that $t$ takes the values $1,2,\ldots,N$ where the parameter $N$ indicates up to what moment the information is available. When a model for the data set is available, then values for the TS can be estimated, which are denoted by $\{\hat{Y}_t\}$. In addition, in order to consider a model as a good one, it is required that the values of $\{\hat{Y}_t\}$ be 'similar' to those of $\{Z_t\}$. The main purpose of this work is to build linear models for the data set to have good estimates of the $K$ unknown values of the phenomenon being studied in the moments $N + 1, N + 2, \ldots, N + K$.

In the forecasting subject, when a TS with these $N + K$ data is available, the set of the first $N$ is called the training set, and is used to construct the model of the series and realize the estimation of its parameters. The set of the last $K$ terms in the series is called the test set, and is used for the comparison of different models to choose the most suitable. Especially, this work is interested in building automated Autoregressive models of order $p$ (AR($p$)). For the TS, the expressions are of the form:

$$Z_t = \delta + \phi_1 Z_{t-1} + \phi_2 Z_{t-2} + \ldots + \phi_p Z_{t-p} + a_t \qquad (1)$$

Where $Z_t$ is the observable variable in question, $\delta$ and $\phi_i$ are the parameters to be determined and the variable $a_t$ represents a random variable of noise called *residual*. The expression (1) means that to predict what will happen at the time $t$ the $p$ values previous to $t$ are required; these values are called *delays* or *lags*.

In the classic theory of linear models, the restriction is set that $a_t$ represents a white noise, but in this work this restriction was not included, which will allow finding AR expressions for the residuals with which it will be possible to increase the accuracy of the models.

The interest in this type of models is originated in the fact that they represent the most important information about the behavior of the series eliminating the noise that may appear. It should also be added that, for these models, it is important that in the expression (1) only a number of terms set in advance appears. This will allow finding models for a TS, controlling the accuracy of the approximation of the same series according to the number of terms utilized.

**Problem 1:** If $\{Z_t\}$ is the original TS and $\{F_t\}$ is the forecasting obtained in the form

$$F_t = \delta + \phi_1 Z_{t-1} + \phi_2 Z_{t-2} + \ldots + \phi_p Z_{t-p} \qquad (2)$$

with $t > p$.

It is necessary to find the values for $\delta$ and $\phi_i$ that minimize the function:

$$\sqrt{\sum_{i=p}^{N}(Z_i-F_i)^2} \qquad (3)$$

This function will be called *Root of the Sum of Squares* (RSS). It is necessary to add that for rapidity in calculation it is preferable to use the square of this function obtaining the same results.

In this initial setting out, the construction of the model is presented as if a linear interpolation problem was solved, and given that the values for $\delta$ and $\phi_i$ will not be arbitrary but will be located in certain intervals, methods are necessary to solve Problem 1, working additionally with bounded variables.

The RSS function can have multiple local optima, and to solve this problem an original version of SAGA algorithms was developed, which allows solving real nonlinear optimization problems with bounded variables. The selection of a self-adaptive version was carried out because it is desired to automate as much as possible the process of building these models.

## 3. Self-Adaptive Genetic Algorithms

The SAGA algorithms were developed by Thomas Back (Back, 1992a, 1992b) and have the characteristic that they alone look for the best parameters for their operation. In them, the parameters that will be self-adaptive are encoded in the representation of the individual, for which they are altered by the actions of the genetic operators. With this, the best values of these parameters will produce better individuals, which have a greater probability of surviving, and in consequence, will spread towards the whole population the best values of the parameters. There are several versions of SAGA that differ especially in the parameters that will be adjusted automatically (Eiben et al., 1999). In the case of this work, four self-adaptive parameters are used: individual probability of crossing $p_c$, repetition of crossing $r_c$, individual probability of mutation $p_m$ and repetition of mutation $r_m$ as presented in (4). The selection of these parameters and their values is based on the idea that genetic operations of crossing and mutation can be multiple, but they cannot have very large values. A binary version of this algorithm already had been used by one of the authors of this work in other problems (Flores, 1999; Garduño, 2000; Garduño, 2001; Sanchez, 2004), and this, as well as the presented one here (with the representation of real numbers) according to the literature reviewed, are original to him.

The individuals for these problems will be proposed as solutions to them, and in addition will have four more components, where the values of: individual probability of crossing $p_c$, repetition of crossing $r_c$, individual probability of mutation $p_m$ and repetition of mutation $r_m$ will be represented. This section of the individual is called the section of the self-adaptive parameters, and with this, our entire individual is represented by:

$$(\delta, \phi_1, \phi_2, \ldots, \phi_p, p_c, r_c, p_m, r_m) \qquad (4)$$

The above mentioned is necessary, because in this model, the probability of crossing and mutation will be characteristic of each individual (not of the population as is traditional in the GA), and in addition it is considered that crossing and mutation can be multiple, that is to say, to operate several times at the same time. Multiple crossing and mutation are repetitions of the crossing and mutation used in the GA, when individuals represented by vectors of real components are used. The way of operating with these parameters is similar to that presented in (Back, 1992a, 1992b).

The limits that were used in the code of this work for the self-adaptive parameters are: individual probability of crossing $p_c$ that changes in the interval (0.5, 0.95), repetition of crossing $r_c$ in (1.0, 4.0), which means that crossing can only occur from one to three times; individual probability of mutation $p_m$ that varies in (0.5, 0.85) and repetition of mutation $r_m$ in (1.0, 5.0), which means that it is possible to mutate from one to four times. The limits of these self-adaptive parameters were chosen on the basis of the experience of other works (Flores, 1999; Garduño, 2000; Garduño, 2001; Sanchez, 2004), where they proved to give good results.

Later, the procedures of crossing and mutation are detailed.

### 3.1 Crossing and Mutation

Given two individuals, the crossing is realized taking as probability of crossing the average of the values of the individual crossings. Once it has been decided whether the individuals cross, the integer part of the average individual crossing is taken, and that is the number of times they cross. The crossing of two individuals consists of exchanging the coordinates of both vectors from a certain coordinate chosen at random. The multiple crossing is the result of applying this procedure several times to the same vectors.
