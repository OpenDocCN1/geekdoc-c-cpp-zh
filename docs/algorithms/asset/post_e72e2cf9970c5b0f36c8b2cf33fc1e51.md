
## 2.6 The fast Fourier transform

## 2.5 Coefficients of two polynomials, $A(x)$ and $B(x)$, of degree $d$

- **Input**: Their product $C = A \cdot B$
- **Selection**: Pick some points $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}$, where $n \geq 2d+1$
- **Evaluation**: Compute $A(x_0), A(x_1), \dots, A(x_{n-1})$ and $B(x_0), B(x_1), \dots, B(x_n)$
- **Multiplication**: Compute $C(x_k) = A(x_k)B(x_k)$ for all $k=0, \dots, n-1$
- **Interpolation**: Recover $C(x) = C_0 + C_1x + \dots + C_d x^{2d}$

The equivalence of the two polynomial representations makes it clear that this high-level approach is correct, but how efficient is it? Certainly the selection step and the $n$ multiplications are no trouble at all, just linear time.$^3$ But (leaving interpolation, about which we know less) how efficient? Evaluating a polynomial of degree $d \leq n$ at a single point takes $O(n)$ steps (Exercise 2.29), and so the baseline for $n$ points is $\Theta(n^2)$. We'll now see that the fast Fourier transform (FFT) is in $O(n \log n)$ time, for a particularly clever choice of $x_0, \dots, x_{n-1}$ in which the computations required by the individual points overlap with one another and can be shared.

### 2.6.2 Evaluation by divide-and-conquer

Here's an idea how to pick the $n$ points to evaluate a polynomial $A(x)$ of degree $\leq n-1$. If we choose these to be positive-negative pairs, that,

> $\pm x_0, \pm x_1, \dots, \pm x_{n/2-1}$.

then the computations required for $A(x_i)$ and $A(-x_i)$ overlap a lot, because the even powers of $x_i$ coincide with those of $-x_i$.

To investigate this, we split $A(x)$ into its odd and even powers, for instance

$3 + 4x + 6x^2 + 2x^3 + x^4 + 10x^5 = (3 + 6x^2 + x^4) + x(4 + 2x^2 + 10x^4)$.

The terms in parentheses are polynomials in $x^2$. More generally,

$A(x) = A_{even}(x^2) + x A_{odd}(x^2)$,

$^3$In a typical setting for polynomial multiplication, the coefficients of the polynomials are real numbers and, moreover, are small enough that basic arithmetic operations (adding and multiplying) take unit time. We will assume this to be the case without any loss of generality; in particular, the time bounds we obtain are easily adjustable to situations with larger numbers.

where $A_e(x)$, with the even-numbered coefficients, and $A_o(x)$, with the odd-numbered coefficients, are polynomials of degree $\leq n/2 - 1$ (assume for convenience $n$ is even). Given paired points $\pm x_i$, the calculations for $A(x_i)$ can be recycled toward computing $A(-x_i)$:

```
$A(x_i) = A_e(x_i^2) + x_i A_o(x_i^2)$
$A(-x_i) = A_e(x_i^2) - x_i A_o(x_i^2)$
```

In other words, evaluating $A(x)$ at $n$ points $\pm x_0, \dots, \pm x_{n/2-1}$ reduces to evaluating $A_e(x)$ and $A_o(x)$ (which have half the degree of $A(x)$) at $n/2$ points, $x_0, \dots, x_{n/2-1}$.

Evaluate:

| $A(x)$ degree $\leq n-1$ | at: | $+x_0$ $-x_0$ | $+x_1$ $-x_1$ | $\dots$ | $+x_{n/2-1}$ $-x_{n/2-1}$ |

Equivalently, evaluate:

| $A_e(x)$ and $A_o(x)$ degree $\leq n/2-1$ | at: | $x_0^2$ | $x_1^2$ | $\dots$ | $x_{n/2-1}^2$ |

The original problem of size $n$ is in this way recast as subproblems of size $n/2$

```
$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n)$
```

which is $O(n \log n)$, exactly what we want.

But we have a problem: the plus-minus trick only works at the top level of the recursion. To reach the next level, we need the $n/2$ evaluation points $x_0^2, \dots, x_{n/2-1}^2$ to be *themselves* plus-minus pairs. But how can a square be negative? The task seems impossible! Unless, of course, we use complex numbers.

Fine, but which complex numbers? To figure this out, we use “reverse engineer” of the process. At the very bottom of the recursion, we find a single point. This point might be as well as 1, in which case the level above must consist of its square roots, $\pm\sqrt{1} = \pm1$.

The next level up then has $\pm\sqrt{+1}=\pm1$ as well as the complex numbers $\sqrt{-1} = \pm i$, where $i$ is the imaginary unit. By continuing in this manner, we-actually reach the initial set of $n$ points. Perhaps you have already guessed what

![](img/3dd1f1364df8f87b80683fc32a15b999_72_0.png)

they are: the $n$ complex roots of unity, that is, the $n$ complex solutions to the equation $z^n=1$.

Figure 2.6 is a pictorial review of some basic facts about complex numbers. The third panel of figure introduces the $n$th roots of unity: the complex numbers $1, \omega, \omega^2, \dots, \omega^{n-1}$, where $\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}$. If $n$ is even,

1. The $n$th roots are plus-minus paired, $\omega^{n/2}=-\omega$.
2. Squaring them produces the $(n/2)$ roots of unity.

Therefore, if we start with these numbers for some $n$ that is a power of 2, then at successive levels of recursion we will have $(n/2^k)$ roots of unity, for $k=0, 1, 2, \dots$. All these sets of numbers are plus-minus paired, and so divide-and-conquer, as shown in the last panel, works perfectly. The resulting algorithm is the fast Fourier transform (Figure 2.7).

### 2.6.3 Interpolation

Let's take stock of where we are. We have developed a high-level scheme for multiplying polynomials (Figure 2.5), based on the observation that polynomials can be represented in two ways, terms of their *coefficients* or in terms of their *values* at selected sets of points.

| Coefficient representation | Value representation |
| :--- | :--- |
| $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{n-1}$ | $A(x_0), A(x_1), \dots, A(x_{n-1})$ |

The value representation makes it trivial to multiply polynomials, but we cannot ignore the coefficient representation since it is the form in which input and output of our overall algorithm specified. So we designed the FFT, a way to move from coefficients to values in time $O(n \log n)$, when the points $[x_i]$ are $n$th roots of unity $(1, \omega, \omega^2, \dots, \omega^{n-1})$.

$(values) = FFT((coefficients), \omega)$.

Solutions to the equation $z^n = 1$.
By the multiplication rule: $z = (1, \theta)$, for $\theta$ multiple of $2\pi/n$ (shown here for $n=6$). For even $n$:
- These numbers are **plus-minus paired**: $(-1, \theta) = (1, \theta+\pi)$.

## 2.7 The fast Fourier transform (polynomial formulation)

```
function FFT(A, \omega)
```

**Input:** Coefficient representation of a polynomial $A(x)$ of degree $\le n-1$, where $n$ is a power of 2, $\omega$, an $n$-th root of unity

**Output:** Value representation $A(\omega^0), \dots, A(\omega^{n-1})$

```
if $\omega=1$: return $A(1)$
express $A(x)$ in the form $A_k(x^2) + xA_k(x^2)$
call $FFT(A_k, \omega)$ to evaluate $A_k$ at even powers of $\omega$
call $FFT(A_k, \omega^2)$ to evaluate $A_k$ at odd powers of $\omega$
for $j=0$ to $n-1$:  compute $A(\omega^j) = A_k(\omega^{2j}) + \omega^j A_k(\omega^{2j})$
```

```
return $A(\omega^0), \dots, A(\omega^{n-1})$
```

The remaining piece of the puzzle is the inverse operation, interpolation. It will turn out, amazingly, that

```
$(\text{coefficients}) = \frac{1}{n}FFT(\text{values}, \omega^{-1})$
```

Interpolation is solved through the simplest most elegant way we could have hoped for—using the same FFT algorithm, but called with $\omega^{-1}$ in place of $\omega$! This might seem like a miraculous coincidence, but it will make a lot more sense when we recall our polynomial operations in the language of linear algebra. Meanwhile, our $O(n \log n)$ polynomial multiplication algorithm (Figure 2.5) is now fully specified.

## **A matrix reformulation**

To get a clearer view of interpolation, let's explicitly set down the relation between our two for a polynomial $A(x)$ of degree $\le n-1$. They are both vectors of $n$ numbers, and one is a linear transformation of the other:

```
$$\begin{bmatrix} A(X_0) \\ A(X_1) \\ \vdots \\ A(X_{n-1}) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & X_0 & \dots & X_0^{n-1} \\ 1 & X_1 & \dots & X_1^{n-1} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & X_{n-1} & \dots & X_{n-1}^{n-1} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_{n-1} \end{bmatrix}$$
```

The matrix in the middle $M$. Its specialized format—a Vandermonde matrix—gives it many remarkable properties, of which the following is particularly relevant to us.

If $X_0, \dots, X_{n-1}$ are distinct numbers, then $M$ is invertible.
