Proof. $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod N$ implies $N$ divides $(x^2 - 1) = (x+1)(x-1)$. But $N$ does not divide either of these individual terms, since $x 
eq \pm 1 \pmod N$. $N$ must have a nontrivial factor in common with each of $(x+1)$ and $(x-1)$. In particular, $\gcd(N, x+1)$ is a nontrivial factor of $N$.

Example. Let $N=15$. Then $4^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$, but $4 
eq \pm 1 \pmod 5$. Thus $\gcd(4-1, 15)=3$ and $\gcd(4+1, 15)=5$ are nontrivial factors of 15.

To complete the connection with periodicity, we need one further concept. Define the order of $x$ modulo $N$ to be the smallest integer $r$ such that $x^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$. For instance, the order of 2 modulo 15 is 4.

Computing the order of a *random* number $x \pmod N$ is closely related to the problem of finding nontrivial square roots, and to factoring. Here is the link.

+   **Lemma** Let $N$ be an odd composite, and $x$ be chosen uniformly at random between 0 and $N-1$. If $\gcd(x,N)=1$, then with probability 1/2, the order $r$ of $x$ modulo $N$ is even, and moreover $x^{r/2}$ is a nontrivial square root of 1 modulo $N$.

The proof of this lemma is an exercise. What it implies is that if we compute the order $r$ of a randomly chosen element $x \pmod N$, there's a chance that this order is even and $x^{r/2}$ is a nontrivial square root of 1 modulo $N$. In which case $\gcd(x^{r/2}-1, N)$ is a factor of $N$.

Example. If $x=2$ and $N=15$, then the order of 2 is 4 because $2^4 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$. Raising 2 to half power, we get a nontrivial root of 1: $2^2 = 4 
eq \pm 1 \pmod 5$. So we get a divisor of 15 by computing $\gcd(4+1, 15)=5$.

We have reduced FACTORING to the problem of ORDER FINDING. The advantage of this task is that it has a natural function associated with it: fix $x$, and consider the function $f(a)=x^a \pmod N$. If $r$ is the order, then $f(0)=f(r)=f(2r)=\dots=1$, and similarly, $f(1)=f(r+1)=f(2r+1)=\dots=x$. Thus $f$ is periodic, with period $r$. And we can compute it by the repeated squaring algorithm from Section 1.2. So to order $N$, all we need to do is figure out how to use the function $f$ to set up a periodic superposition with period $r$; whereupon we can use quantum Fourier sampling as-is described in the next box.
