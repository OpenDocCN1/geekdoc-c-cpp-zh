
Let's investigate the issue by describing what we expect of these three multipliers, call them $y_1, y_2, y_3$.

| Multiplier | Inequality |
| :--- | :--- |
| $y_1$ | $x_1 \le 200$ |
| $y_2$ | $x_2 \le 300$ |
| $y_3$ | $x_1 + x_2 \le 400$ |

To start, these $y_i$'s must be nonnegative, or otherwise they are unqualified to multiply inequalities (multiplying an inequality by a negative number would flip the $\le$ to $\ge$). After the multiplication and addition, we get the bound:

$$(y_1 + y_3)x_1 + (y_2 + y_3)x_2 \le 200y_1 + 300y_2$$

We want the left-hand side to look like our objective function $x_1 + 6x_2$ so the right-hand side is an upper bound on the optimum solution. For this we need $y_1 + y_3$ to be 1 and $y_2 + y_3$ to be 6. Come to think of it, we would find if $y_1 + y_2$ are larger than 1—the resulting certificate would be all the more convincing. Thus, we get an upper bound

$$x_1 + 6x_2 \le 200y_1 + 300y_2 + 400y_3 \quad \text{if} \begin{cases} y_1 + y_3 \ge 1 \\ y_2 + y_3 \ge 6 \end{cases}$$

We can easily find $y$'s that satisfy the inequalities on the right by simply making them large enough, $(y_1, y_2, y_3) = (5, 3, 6)$. But these particular multipliers would tell us that the optimum of the LP is at most $200\cdot5+300\cdot3+400\cdot6=4300$, which is far too loose to be of interest. What we want a bound that is as tight as possible, so we minimize $200y_1 + 300y_2 + 400y_3$ subject to the preceding inequalities. *And this is a new linear program*

Therefore, finding the set of multipliers that give the best upper bound on our original LP is tantamount to solving a new LP:

$$200y_1 + 300y_2 + 400y_3 \quad \text{subject to} \begin{cases} y_1 + y_3 \ge 1 \\ y_2 + y_3 \ge 6 \\ y_1, y_2, y_3 \ge 0 \end{cases}$$

By design, any feasible value of this dual LP is an upper bound on the original *primal*. If we somehow find a pair of primal and dual feasible values that are equal, then they must be optimal. Here is just such a pair:

$$Primal : (x_1, x_2) = (100, 300); \quad Dual : (y_1, y_2, y_3) = (0, 5, 1).$$

They both have value 1900, and therefore they certify each's optimality (Figure 7.9).

# Visualizing duality

One can solve the shortest-path problem by the following "analog" device: Given a weighted graph, build a physical model of it in which each edge is a string of length equal to the edge's weight, and each node is a knot at which the appropriate endpoints of strings are tied together. To find the shortest path from s to t, just pull away from t until the gadget is taut. It is intuitively clear that this finds the shortest path from s to t.

![](img/3dd1f1364df8f87b80683fc32a15b999_219_0.png)

There is nothing remarkable or surprising about this until we notice the following: the shortest-path problem is a minimization problem, right? Why are we pulling s from t, an act whose purpose is, obviously, maximization? Answer: By pulling s away from t we solve the dual of the shortest-path problem! This dual has a very simple form (Exercise 7.28), with one variable xi for each node u:

```
max xs - xi
|xu - xv| <= wuv for all edges [u, v].
```

In words, the dual problem is to stretch s and t as far apart as possible, subject to the constraint that the endpoints of any edge [u, v] are separated by a distance of most $w_{uv}$.

# 7.5 Zero-sum games

We can represent various conflict situations in life by matrix games. For example, the schoolyard rock-paper-scissors game is specified by the payoff matrix illustrated here. There are two players, called Row and Column, and each pick a move from $[r, p, s]$. They then look up the matrix entry corresponding to their moves, and Column pays Row's gain and Column's loss.

$G = \begin{array}{c|ccc} & r & p & s \\ \hline Row & 0 & -1 & 1 \\ p & 1 & 0 & -1 \\ s & -1 & 1 & 0 \end{array}$

Now suppose the two of them play this game repeatedly. If Row always makes the same move, Column will quickly catch on and will always play the counter, winning every time. Row might mix things up; we can model this by allowing Row to have a *mixed strategy*, in which each turn she plays $r$ with probability $x_1$, $p$ with probability $x_2$, and $s$ with probability $x_3$. This strategy is specified by the vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$, positive numbers that add up to 1. Similarly, Column's mixed strategy is $y=(y_1, y_2, y_3)$.

On any given round of the game, there is $x_iy_j$ chance that Row and Column will play the $i$-th and $j$-th moves, respectively. Therefore the expected (average) payoff is:

$$\sum_{i,j} G_{ij} \cdot \text{Row[Row plays $i$, Column plays $j$]} = \sum_{i,j} G_{ij}x_iy_j.$$

Row wants to *maximize*, while Column wants to *minimize*. What payoffs do they hope to achieve in rock-paper-scissors? Well, suppose for instance Row plays the "completely random" strategy $\mathbf{x} = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$. If Column plays $r$, then the average payoff (reading the first column of the matrix) will be

$$\frac{1}{3} \cdot 0 + \frac{1}{3} \cdot 1 + \frac{1}{3} \cdot -1 = 0.$$

This is also true if Column plays $p$, and the payoff of any mixed strategy $(y_1, y_2, y_3)$ is just a weighted average of the individual payoffs for $r, p$, and $s$, it must also be so. This can be seen directly from the preceding formula,

$$\sum_{i,j} G_{ij}x_iy_j = \sum_{i,j} G_{ij}y_j = \sum_{j} y_j(\sum_{i} G_{ij}) = \sum_{j} y_j \cdot 0 = 0,$$

where the second-to-last equality is the observation that every column of $G$ adds up to zero. By playing the "completely random" strategy, Row forces an expected payoff of zero, no matter what Column does. This means Column cannot hope for a negative (expected) payoff (remember that she wants the payoff to be as small as possible). But symmetrically, if Column plays the completely random strategy, she also forces an expected payoff of zero, and Row cannot hope for a positive (expected) payoff.
