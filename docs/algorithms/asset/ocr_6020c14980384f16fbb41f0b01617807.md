# 6.5 Chain matrix multiplication

As you can see, the order of multiplications makes a big difference in the final time! Moreover, the natural greedy approach, to always perform the cheapest matrix multiplication available, leads to the second parenthesization shown here and is therefore a failure.

How do we determine the optimal, if we want to compute $A_1 \times A_2 \times \dots \times A_n$ where $A_i$'s have dimensions $m_0 \times m_1, m_1 \times m_2, \dots, m_{n-1} \times m_n$, respectively? The first thing to notice is that a particular parenthesization can be represented very naturally by a binary tree in which the individual nodes are leaves, the root is the final product, and interior nodes are intermediate products (Figure 6.7). The possible orders in which to do the multiplication are the various full binary trees with $n$ leaves, whose number is exponential in $n$ (Exercise 2.13). We certainly cannot try each tree, and with brute force ruled out, we turn to dynamic programming.

The binary trees of Figure 6.7 suggest: for a tree to be optimal, its subtrees must also be optimal. What are the subproblems corresponding to the subtrees? They are the product of $A_i \times A_{i+1} \times \dots \times A_j$. Let's see if this works for: $1 \le i \le j$, define

```
$C(i, j) = \text{cost of multiplying } A_i \times A_{i+1} \times \dots \times A_j.$
```

The size of this subproblem is the number of matrix multiplications, $|j - i|$. The smallest subproblem is when $i = j$, in which case there's nothing to multiply, $C(i, i) = 0$. For $j > i$, consider the optimal subtree for $C(i, j)$. The first branch in this subtree, the one at the top, will split the product in two pieces, of the form $A_i \times \dots \times A_k$ and $A_{k+1} \times \dots \times A_j$, for some $k$ between $i$ and $j$.
