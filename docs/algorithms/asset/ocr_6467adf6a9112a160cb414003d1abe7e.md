Arithmetic operations on arbitrarily large numbers cannot possibly be performed in a single, constant-time step. We need to audit our earlier running time estimates and make them more honest.

We will see in Chapter 1 that the addition of two $n$-bit numbers takes time roughly proportional to $n$; this is not hard to understand if you think back to the gradeschool procedure for addition, which works on one digit at a time. $fib1$, which performs actually uses a number of basic steps roughly proportional to $fibn$; likewise, the number of steps taken by $fib2$ is proportional to $n^2$, still polynomial in $n$ and therefore exponentially superior to $fib1$. # 0.3 Big-O notation

We've just seen how looseniness in the analysis of running times can lead to an unacceptable level of inaccuracy in the result. The opposite danger is also present: it is possible to be precise. An insightful analysis is based on the right simplifications.

Expressing running time in terms of basic computer steps is already a simplification. After all, the time taken by each one step depends crucially on the particular processor and on details such as caching strategy (a result of which the running time can differ from one execution to the next). Accounting for these architecture-specific minutiae is a nightish task and yields a poor result; therefore makes more sense to see an cluttered, machine-independent characterization of an algorithm's efficiency. To this end, we will always express running time by counting the number of basic computer steps, as a function of the size of the input.

And this simplification leads to another. Instead of repeating the algorithm steps, $5n^2+4n+3$ steps on an input of size $n$, it is much simpler to leave out lower terms such as $4n$ and $3$ (which become insignificant as $n$ grows), and even the detail of the coefficient $c$ in the leading term (which will be five times faster in a few years nowadays), and just that the algorithm takes time $O(n^2)$ (pronounced "big oh of $n$").

It is time to define this notation precisely. In what follows, think of $f(n)$ and $g(n)$ as the running times of two algorithms on inputs of size $n$.

Let $f(n)$ and $g(n)$ be functions from positive integers to positive reals. We say $f=O(g)$ (which means that "$f$ grows faster than $g$") if there is a constant $c > 0$ such that $f(n) \le c \cdot g(n)$.
