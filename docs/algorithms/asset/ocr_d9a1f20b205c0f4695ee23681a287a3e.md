# # Chapter 9
Coping with NP-completeness

You are the junior member of a seasoned project team. Your current task is to write code for a simple-looking problem involving graphs and numbers. What are you supposed to do?

If you are very lucky, your problem will be among the half-dozen problems concerning graphs (shortest path, minimum spanning tree, maximum flow, (etc.), that we have solved in this book. Even if this is the case, recognizing a problem in its natural habitat—grungy and obscured by reality and context—requires practice and skill. It is more likely that you will need to reduce your problem—or solve using dynamic programming or linear programming.

But chances that nothing like this will happen. The world of search problems is a bleak landscape. There are a few spots of light—brilliant algorithmic ideas—each illuminating a small area around it (the problems that reduce to; two of these areas, linear and dynamic programming, are in decentably large). The remaining expanse is pitch dark: NP-complete. What are you to do?

You can start by proving that your problem is actually NP-complete. Often a proof by generalization (recall the discussion on page 256 and Exercise 8.10) is all you need; and sometimes a reduction from 3SAT or ZOE is not too difficult to find. This sounds like a theoretical exercise, but, if carried out successfully, it brings some tangible rewards: your status in the team has been elevated, and you are no longer the kid who can't do, and you have become the noble knight with the impossible quest.

But, unfortunately, a problem does not go away when proven NP-complete. The real question is, What do you do next?

This is the subject of the present chapter and also the inspiration for some of the most important modern research on algorithms and complexity. NP-completeness is not a death certificate—it is only the beginning of a fascinating adventure.
