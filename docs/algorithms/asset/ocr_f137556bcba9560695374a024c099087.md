There are cases. If the optimum value of $z_1 + \dots + z_m$ is zero, then all $z_i$'s are zero, and hence from the optimum vertex of the new LP we get a starting feasible vertex of the original LP, just by ignoring the $z_i$'s. We can at start simplex!

But what if the optimum objective turns out be positive? Let us think. We tried to minimize the $z_i$'s, but simplex decided that it cannot be zero. But this means that the linear region is infeasible: *it needs nonzero $z_i$* to become feasible. This is how simplex reports and that an LP is infeasible.

# **Degeneracy:**

In the polyhedron of 7.12 vertex $B$ is degenerate. Geometrically, this means that it is the intersection of more than $n = 3$ Faces of the polyhedron (in this case, ②, ③, ④, ⑤). Algebraically, it means if we choose any one of four sets of inequalities (②, ③, ④), (②, ③, ⑤), and (③, ④, ⑤) and solve the corresponding system of three linear equations in three unknowns, we get the same solution in all cases: (0, 300, 0). This is a serious problem: simplex may return a suboptimal degenerate vertex because all its neighbors are identical to it and thus has no better objective. And if we modify the simplex so that it detects degeneracy and continues to hop from vertex to vertex despite of any improvement in the cost, it may end up looping.

One way to fix this is by a *perturbation*: change $b$ by any tiny amount that doesn't change the element of the LP since the $\epsilon_i$'s are tiny, but it has the effect of differentiating the solutions of the linear systems. We can see why geometrically, that the four planes ②, ③, ④, ⑤ were jolted a little. Wouldn't vertex $B$ split into two vertices, very close to one another?

# **Unboundedness:**

In cases an LP is unbounded, in its objective function can be made arbitrarily large (or small, in minimization problem). If this is the case, simplex will discover: in exploring the neighborhood of a vertex, it will notice that adding an inequality leads to an underdetermined system of equations that has infinity of solutions. And in fact (this is an easy test) the space of solutions contains a whole line across which the objective become larger and larger, all the way to $\infty$. In this case simplex halts and completes.
