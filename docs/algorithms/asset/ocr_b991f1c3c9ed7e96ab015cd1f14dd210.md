# 2.2 Recurrence relations

Divide-and-conquer algorithms follow a generic pattern: they tackle a problem of size n by recursively solving, say, a subproblems of size n/b and then combining their answers in O(n^d) time, as (in the multiplication algorithm, a=3, b=2, and d=1). Their running time can be captured by the equation T(n) = aT(n/b) + O(n^d We derive a closed-form solution to this recurrence so that we no longer have to solve it explicitly in each new instance.

Master theorem² If T(n) = aT(n/b) + O(n^d) for some constants a > 0, b > 1, and d ≥ 0,

```
T(n) = \begin{cases} O(n^d) & d > log_b a \ O(n^d log_b a) & d = log_b a \ O(n^{log_b a}) & d < log_b a \end{cases}
```

This theorem tells us the running times of the divide-and-conquer procedures we are likely to use.

Proof. To prove the claim

Next, that the size of subproblems decreases by a factor of b with each level of recursion, and therefore reaches the base case after log n levels. The height of the recursion tree. Its branching factor is a, so the kth level of the tree is made up of a^k subproblems, each of size n/b^k (Figure 2.3). The total work done at this level is

```
a^k \times O(\frac{n^d}{b^{kd}}) = O(n^d) \times (\frac{a}{b^d})^k
```

As k goes from 0 (the root) to n (the leaves), these numbers form a geometric series with ratio a/b^d. Finding the sum of such a series in big-O notation is (Exercise 0.2), and comes to three cases.

+   1. The ratio is less than 1.
Then the series is decreasing, and its sum is just given by its first term, O(n^d).\2. The ratio is greater than 1.
The series is increasing and its sum is given by its last term, O(n^{log_b a}):

```
n^d (\frac{a}{b^d})^{log_b n} = n^d \frac{a^{log_b n}}{b^{d log_b n}} = a^{log_b n} = a^{log_b n^{log_b a}}
```

+   3. The ratio is exactly 1.
In this case O(log n) terms of the series are equal to O(n^d).

These cases translate directly into the three cases in the theorem statement.
