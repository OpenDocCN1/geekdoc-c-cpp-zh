## Chapter 9
## Coping with NP-completeness

You are the junior member of a seasoned project team. Your current task is to write code for a simple-looking problem involving graphs and numbers. What are you supposed to do?

If you are very lucky, your problem will be among the half-dozen problems concerning graphs (shortest path, minimum spanning tree, maximum flow, (etc.), that we have solved in this book. Even if this is the case, recognizing a problem in its natural habitat—grungy and obscured by reality and context—requires practice and skill. It is more likely that you will need to reduce your problem—or solve using dynamic programming or linear programming.

But chances that nothing like this will happen. The world of search problems is a bleak landscape. There are a few spots of light—brilliant algorithmic ideas—each illuminating a small area around it (the problems that reduce to; two of these areas, linear and dynamic programming, are in decentably large). The remaining expanse is pitch dark: NP-complete. What are you to do?

You can start by proving that your problem is actually NP-complete. Often a proof by generalization (recall the discussion on page 256 and Exercise 8.10) is all you need; and sometimes a reduction from 3SAT or ZOE is not too difficult to find. This sounds like a theoretical exercise, but, if carried out successfully, it brings some tangible rewards: your status in the team has been elevated, and you are no longer the kid who can't do, and you have become the noble knight with the impossible quest.

But, unfortunately, a problem does not go away when proven NP-complete. The real question is, What do you next?

This is the subject of the present chapter and also the inspiration for some of the most important modern research on complexity. NP-completeness is not a death—it is only the beginning of a fascinating adventure.

Your problem's NP-completeness probably constructs graphs that are complicated and weird, much like those that come up in application. For example, even though SAT is NP-complete, satisfying assignments for HORN SAT (the logic programming) can be found efficiently (recall Section 5.3). Or, suppose the graphs that arise in your application are trees. In this case, many NP-complete problems can be solved in linear time by dynamic programming (recall Section 6.7).

Unfortunately, this approach does not always work. We know that 3SAT is NP-complete, and INDEPENDENT SET, along with many other NP-complete problems, remains even for planar graphs (graphs that can be drawn in the plane without crossing edges). Moreover, you cannot neatly characterize the instances that come up in application. Instead, you will have to rely on some form of intelligent search—procedures such as branch and bound which are exponential in the worst case but with the right design, could be very efficient on typical instances. We discuss these methods in Section 9.1.

Or you can develop an algorithm for your NP-complete problem that falls short but never too much. For example, in Section 5.4 we saw that a greedy algorithm achieves a set cover that is no more than log times the optimal. An algorithm that achieves such a guarantee is called an approximation algorithm. As we see in Section 9.2, such algorithms are known for many NP-complete optimization problems, and they are some of the most clever and sophisticated algorithms. The theory of NP-completeness can be used as a guide in this endeavor, showing that, for some problems, there are limits to how well they can be approximated—unless P = NP.

Finally, there are heuristics with no guarantees on either the running time or degree of approximation. They rely on ingenuity, intuition, a good understanding of the problem, and often insights from physics to attack some common kinds in Section 9.3.

## 9.1 Intelligent exhaustive search

### 9.1.1 Backtracking

Backtracking is based on the observation that it is possible to reject a solution by looking at just a small portion of it. For example, if an instance of SAT contains the clause (x1 ∨ x2), then all assignments with x1 = x2 = 0 (i.e., false) can be instantly eliminated. To put it differently, by quickly checking and crediting the partial assignment, we are able to prune a quarter of the entire search space. A promising direction, but it can be systematically exploited?

Here's how it is done. Consider the Boolean function φ(w, x, z) specified by the set of clauses
```
(w ∨ x ∨ y ∨ z), (w ∨ x), (x ∨ y), (y ∨ z), (z ∨ w), (w ∨ z).
```
We will incrementally grow a tree of partial solutions. We start by branching on any variable, say $w$:

In this manner, backtracking explores the space of assignments, growing the tree only at nodes where there is uncertainty about the outcome, and stopping if any satisfying assignment is encountered.
