The existence of $M^{-1}$ allows to invert the preceding matrix equation so as to express coefficients in terms of values. In brief,

Evaluation is multiplication by $M$, while interpolation is multiplication by $M^{-1}$.

This reformulation of our polynomial operations reveals their essential nature more clearly. Among other things, it finally justifies an assumption we have been making throughout, that $A(x)$ is uniquely characterized by its values at any $n$ points—in fact, we now have an explicit formula that will give us the coefficients of $A(x)$ in this situation. Vandermonde matrices also have the distinction of being unique to invert over general matrices, in $O(n^2)$ instead of $O(n^3)$. However, using this for interpolation would still not be enough for us, so once again we turn to our special choice of points—the complex roots of unity.

## Interpolation resolved

In linear algebra terms, the FFT multiplies by an arbitrary $n$-dimensional vector—which we have been calling the $n \times n$ matrix

```
$$\begin{array}{ccccccc} 1 & 1 & 1 & & & & 1 \\ \omega & \omega^2 & & & \omega^{n-1} & & \\ \ & & \omega^2 & \omega^4 & & & \omega^{2(n-1)} & & \omega^2 \\ M_n(\omega) = & 1 & & \omega^j & & \omega^{(n-1)} & & \omega^1 & & & \vdots & & \vdots & & & \ & & & \omega^{(n-1)} & \omega^{2(n-1)} & & & \omega^{(n-1)(n-1)} & & \omega^{n-1} \end{array}$$
```

where $\omega$ is a complex $n$th root of unity, and $n$ is a power of 2. Notice how this matrix describes: its $(j, k)$ entry (starting row- and column-count at zero) is $\omega^{jk}$.

Multiplication by $M(\omega)$ maps the $k$th coordinate axis (the vector with zeros except for 1 at $k$ position) of $M$. Here's the crucial observation, which we'll prove shortly: the columns of $M$ are orthogonal (at right angles) to each other. They can then be thought of as axes of an alternative coordinate system, which is often called the Fourier basis. The effect of the Fourier basis, which is defined by the columns of $M$ is a change of basis, a *rigid rotation*. The inverse of $M$ is the opposite rotation, and when we write out the orthogonality precisely, we will be able to read off this transformation with ease:

## Inversion formula $M_n(\omega)^{-1} = \frac{1}{n}M_n(\omega^{-1})$.

$\omega^{-1}$ is an $n$th root of unity, and so interpolation—or equally, multi-multiplication by $M_n(\omega)^{-1}$—is itself just an FFT, but with $\omega$ replaced by $\omega^{-1}$.

Now let's get into the details. Take $\omega$ to be $e^{2\pi i/n}$ for convenience, and then the columns of $M$ as vectors in $\mathbb{C}$. Recall that the angle between the two vectors $u=(u_0, \dots, u_{n-1})$ and $v=(v_0, \dots, v_{n-1})$ in $\mathbb{C}$ is just a scaling factor times their inner product

For instance, points in direction $x_1$ get mapped into direction $f_1$.

![](img/3dd1f1364df8f87b80683fc32a15b999_76_0.png)

$u \cdot v^* = u_0v_0^* + u_1v_1^* + \dots + u_{n-1}v_{n-1}^*$.

where $z^*$ denotes the complex conjugate of $z$. This quantity is maximized when the vectors lie in the same direction and is zero when the vectors are orthogonal to each other.

The fundamental observation we note is the following.

**Lemma** The columns of matrix $M$ are orthogonal to each other.

Proof. Take the inner product of any columns $j$ and $k$ of matrix $M$,

$1 + \omega^{(j-k)} + \omega^{2(j-k)} + \dots + \omega^{(n-1)(j-k)}$.

This is a geometric series with first term 1, last term $\omega^{(n-1)(j-k)}$, and ratio $\omega^{(j-k)}$, which is 0—except when $j=k$, in which case all terms are 1 and the sum is $n$.

The orthogonality property can be summarized in the single equation

$MM^* = nl$.

$(MM^*)_{jk}$ is the inner product of the $j$th and $k$th columns of $M$ (do you see why?). This implies $M^{*-1} = (1/n)M^*$; we have an inversion formula! Is this the same formula we claimed? Let’s see—$(j,k)$th entry of $M^*$ is the complex conjugate of the corresponding entry of $M$, in other words $\omega^{-jk}$. Where $M^* = M_n(\omega^{-1})$, and we’re done.

And now we can finally step back and view the whole affair. The task we need to perform, polynomial multiplication, is a lot easier than the Fourier. The complex conjugate of a number $z=re^{i\theta}$ is $z^*=re^{-i\theta}$. The complex conjugate of a vector (or matrix) is obtained by taking the complex conjugates of all its entries. Then in the standard basis, rotate vectors within the basis (evaluation), then perform the task (multiplication), and finally rotate back (interpolation). The initial vectors are coefficient representations, while their rotated counterparts are value representations. To efficiently switch between these, is the province of the FFT.

## 2.6.4 A closer look at the fast Fourier transform

Now that our efficient scheme for polynomial multiplication is fully realized, lets hone in more closely on the core that makes it possible, the fast Fourier transform.

The definitive algorithm The FFT takes as input vector $a = (\tilde{a}_0, \dots, \tilde{a}_{n-1})$ and a complex number $\omega$ whose powers $1, \omega, \omega^2, \dots, \omega^{n-1}$ are the $n$th roots of unity. It multiplies vector $a$ by the $n \times n$ matrix $M(\omega)$, which has $(j, k)$ entry (starting row- and column-count at zero) $\omega^{jk}$. The potential for using divide-and-conquer in matrix multiplication becomes when $M(\omega)$ is segmented into even and odds:

![](img/3dd1f1364df8f87b80683fc32a15b999_77_0.png)

In the second step, we have simplified entries in the lower half of the matrix using $\omega^{n/2} = -1$ and $\omega^n = 1$. The top left and bottom right are $M_{n/2}(\omega^2)$. The top left and bottom right submatrices are same as $M_{n/2}(\omega^2)$, but with their rows multiplied by $\omega^j$ and $-\omega^j$, respectively. Therefore the final product is:

![](img/3dd1f1364df8f87b80683fc32a15b999_77_1.png)

In short, the product of $M_n(\omega)$ with vector $(\tilde{a}_0, \dots, \tilde{a}_{n-1})$, a size-$n$ problem, can be expressed in terms of two size-$n/2$ problems: the product of $M_{n/2}(\omega^2)$ with $(a_0, a_2, \dots, a_{n-2})$ and with $(a_1, a_3, \dots, a_{n-1})$. This divide-and-conquer strategy leads to the FFT algorithm of Figure 2.9, whose running time is $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n \log n)$.

## Figure 2.9 The fast Fourier transform

```
function FFT(a, w)
Input: An array a = (a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{n-1}), for $n$ a power of 2
A primitive $n$th root of unity, $\omega$
Output: M_n(\omega)
```

```
if \omega = 1: return a
(s_0, s_1, \dots, s_{n/2-1}) = FFT((a_0, a_2, \dots, a_{n-2}), \omega^2)
(s_0, s_1, \dots, s_{n/2-1}) = FFT((a_1, a_3, \dots, a_{n-1}), \omega^2)
for j = 0 to n/2 - 1:
r_j = s_j + \omega^j s'_j
r_{j+n/2} = s_j - \omega^j s'_j
return (r_0, r_1, \dots, r_{n-1})
```

## The fast Fourier transform unraveled

Throughout our discussions, the fast Fourier transform has remained tightly cocoon within a divide-and-conquer formalism. To fully expose its structure, we now unravel the recursion.

The divide-and-conquer step of the FFT can be drawn as a simple circuit. Here is how a problem of size $n$ is reduced to two subproblems of size $n/2$ (for clarity, one pair of outputs $(j, j + n/2)$ is singled out):

```
FFT_n (input: a_0, \dots, a_{n-1}, output: r_0, \dots, r_{n-1})
```

| a_0 |
| a_1 |
| :--- |
| FFT_{n/2} | r_j |
| a_{n-2} | |
| a_1 |
| a_3 |
| :--- |
| FFT_{n/2} | r_{j+n/2} |
| a_{n-1} | |

Using a particular shorthand: the edges are wiring complex numbers from left to right. A weight of j means "multiply the number on this wire by \omega^j." And when two wires come into a junction from the left, the numbers are carrying added up. The two outputs depicted are executing the commands

```
r_j = s_j + \omega^j s'_j
r_{j+n/2} = s_j - \omega^j s'_j
```
