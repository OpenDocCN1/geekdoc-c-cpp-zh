# Of mice and men

Our bodies are extraordinary machines: flexible in function, adaptive to new environments, and able to interact and reproduce. All these capabilities are specified by a program unique to each of us, a string that is 3 billion characters long the alphabet [A, C, G, T]—DNA.

The DNA sequences of any two people differ by only about 0.1%, however this still leaves 3 million positions on which vary, more than enough to explain the vast range of human diversity. These differences are of great scientific and medical interest—for instance, they might help predict which people are prone to certain diseases.

DNA is a vast and seemingly inscrutable program, but it can be broken down into smaller units that are precise in their role, like subroutines. These are called genes. Computers have a crucial tool in understanding the genes of humans and other organisms, to the extent that computational genomics is now a field in its own right. Here are examples of typical questions that arise:

+   1. When a new gene is discovered, one way to gain insight into its function is to find genes that match closely. This is particularly helpful in transferring knowledge from well-studied species, such as mice, to human beings.

A basic primitive in this search problem is to define an efficiently computable match of when strings approximately match. The biology suggests a generalization of edit distance, and dynamic programming can be used to compute it.

Then there's the problem of searching through the vast thick of known genes: the database GenBank already has a total length of over 10¹¹, and this number is growing rapidly. The current method of choice is BLAST, a clever combination of algorithmic tricks and biological intuitions that have made it the most widely used software in computational biology.

+   2. Methods for sequencing DNA (that is determining the string of characters that constitute it) typically only fragments of 500-700 characters. Billions of randomly scattered fragments can be generated, but how can they be assembled into a coherent DNA sequence? For one, the position of any fragment in the final sequence is unknown and may be inferred by piecing together overlapping fragments.

A centerpiece of these efforts is the draft of human DNA completed in 2001 by two groups: the publicly funded Human Genome Consortium and the private Celera Genomics.

+   3. When a particular gene has been sequenced in several places, can this information be used to reconstruct the evolutionary history of these species?

We explore these problems in the exercises at the end of this chapter. Dynamic programming has turned out to be an invaluable tool for some of them and for computational biology in general.
