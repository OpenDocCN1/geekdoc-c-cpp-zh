## Prologue

Look around you. Computers and networks are everywhere, enabling an intricate web of complex human activities: education, commerce, entertainment, research, manufacturing, health management, human communication, even war. Of the two main technological underpinnings of this amazing proliferation, one is obvious: the breathtaking pace with which advances in microelectronics and chip design have been bringing us faster and faster hardware.

This book tells the story of the other intellectual pieces that is crucially fueling the computer revolution: efficient algorithms. It is a fascinating story.

Gather 'round and listen close.

### 0.1 Books and algorithms

Two ideas changed the world. In 1448 in the German city of Mainz a goldsmith named Johann Gutenberg discovered a way to print books by putting together metallic pieces. Literacy spread, the Dark Ages ended, the human intellect was liberated, science and technology triumphed, the Industrial Revolution happened. Many historians say we owe all this to typography. Imagine a world in which only an elite could read these lines! But others insist that the key development was not typography, but *algorithms.*

### 0.2 Enter Fibonacci

Today we are so used to writing numbers in decimal, that it is easy to forget Gutenberg wrote the number 1448 as MCDXLVIII. How do you add two Roman numerals? What is MCDXLVIII + DCCXIII? (And just try to think about multiplying them.) Even a clever man like Gutenberg only knew how to add and subtract small numbers using his fingers; for anything more complicated had to consult an abacus specialist.

The decimal system, invented in India around AD 600, was a revolution in quantitative reasoning: using only 10 symbols, very large numbers could be written compactly, and arithmetic could be done efficiently on them. Nonetheless these ideas took a long time to spread, hindered by traditional barriers of language, distance, and ignorance. The most influential medium of transmission turned out to be a textbook, written in Arabic in the ninth century by a man who lived in Baghdad. Al Khwarizmi laid the basic methods for adding, multiplying, and dividing numbers—even squaring roots and calculating digits of $\pi$. These procedures were precise, unambiguous, mechanical, efficient, correct—in short, they were algorithms, a term coined to honor the wise man after the decimal system was finally adopted in Europe, many centuries later.

Since then, this decimal positional system and its numerical algorithms have played an enormous role in Western civilization. They enabled science and technology; they accelerated industry and commerce. And when, much later, the computer was finally designed, it explicitly embodied the positional system in its bits and words and arithmetic unit. Scientists everywhere got busy developing more and more complex algorithms for all kinds of problems and inventing new applications—ultimately changing the world.

Al Khwarizmi's work could not have gained a foothold in the West not for the efforts of one man: the 13th century Italian mathematician Leonardo Fibonacci, who saw the potential of the positional system and worked hard to develop it and propagate it.

But today Fibonacci is most known for his famous sequence of numbers

0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, \dots$,

each the sum of its immediate predecessors. More formally, the Fibonacci numbers $F_n$ are generated by the simple rule

```
$F_n = \begin{cases} F_{n-1} + F_{n-2} & \text{if } n > 1 \\ 1 & \text{if } n = 1 \\ 0 & \text{if } n = 0. \end{cases}$
```

## Algorithms 3

No other sequence of numbers has been studied extensively, or applied to more fields: biology, demography, art, architecture, music, to name just a few. And, together with the powers of 2, it is computer science's favorite sequence.

In fact, the Fibonacci numbers grow almost as the powers of 2: for example, $F_{30}$ is over a million, and $F_{100}$ is already 21 digits long! In general, $F_n \approx 2^{0.694n}$ (see Exercise 0.3).

But what is the precise value of $F_{100}$, or $F_{200}$? Fibonacci himself would wanted to know such things. To answer, we need an algorithm for computing the $n$th Fibonacci number.

### An exponential algorithm

One idea is to slavishly implement the recursive definition of $F_n$. Here is the resulting algorithm, in the “pseudocode” notation used throughout this book:

```
function fib1(n)
  if n = 0: return 0
  if n = 1: return 1
  return fib1(n-1) + fib1(n-2)
```

Whenever we have an algorithm, correct? there are three questions we always ask about it:

1. Is it correct?
2. How much time does it take, as a function of $n$?
3. And can we do better?

The first question is most here, as this algorithm is precisely Fibonacci’s definition of $F_n$. The second demands an answer. Let $T(n)$ be the number of computer steps needed to compute `fib1(n)`; what can we say about this function? For starters, if $n$ is less than 2, the procedure halts almost immediately, after just a couple of steps. Therefore,

$T(n) \le 2$ for $n \le 1$.

For large values of $n$, there are two recursive invocations of $fib1$, taking time $T(n-1)$ and $T(n-2)$, respectively, plus three computer steps (checks on the value of $n$ and a final addition).

$T(n) = T(n-1) + T(n-2) + 3 \text{ for } n > 1.$

Compare this to the recurrence relation for $F_n$: we immediately see that $T(n) \geq F_n$.

This is bad news: the timing of the algorithm grows as fast as the Fibonacci numbers $T(n)$ is exponential in $n$, which implies that the algorithm is practically slow except for very small values of $n$.

Let's be a little more concrete about exponential time. To compute $F_{200}$, the $fib1$ algorithm executes $T(200) \geq F_{200}$ elementary steps. How long this actually depends, of course, on the computer used. At this time, the fastest computer in the world is the NEC Earth Simulator, which clocks 40 trillion steps per second. Even on this, $fib1(200)$ would take at least $2^{92}$ seconds. This means that, if we start the computation today, it would still be long after the sun turns into a red giant.

But technology is rapidly improving—computer speeds doubling every 18 months, a phenomenon called Moore's law. With this extraordinary growth, $fib1$ will run a lot faster on next year's machines. Let's see the time of $fib(n)$ is proportional $\approx (1.6)^n$, so it takes 1.6 times longer to compute $F_{n+1}$ from $F_n$. Under Moore's law, computers get roughly 1.6 times faster each year. So if we can reasonably compute $F_{100}$ with this year's technology, next year we will manage $F_{101}$. And the year after, $F_{102}$. And the so on: one more Fibonacci number every time.

In short, our recursive algorithm is correct but hopelessly inefficient. Can we do better?

### A polynomial algorithm

Let's try to understand why $fib1$ is so slow. Show the cascade of recursive invocations triggered by a single call to $fib1(n)$.

A more sensible scheme would store the intermediate results—values $F_0, F_1, \dots, F_{n-1}$—as soon as they become known.

``` evenfunction fib2(n)
  if n = 0:
    return 0
  create an array f[0...n]
  f[0] = 0, f[1] = 1
  for i = 2..n:
    f[i] = f[i-1] + f[i-2]
  return f[n]
```
