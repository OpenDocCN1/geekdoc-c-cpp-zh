# 7.1.1 Example: profit maximization

A Boutique chocolatier has two products: its flagship assortment of truffle chocolates, called *Pyramid*, and the more decadent and deluxe Pyramid Nut. How much of each should it produce to maximize profits? Let's say it makes $x_1$ boxes of Pyramid per day, at a profit of $1 each, and $x_2$ boxes of Nut, at a more substantial profit of $6 apiece; $x_1$ and $x_2$ are unknown values that we wish to determine. But this is not all; there are some constraints on $x_1$ and $x_2 \ge 0$. First, the daily demand for these exclusive chocolates is limited to at most 200 boxes of Pyramid and 300 boxes of Nut. Also, the current workforce can produce a total of at most 400 boxes of chocolate per day. What are the optimal levels of production?

We represent the situation by a *linear program*, as follows.

```
Objective function $\max x_1 + 6x_2$
Constraints $x_1 \le 200$
$x_2 \le 300$
$x_1 + x_2 \le 400$
$x_1, x_2 \ge 0$
```

A linear equation in $x_1$ and $x_2$ defines a line in the two-dimensional (2D) plane, and a linear inequality designates a *half-space*, the region on one side of the line. The set of all *feasible solutions* of this linear program, to the points $(x_1, x_2)$ which satisfy all constraints, is the intersection of five half-spaces. It is a convex polygon, shown in Figure 7.1.

We want to find the point in this polygon that the objective function—profit—is maximized. The points with a profit of $c$ dollars lie on the line $x_1 + 6x_2 = c$, which has a slope of $-1/6$ and is shown in Figure 7.1 for selected values of $c$.
