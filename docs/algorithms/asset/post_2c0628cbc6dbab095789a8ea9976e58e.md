Suppose Row announces that she will play the mixed strategy $x = (1/2, 1/2). What should Column do? Move m will incur an expected loss of 1/2, while t will incur an expected loss of 0. The best response of Column is the pure strategy $y = (0, 1)$.

More generally, once Row's strategy $x = (x_1, x_2)$ is fixed, there is a pure strategy that is optimal for Column: either $m$, with payoff $3x_1-2x_2$, or $t$, with payoff $-x_1+x_2$, whichever is smaller. After all, any mixed strategy $y$ is a weighted average of these two pure strategies and cannot beat the best of the two.

## 7.5 Zero-sum games

for fixed $x_1$ and $x_2$ the following are equivalent:

$$\max z$$
$$z = \min[3x_1 - 2x_2, -x_1 + x_2]$$
$$Z \le 3x_1 - 2x_2$$
$$Z \le -x_1 + x_2$$

And Row needs to choose $x_1$ and $x_2$ to maximize this $z$.

$$\max z$$
$$-3x_1 + 2x_2 + z \le 0$$
$$x_1 - x_2 + z \le 0$$
$$x_1 + x_2 \ge 0$$

Symmetrically, if Column has to announce her strategy first, her best is to choose the mixed strategy that minimizes under Row's best response, in other words,

Pick $(y_1, y_2) \text{ that minimizes} \max(3y_1 - y_2, -2y_1 + y_2)$$
$$\text{outcome of Row's best response to } y$$

In LP form, this is

$$\min w$$
$$-3y_1 + y_2 + w \ge 0$$
$$2y_1 - y_2 + w \ge 0$$
$$y_1 + y_2 = 1$$
$$y_1 + y_2 \ge 0$$

The crucial observation is that these two LPs are dual to each other (see Figure 7.11)! They have the same optimum, call it V.

Let us summarize. By solving LP, Row (the maximizer) can determine a strategy for herself that guarantees an expected outcome of at least $V$ no matter what Column does. And by solving the dual LP, Column (the minimizer) can guarantee an expected outcome of at most $V$, no matter what Row does. It follows that this is the uniquely defined optimal play: a priori it wasn't even certain that such a player exists. $V$ is known as the value of the game. In our example, it is $1/7$ and is realized when Row plays her optimal mixed strategy $(3/7, 4/7)$ and Column plays her optimum mixed strategy $(2/7, 5/7)$.

This example easily generalizes to arbitrary games and shows the existence of mixed strategies that are optimal for both players and achieve the same value—a fundamental result of game theory called the min-max theorem. It can be written in equation form as follows:

$$\max_{x} \sum_{i,j} G_{ij}x_i y_j = \min_{y} \sum_{i,j} G_{ij}x_i y_j$$

This is surprising, because the left-hand side, in which Row has to announce her strategy first, presumably better for Column than the right-hand side, while which he has to go first. Duality equalizes the two, as it with maximum flows and minimum cuts.

## 7.6 The simplex algorithm

The extraordinary power and expressiveness of linear programs would be little solution if we did not have a way to solve them efficiently. This is the role of the simplex algorithm.

At a high level, the simplex algorithm takes a set of linear inequalities and a linear objective function finds the optimal feasible point by the following strategy:

```
let $\nu$ be any vertex of the feasible region
while there is a neighbor $\nu'$ of $\nu$ with better objective value:
    set $\nu = \nu'$
```

In our 2D and 3D examples (Figure 7.1 and Figure 7.2), this was to visualize and make intuitive sense. But what if there are $n$ variables, $x_1, \dots, x_n$?

Any set of the $x_i$'s can be represented by an $n$-tuple of real numbers and plotted in $n$-dimensional space. The feasible region of the linear program is specified by a set of inequalities and is therefore the intersection of half-spaces. But what do the concepts of *vertex* and *neighbor* mean in this context?

### 7.6.1 Vertices and neighbors in $n$-dimensional space

Figure 7.12 recalls an earlier example. Looking at it closely, we see that each *vertex* is the point at which some hyperplanes meet. Vertex A, for instance, the hyperplanes corresponding to ④ and ⑥ do not define a vertex, because their intersection is a line. Let's make this definition precise.

> Pick a subset of the inequalities. If there is a unique point that satisfies them with equality and this point happens to be feasible, then it is a vertex.

How many equations are needed to uniquely identify a point? When there are $n$ variables, we need at least $n$ linear equations if we want a unique solution. On the other hand, having more than $n$ equations is redundant: at least one of them can be rewritten as a linear combination of the others and can therefore be disregarded. In short, each vertex is specified by a set of $n$ inequalities.

A notion of *neighbor* now follows naturally. Two vertices are *neighbors* if they have $n-1$ defining inequalities in common. In Figure 7.12, for instance, vertices A and C share the inequalities ③ ⑦ and are thus neighbors.

### 7.6.2 The algorithm

- 1. Check whether the current vertex is optimal (and if so, halt).
