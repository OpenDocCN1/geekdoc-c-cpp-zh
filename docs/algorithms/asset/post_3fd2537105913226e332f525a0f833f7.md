And so, if we apply the Hadamard gate to the output of a Hadamard gate, it restores the qubit to its original state!

Another basic gate is the controlled-NOT, CNOT. It operates upon two qubits, with the first acting as a control qubit and the second as the target qubit. The CNOT gate flips the second bit if and only if the first qubit is 1. Thus $CNOT(|00\rangle) = |00\rangle$ and $CNOT(|10\rangle) = |11\rangle$.

Now let us consider the following question: Suppose we have a quantum state on $n$ qubits, $|\psi\rangle = \sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}\alpha_x|x\rangle$. How many of $2^n$ amplitudes change if we apply the Hadamard gate to only the first qubit? The surprising answer is—all of them! The new superposition $|\beta\rangle = \sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}\beta_x|x\rangle$, where $\beta_0 = \frac{\alpha_0+\alpha_1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\beta_1 = \frac{\alpha_0-\alpha_1}{\sqrt{2}}$. Looking at the results closely, the quantum operation on the first qubit deals with each $a_{0y}$ and $a_{1y}$ by transforming them into $(a_{0y}+a_{1y})/\sqrt{2}$ and $(a_{0y}-a_{1y})/\sqrt{2}$. This is exactly the effect that will give us an exponential speedup in the quantum Fourier transform.

### 10.5.2 Two basic types of quantum circuits

A quantum circuit takes some number $n$ of qubits as input, and outputs the same number of qubits. In the diagram these $n$ qubits are carried by the $n$ wires going from left to right. The quantum circuit consists of the application of a sequence of elementary quantum gates (of the kind described above) to single qubits and pairs of qubits.

At a high level, there are two basic functionalities of quantum circuits that we use in the design of quantum algorithms:

- **Fourier Transform** These quantum circuits take as input $n$ qubits in some state $|\alpha\rangle$ and output the state $|\beta\rangle$ resulting from applying the QFT to $|\alpha\rangle$.

- **Classical Functions** Consider a function $f$ with $n$ input bits and $m$ output bits, and suppose we have a classical circuit that outputs $f(x)$. Then there is a quantum circuit that, on consisting of an $n$-bit string padded with $m$ 0's, outputs $x$ and $f(x)$:

### 10.5.3 The quantum Fourier transform circuit

Here we have reproduced the diagram (from Section 2.6.4) showing how the classical FFT for $M$-vectors is composed of two FFT circuits for $(M/2)$-vectors followed by some simple gates.

FFT$_M$ (input: $\alpha_0, \dots, \alpha_{M-1}$, output: $\beta_0, \dots, \beta_{M-1}$)

![](img/3dd1f1364df8f87b80683fc32a15b999_319_1.png)

Let us see how to simulate this on a quantum system. The input is encoded in the $2^m$ amplitudes of $m = \log M$ qubits. The decomposition of the inputs into even and odd, as shown in the preceding figure, is clearly determined by one—the least significant qubit. How do we separate the even and odd inputs and apply the recursive circuits to compute FFT$_{M/2}$ on each half? The answer is remarkable: just apply the quantum circuit FFT$_{M/2}$ to the remaining $m-1$ qubits. The effect of this is to apply FFT$_{M/2}$ to the superposition of all the $m$-bit strings of $x0$ (of which there are $M/2$), and separately to the superposition of all the $m$-bit strings of $x1$. The two recursive classical circuits can be emulated by a single quantum circuit—an exponential speedup we will unwind the recursion!

Let us consider the cases in the classical FFT circuit *after* the recursive calls to $FT_{M/2}$: the wires pair up $j$ with $M/2+j$, and ignoring for now the phase that is applied to the contents of the $(M/2+j)$ wire, we must add and subtract these two quantities to obtain the $j$th and $(M/2+j)$ outputs, respectively. How would a quantum circuit achieve the result of these $M$ classical gates? Simple: perform the Hadamard gate on the first qubit! Recall from the preceding discussion (Section 10.5) that for every possible configuration of the $M-1$ qubits, we pair the strings $0x$ and $1x$. Translating from binary, this means we are pairing $x$ and $M/2+x$. The result of the Hadamard gate is that for each, the amplitudes are replaced by the sum and difference (normalized by $1/\sqrt{2}$), respectively. So the QFT requires almost no gates at all!

The phase that must be applied to the $(M/2+j)$ wire for each $j$ requires a little work. Notice that the phase of $\omega^j$ must be applied only if the first qubit is 1. If $j$ is represented by $M-1$ bits $j_1...j_{M-1}$, then $\omega^j=\prod\omega^{j_l l}$. Thus the phase $\omega^j$ can be applied by applying the $l$th wire (for each $l$) a phase of $\omega^l$ if the $l$th qubit is 1 and the first qubit is a 1. This task can be accomplished by another two-qubit quantum gate—the controlled phase gate. It leaves the two qubits unchanged they are both 1, in which case it applies a specified phase factor.

The QFT circuit is now specified. The number of quantum gates is given by the formula $S(m)=S(m-1)+O(m)$, which works out to $S(m)=O(m^2)$. The QFT on inputs of size $M=2^m$ requires $O(\log M)$ quantum operations.

## 10.6 Factoring as periodicity

We have seen how the quantum Fourier transform can be used to find the period of a periodic superposition. Now we show, by a sequence of simple reductions, how factoring can be recast as a period-finding problem.

Fix an $N$. A nontrivial square root of 1 modulo $N$ (recall Exercises 1.36 and 1.40) is any integer $x \not\pm1 \pmod N$ such that. If we find a nontrivial square root of 1 mod $N$, then it is easy to decompose $N$ into a product of two nontrivial factors (and repeating the process would factor $N$):

> **Lemma** If $x$ is a nontrivial square root of 1 modulo $N$, then $\gcd(x+1, N)$ is a nontrivial factor of $N$.

Proof. $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod N$ implies $N$ divides $(x-1)(x+1)$. Since $x$ is a nontrivial square root, $N$ does not divide $x-1$ or $x+1$. Thus $N$ must share a factor with $x-1$ and $x+1$, so $\gcd(x+1, N)$ is a nontrivial factor of $N$.

Example. If $x=2$ and $N=15$, then the order of 2 is 4 because $2^4 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$. Raising 2 to half power, we get a nontrivial root of 1: $2^2 = 4 \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 5$. So we get a divisor of 15 by computing $\gcd(4+1, 15)=5$.

To complete the connection with periodicity, we need one further concept. Define the order of $x$ modulo $N$ to be the smallest integer $r$ such that $x^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$.

> **Lemma** Let $N$ be odd composite, and $x$ chosen uniformly at random. If $\gcd(x,N)=1$, then with probability 1/2, the order $r$ of $x$ modulo $N$ is even, and moreover $x^{r/2}$ is a nontrivial square root of 1 modulo $N$.

The proof of this lemma is an exercise. What it implies is that if we compute the order $r$ of a randomly element $x \pmod N$, there's a chance that this order is even and $x^{r/2}$ is a nontrivial square root of 1 modulo $N$. In which case $\gcd(x^{r/2}-1, N)$ is a factor of $N$.

We have reduced FACTORING to the problem of ORDER FINDING. The advantage of this task is that it has a natural function associated with it: fix $x$, and consider the function $f(a)=x^a \pmod N$. If $r$ is the order, then $f(0)=f(r)=f(2r)=\dots=1$, and similarly, $f(1)=f(r+1)=f(2r+1)=\dots=x$. Thus $f$ is periodic, with period $r$. And we can compute it by the repeated squaring algorithm from Section 1.2. So to order $N$, all we need to do is figure out how to use the function $f$ to set up a periodic superposition with period $r$; whereupon we can use quantum Fourier sampling as-is described in the next box.
