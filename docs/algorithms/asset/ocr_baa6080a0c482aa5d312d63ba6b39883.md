# Implications for computer science and quantum physics

In the early days of computer science, people wondered whether there were much more powerful computers than those made up of circuits of elementary gates. But since seventies this question has been considered well settled. Computers implementing the Neumann architecture on silicon are obvious winners, and it was widely accepted that any other way of implementing computers is polynomially equivalent to them. That is, T-step computation on any computer takes at most a polynomial in T steps on. This fundamental principle is called the extended Church-Turing thesis. Quantum computers validate this fundamental thesis and therefore call into question some of our most assumptions about computers.

Can quantum computers be built? This is the challenge that is keeping many research teams of physicists and computer scientists around the world. The main problem is that quantum superpositions are very fragile and need to be protected from any inadvertent measurement by the environment. There is progress, but it is very slow: so far, the most ambitious quantum computation was the factorization of the number 15 into its factors 3 and 5 using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). And even in this experiment, there are questions about how faithfully the quantum factoring algorithm was implemented. The next decade promises to be really exciting in terms of our ability to physically manipulate bits and implement quantum computers.

But there is another possibility: What if all these efforts at implementing quantum computers fail? This would be even more interesting, because it would point to some fundamental flaw in quantum physics, a theory that has stood unchallenged for a century.

Quantum computation is motivated as much by trying to clarify the mysterious nature of quantum physics as by trying to create novel and superfluous computers.
