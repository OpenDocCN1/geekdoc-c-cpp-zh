At the very top level, when $k=0$, this works out to $O(n)$. At the bottom, when $k=\log_2 n$, it is $O(n^{\log_2 3})$, which can be rewritten as $O(n^{1.58})$. Between these two endpoints, the work increases geometrically from $O(n)$ to $O(n^{3.3})$, by a factor of 3/2 per level. The sum of any increasing geometric series is simply the last term of the series: such is the rapidity of the increase (Exercise 0.2). Therefore the overall running time is $O(n^{\log_2 3})$.

In the absence of Gauss's trick, the recursion tree would have the same height, but the branching factor would be 4. There would be $4^{n}=n^2$ leaves, and therefore the running time would be much slower. In divide-and-conquer algorithms, the number of subproblems translates into the branching factor of the recursion tree; small changes in this coefficient can have a big impact on running time.

A practical note: it generally does not make sense to recur all the way to 1 bit. For most processors, 16- or 32-bit multiplication is a single operation, so by the time the numbers get into this range they should be handed over to the built-in procedure.
