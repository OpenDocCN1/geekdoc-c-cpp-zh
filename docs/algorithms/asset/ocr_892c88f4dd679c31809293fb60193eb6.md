Evaluation is multiplication by $M$, while interpolation is multiplication by $M^{-1}$.

This reformulation of our polynomial operations reveals their essential nature more clearly. Among other things, it finally justifies an assumption we have been making throughout, that $A(x)$ is uniquely characterized by its values at any $n$ points—in fact, we now have an explicit formula that will give us the coefficients of $A(x)$ in this situation. Vandermonde matrices also have the distinction of being unique to invert over general matrices, in $O(n^2)$ instead of $O(n^3)$. However, using this for interpolation would still not be enough for us, so once again we turn to our special choice of points—the complex roots of unity.

where $\omega$ is a complex $n$th root of unity, and $n$ is a power of 2. Notice how this matrix describes: its $(j, k)$ entry (starting row- and column-count at zero) is $\omega^{jk}$.

Multiplication by $M(\omega)$ maps the $k$th coordinate axis (the vector with zeros except for 1 at $k$ position) of $M$. Here's the crucial observation, which we'll prove shortly: the columns of $M$ are orthogonal (at right angles) to each other. They can then be thought of as axes of an alternative coordinate system, which is often called the Fourier basis. The effect of the Fourier basis, which is defined by the columns of $M$ is a change of basis, a *rigid rotation*. The inverse of $M$ is the opposite rotation, and when we write out the orthogonality precisely, we will be able to read off this transformation with ease:

# Inversion formula $M_n(\omega)^{-1} = \frac{1}{n}M_n(\omega^{-1})$.
