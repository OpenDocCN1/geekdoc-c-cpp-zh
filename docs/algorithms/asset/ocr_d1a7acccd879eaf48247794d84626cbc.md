# Why multiply polynomials?

For one thing, it turns out that the fastest algorithms we have for multiplying integers heavily on polynomial multiplication; after all, polynomials and binary integers are quite similar—just replace the variable x by the base 2, and watch out for carries. But perhaps more importantly, multiplying polynomials is crucial for signal processing

A signal is any quantity that is a function of time (as in Figure (a)) or position. It might, for instance, capture a human voice by measuring fluctuations in air pressure close to the speaker's mouth, or alternatively, the pattern of stars in the night sky, measuring brightness as a function of angle.

In order to extract information from a signal, we will need to digitize it by sampling (Figure (b)—and, then, to put it through a system that will transform it in some way. The output is called the *response* of the system:

signal $\longrightarrow$ response.

An important class of systems are those that are linear—the response to the sum of signals is just the sum of their individual responses—and time invariant—shifting the input signal by time $t$ produces the same output, also shifted by $t$. Any system with these properties is characterized by its response to the simplest possible input: the unit impulse $\delta(t)$, consisting of a "jerk". To see this, consider the close $\delta(t-i)$, a shifted impulse in which the jerk occurs at time $i$. Any signal $a(t)$ can be expressed as a linear combination of these, letting $\delta(t-i)$ pick out its behavior at time $i$,

```
$y(t) = \sum_{i=0}^{T} a(i)\delta(t-i)$
```

(if the signal consists of $T$ samples). By linearity, the response to input $a(t)$ is determined by the responses to the various $\delta(t-i)$. And by time invariance, these are in just shifted copies of the impulse response $b(t)$, the response to $\delta(t)$.

In other words, the output of the system at time $k$ is

```
$c(k) = \sum_{i=0}^{k} a(i)b(k-i)$,
```

exactly the formula for polynomial multiplication!
