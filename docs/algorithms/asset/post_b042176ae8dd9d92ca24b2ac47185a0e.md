# Preface

This book evolved over the past ten years from a set of lecture notes developed while teaching the Algorithms course at Berkeley and U.C. San Diego. Our way of teaching this course evolved over these years in a number of directions, partly to address our students' background (undeveloped formal skills), and partly to reflect the mentoring of the field in general, The notes increasingly crystallized into a narrative, and we progressively structured the course to emphasize the “story line” implicit in the material. As a result, the topics were carefully selected. No attempt was made to be encyclopedic, and this freed us to include topics traditionally deemphasized or omitted from most Algorithms books. Playing on the strengths of our students (shared by most undergraduates' undergraduates), instead of dwelling on formal proofs we distilled in each case the mathematical idea that makes the algorithm work. In other words, we emphasized rigor over formalism. We found that our students were much more receptive to mathematical rigor of this form. It is this progression of ideas that helps weave the story. Once you think about Algorithms in this way, it makes sense to start at the beginning of it all, when addition, the characters are familiar and the contrasts dramatic: numbers, primacy, and factoring. This is the subject of Part I of the book, which also includes the RSA cryptosystem, and divide-and-conquer algorithms for integer multiplication, sorting and finding median, as well as the Fast Fourier transform. There are three other parts: Part II, the most traditional section of the book, contains data structures and graphs; the contrast here is the intricate structure of the underlying problems and the short and crisp pieces of pseudocode they solve. Students wishing to teach a more traditional course can simply start with Part II, which is self-contained (following the pedagogy), and then cover I as required. In Parts I and II we introduced techniques (such as greedy and divide-and-conquer) which work for special kinds of problems; Part III deals with “sledgehammers” of the trade, techniques that are powerful and general: dynamic programming (a novel approach helps clarify this traditional blocking for students) and linear programming (a clean and intuitive treatment of the simplex algorithm, duality, and reductions to the basic problem). The final Part IV is about ways of dealing with hard problems: NP-completeness, various heuristics, as well as quantum algorithms, perhaps the most advanced and modern topic. As it happens, we end the story exactly where we started it, with Shor’s algorithm for factoring.
