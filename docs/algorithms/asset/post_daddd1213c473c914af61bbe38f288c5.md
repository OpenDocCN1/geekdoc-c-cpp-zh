
Finally, there is another class of problems, between the two here given, for which the approximation ratio is about log n; SET COVER is an example.

(A humbling reminder: All this is contingent on the assumption P ≠ NP. Failing this, this hierarchy collapses down to P, and all NP-complete optimization problems can be solved exactly in polynomial time.)

A final point on approximation algorithms: often these, or their variants, perform much better on typical instances than their worst-case approximation ratio would have you believe.

## 9.3 Local search heuristics

Our strategy for coping with NP-completeness is inspired by evolution (which is, after all, the world's best-tested optimization procedure)—by its incremental process of introducing small mutations, trying them out, and keeping them if they work well. This paradigm is called local search and can be applied to any optimization task. Here is how it looks for a minimization problem.

```
let s be any initial solution
while there is some solution s' in the neighborhood of s
  for which cost(s') < cost(s): replace s by s'
return s
```

On each iteration, the current solution is replaced by a better one to close, in its neighborhood. This neighborhood structure is something we impose upon the problem and is the central design decision in local search. As an illustration, let's revisit the traveling salesman problem.

### 9.3.1 Traveling salesman once more

Assume we have interpoint distances between n cities, giving a search space of (n - 1)! different tours. What is a good notion of neighborhood?

The obvious notion is to consider two tours as being close if they differ in just a few edges. They can't differ in just one edge (do you see why?), so we consider differences of two edges. We define the 2-change neighborhood of tour s as the set of tours that can be obtained by removing two edges of s and putting in two other edges. Here's an example of a local move:

We now have a well-defined local search procedure. How does it measure up under our two standard criteria for algorithms—what is its overall running time, and does it always return the best solution?

Embarrassingly, neither of these questions has a satisfactory answer. Each iteration is certainly fast, because a tour has only $O(n^2)$ neighbors. However, it is not clear how many iterations will be needed: for instance, there might be an exponential number of them. Likewise, we can easily say about the final tour that it is locally optimal—that is, it is superior to the tours in its immediate neighborhood. There might be better solutions further away. For instance, the following picture shows a possible final answer that is clearly suboptimal; the range of local moves is simply too limited to improve upon it.

![](img/3dd1f1364df8f87b80683fc32a15b999_296_0.png)

To overcome this, we may try a more generous neighborhood, for instance 3-change, consisting of tours that differ up on three edges. And indeed, the preceding bad case gets fixed:

![](img/3dd1f1364df8f87b80683fc32a15b999_296_1.png)

But there is a downside, in that the size of a neighborhood becomes $O(n^2)$, making each iteration more expensive. Moreover, there may still be suboptimal local minima, although fewer than before. To avoid this, we would have to go up to 4-change, or higher. In this manner, efficiency and quality often turn out to be competing considerations in a local search. Efficiency demands neighborhoods that can be searched quickly, but smaller neighborhoods increase the abundance of low-quality local optima. The appropriate compromise is typically determined by experimentation.

The hope is that with a judicious choice of neighborhood structure, most local optima will be reasonable. ### 9.3.2 Graph partitioning

The problem of graph partitioning arises in diversity of applications, from circuit layout to image segmentation. We saw a special case of it, BALANCED CUT, in Chapter 8.

**Graph Partitioning**
- **Input:** An undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ with nonnegative edge weights; a real number $\alpha \in (0, 1/2)$.
- **Output:** A partition of the vertices into two groups $A$ and $B$, each of size at least $\alpha|V|$.
- **Goal:** Minimize the capacity of the cut $(A, B)$.

Figure 9.9 shows an example in which the graph has 16 nodes, all edge weights are 0 or 1, and the optimal solution has cost 0. Removing the restriction on sizes of A and B would give the MINIMUM CUT problem, which we know is efficiently solvable using flow techniques. The present variant, however, is NP-hard. In designing a local search algorithm, it will be a convenience to focus on the special case $\alpha=1/2$, in which $A$ and $B$ are forced to contain exactly half the vertices. The loss of generality is purely cosmetic, as GRAPH PARTITION reduces to this case.

We need to decide upon a neighborhood structure for our problem, and there is one obvious way to do this. Let $(A, B)$, with $|A|=|B|$, a candidate solution; we will define its neighbors to be all solutions obtainable by swapping one pair of vertices across the cut, that is all solutions of the form $(A-\{a\}, B\{b\})$ where $a \in A$ and $b \in B$.
