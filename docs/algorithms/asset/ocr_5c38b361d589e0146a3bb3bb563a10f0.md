# An n log n lower bound for sorting (continued )

This way of looking at sorting algorithms is useful because it allows one to argue that mergesort is optimal, in the sense that $\Omega(n \log n)$ comparisons are necessary for n elements.

Here is the argument: Consider any tree that stores an array of n elements. Each of its leaves is labeled by a permutation of [1, 2, ..., n]. In fact, every permutation must appear as the label of a leaf. The reason is simple: if a particular permutation is missing, what happens if we feed the algorithm an input ordered according to this permutation? Since there are n! permutations of n elements, it follows that the tree has at least n! leaves.

We are almost done: This is a binary tree, and we argued that it has at least n! leaves. Recall that a binary tree of depth d has at most 2^d leaves (an easy proof by induction over d), so the depth of our tree—and the complexity of our algorithm—must be at least log(n!).

And it is known that $\log(n!) \geq c \log n$ for some $c > 0$. There are many ways to see this. The easiest is to notice that $n! \geq (n/2)^{n/2}$ because $n! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot \dots \cdot n$ contains at least n/2 factors larger than n/2; and thus take logs of both sides. It is also nice to recall Stirling's formula

```
$n! \approx \sqrt{2\pi n} (2n/3)^n \cdot e^{-n}$
```

Either way we have established that any comparison tree that sorts n elements must make, in the worst case, $\Omega(n \log n)$ comparisons, and hence mergesort is optimal! Well, there is some fine print: this neat argument applies only to algorithms that use comparisons. It is conceivable that there are alternative sorting strategies, perhaps using sophisticated numerical manipulations, that work in linear time? The answer is yes, under certain exceptional circumstances: the canonical example is when the elements to be sorted are integers that lie in a small range (Exercise 2.20).

# 2.4 Medians

The median of a list of numbers is its 50th percentile: half the numbers are bigger than it, and half are smaller. For instance, the median of [45, 1, 10, 30] is 25, since this is the middle element when the numbers are arranged in order. If the list has even length, there are two choices for what the middle element could be, in which case we pick the smaller of the two, say.

The purpose of the median is to summarize a set of numbers by a single, typical value. The mean, or average, is also very common for this, but the median is in a sense more typical of the data: it is always one of the data values, unlike the mean, and it is less sensitive to outliers. For instance, the median of a list of hundred 1's is (rightly) 1, as is the mean. If one of the numbers gets accidentally corrupted to 10,000, the mean shoots up above hundred, while the median remains unaffected.
