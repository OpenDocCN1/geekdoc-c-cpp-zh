# Review: Peculiarities of Medicare Data

Refer to the reviews in Chapters 6, 7, and 8 to review the material relevant to this chapter's study of chronic conditions and quality. As mentioned above, quality measurement can be controversial because it involves relating provider performance. With this in mind, let's review some peculiarities and possible limitations of Medicare administrative data so we can be better prepared to interpret our output.

+   - Medicare coverage for the elderly is praised regardless of medical history. A beneficiary diagnosed with COPD who becomes eligible for Medicare is granted coverage regardless of her COPD diagnosis. If you are used to working with commercial healthcare data, you will likely notice some characteristics of the Medicare, like beneficiaries having at least one chronic condition (this may change the implementation of the Affordable Care Act).

+   - Some services may not appear in Medicare administrative data. For example, prescriptions administered during a hospital stay may not appear in the data at all. Additionally, services paid for by Medicare Part C may not appear in the administrative data files because managed care providers pay them.

+   - The administrative data we use for research purposes is updated on a regular basis, but only with claims that have been received, adjusted, and deemed final action. As such, the files we use at any time do not contain final claim submitted and paid up to the date of extraction of data. In some rare cases, when not impractical, medical records can fill in gaps of information. What's more, we will see changes to the information available for quality measurement as electronic medical records become prevalent.

+   - The correct composition of any study population must be determined carefully. In Chapter 6, we determined that our example project will include only those beneficiaries continuously enrolled in fee-of-service Medicare during a defined timeframe, which has implications for the out-of-service of our example researching project. More specifically, this step resulted in eliminating beneficiaries for whom we may not have a full set of claims, giving us more confidence that we are not missing claims for services like retinal eye or hospital admissions for our study population.
