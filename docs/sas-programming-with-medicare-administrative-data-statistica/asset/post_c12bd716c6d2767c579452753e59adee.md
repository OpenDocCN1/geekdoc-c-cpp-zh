For now, let's get with some well-known examples of how particulars of the administration of the Medicare program influence the content of administrative data files.

- With about 47 million Medicare beneficiaries, we can expect the administrative data files we use to be quite large. As such, we will need to consider efficient programming techniques. Many of the exercises in later address efficiency topics.

- Some services that you want to study may not appear in Medicare administrative data or, the least, may require searching multiple files. For Part D prescription data contains information for prescription drug files. Prescription drugs administered during a hospital stay may not appear in the claims at all. Additionally, services paid for Medicare Part C may not appear in the administrative claims files because they are paid by managed care providers.¹¹²

- Medicare pays for some services (e.g., home health agencies, hospice, hospital inpatient, skilled nursing, or acute inpatient hospitals) using what are called prospective payment systems (PPS). In very terms, a PPS reimburses providers using a fixed amount derived from a predetermined classification system.¹¹³ We will discuss payment systems more in Chapter 8.

- As a social insurance program, Medicare coverage is provided regardless of medical history. Therefore, if you are to use working with commercial healthcare claims data, you will likely notice some unique characteristics of the Medicare population, such as a higher prevalence of chronic conditions.

- The administrative data we use for research purposes are updated on a regular basis, but only claims that have been received and adjudicated deemed final action. As such, the files we use at any given time do not contain all final claims submitted and up to the date of extraction of the data. It is common practice to wait at least three months for paid claims to appear in the files maintained by CMS. For example, a request for the full calendar year 2014 is best made on or after April 1, 2015.

- Depending on what you are studying, care be taken to determine the correct composition of your study population. For instance, our example research project will only show those beneficiaries continuously enrolled in fee-for-service Medicare over a defined timeframe. Other studies may wish to focus on beneficiaries entitled to Medicare based on being disabled. We will see in Chapter 6 that we can use enrollment data to determine a beneficiary’s reason for entitlement and define our study population.

## Chapter Summary

In this chapter, we set a foundation for programming with SAS and Medicare administrative data by examining the following:

- Understanding the Medicare program and the particulars of Medicare coverage is absolutely essential to successfully programming with Medicare administrative data.
- Medicare is a social insurance program that provides beneficiaries with an array of insurance coverage, regardless of income or medical history.
- The majority of Medicare beneficiaries are eligible for Medicare insurance because they are aged 65 and over. However, Medicare also insurers who are permanently disabled (receiving Social Security Insurance or SSDI), have ESRD, and have ALS.
- Medicare benefits are divided and divided into four parts: Part A (Hospital Insurance), Part B (Supplemental Medical Insurance), Part C (Medicare Advantage), and Part D (outpatient prescription drug coverage). Each Part covers a different type of care or services. These Parts are a way of describing organizing the way we think about the administrative data files we use throughout this book.
- Like other health insurance plans, Medicare does not cover every medical service or procedure.
- The primary purpose of Medicare payment systems is not to create data for research, but to adjudicate and pay claims. This has implications for using the administrative data files and means that the user must understand the Medicare program.
