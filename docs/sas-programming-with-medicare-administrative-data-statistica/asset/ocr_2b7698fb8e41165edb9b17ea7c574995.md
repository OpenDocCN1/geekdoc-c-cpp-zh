# Introduction and Goals

Measuring cost is important because it gets to the heart of the sustainability of any insurance program, and Medicare is certainly no exception. It is no understatement to say that the measurement of cost in the Medicare program is a hot topic. Any newspaper or magazine or turn on the evening news and you will see that reducing cost without negatively affecting the quality of care is the order of the day. In other words, the mission is to monitor and improve the value of purchased healthcare. To play a part in accomplishing this mission, we must learn how to measure the cost of. In addition, we must learn to measure the quality of cost, which we will do in Chapter 9 as we look at chronic conditions.

Utilization and cost are intimately linked to cost, but not in ways one might assume. In your research, may wish to go beyond simple measures of utilization and actually define categories of services whose payments may have been reduced by an incentive program. For example, did a program reduce the number of hospital stays, reducing total cost per beneficiary? Alternatively, did a program reduce the number of annual checkups, resulting in higher costs driven by an increase in the number of hospital stays?

In this text, the term “cost” is limited to the cost of service to Medicare. We will not study the total cost of services, which includes costs to beneficiaries, like coinsurance and deductibles. We are not studying services not covered by Medicare, like cosmetic surgery.
