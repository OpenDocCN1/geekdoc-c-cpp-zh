# A Note on the Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW)

Readers with Medicare administrative data may be trying to reconcile two facts:

- We are coding algorithms to identify beneficiaries with two chronic conditions, diabetes and COPD, using claims data.
- CMS provides files containing this information to the research community through the Chronic Condition Data Warehouse (CCW). The CCW "is a research database designed to make Medicare, Assessments, and Part D Drug Event more readily available to support research designed to improve the quality of care and reduce costs and utilization." These are available through ResData and CMS's data distribution contractor.

With some simplifications, we will draw on the CCW's definitions of diabetes and COPD for coding the identification of diabetes and COPD in our beneficiary population. Why do we code these ourselves when flagging beneficiaries with chronic conditions is provided by CMS? Coding chronic conditions is useful for instrumental purposes, providing a context for understanding potentially modifying chronic conditions not available in the CCW. In addition, some investigators choose to define conditions because they wish to modify the definition in the CCW. For example, the CCW definition of diabetes includes secondary diabetes, which may not be the right choice for your particular study. It should go without saying that readers are encouraged to take full advantage of the data offered in the CCW.

# Algorithms: Identifying Beneficiaries with Diabetes or COPD

Our first task is to identify beneficiaries in our population with diabetes or COPD. At the risk of being repetitive, before we begin, it is very important to note that the diagnosis codes we use will change from year to year. The same goes for all of the codes we use in this book. For example, procedure and diagnosis codes are updated annually (new codes are created and some are retired). Therefore, it is good practice to regularly check the codes you use to identify diagnoses or services (anything, for that matter) to ensure they are correct. This will be especially true after October 2014, as all codes will change when a new diagnosis coding system, ICD-10-CM, is implemented.

The algorithm to identify beneficiaries with diabetes is adapted from instructions available from the CCW, with some simplifications (e.g., the identification of diabetes we do not require looking across two years of data). Our SAS code involves searching for specific diagnosis codes (see the below algorithms for the lists of specific diagnosis codes). Codes can occur at any position on the claim (i.e., they do not have to be primary diagnoses). The setting in which these diagnosis codes occurs is important.

Specifically, if we find evidence a beneficiary was diagnosed with diabetes on only one occasion in an inpatient or skilled nursing facility setting, we will count that as having diabetes. In these settings, true coding experts review medical records, consult with physicians, and only record a diagnosis if the patient actually has the condition. However, we need separate events of a diagnosis of diabetes when searching in carrier and hospital inpatient claims. Why? Because the occurrence of a single diagnosis of diabetes in these settings may be called a "rule out" diagnosis, where the physician orders a lab test to check for diabetes. The second diagnosis confirms the beneficiary does have diabetes. In all cases, the diagnoses can occur any time during a two-year period (but we will search just one year of data in our example). Finally, we will note that we use home health claims data, not hospice or DME data, in our identification of beneficiaries with diabetes or COPD. We use home health data even though investigators view diagnosis codes on home health claims as unwise. We do not use hospice claims due to the nature of care provided in a hospice setting. Lastly, for obvious reasons, the DME file cannot be used to reliably identify beneficiaries with diabetes or COPD.

The algorithm to identify beneficiaries with COPD is constructed in a similar manner (and also adapted from information provided by the CCW) to identify the beneficiaries with diabetes, but using different diagnosis codes (and the search typically occurs over one-year period of claims data). Therefore, we will use a single algorithm to search inpatient, SNF, outpatient, and Part B carrier for evidence of these diagnosis codes, setting flags where appropriate. Then, we will count the flags created, and use these counts to identify beneficiaries with diabetes or COPD.

In Step 9.1, we present an algorithm for identifying the presence of diagnosis codes used to identify diabetes or COPD in our inpatient, SNF, home health, and outpatient claims data sets. This algorithm is similar to code used in previous chapters to identify diagnosis codes in data. We used a macro called CLM LOOP and array logic to create separate flags set to 1 in the presence of diabetes (DIA_IA, DIA_SN, DIA_OP, and DIA_HH) and COPD (COPD_IP, COPD_SN, COPD_OP, and COPD_HH) on each claim. Each of these data sets contains 25 diagnosis variables (ICD_1_DGNS_CD25). In addition, we use conditional processing for our inpatient data sets first-case, as we are only searching claims for inpatient short and critical access hospitals with non-negative values of PMT_AMT. We use a %if clause to create the inpatient patients in UTIL_IP_2010_FNL. We output only those records where the flag variables were equal to 1. In Step 9.3 and Step 9.4, we will use these records as definitive proof that the beneficiary has diabetes or COPD (with the exception of the flags derived from inpatient data sets).

```sas
* STEP 9.1: SEARCH INPATIENT, SNF, HOME HEALTH, AND OUTPATIENT CLAIMS
FOR DIABETES AND COPD DIAGNOSIS
* NOTE THIS MACRO WILL NOT ERROR IF CLMTYP VALUE IS NOT CREATE */
%macro clm_loop(&clmtyp);
data cc_&clmtyp._search(keep=bene_id dia_&clmtyp copd_&clmtyp);
  if &clmtyp=sn or &clmtyp=op or &clmtyp=hh then do
    set utl_&clmtyp_2010_fnl;
  else if &clmtyp=ip then do
    set utl_ip_2010_fnl;
  end;
  array dgnsc(25) icd_dgns_cd1-icd_dgns_cd25;
  do i=1 to 25;
    /* diabetes */
    if substn(dgnsc(i),1,4)='3572'
    or substn(dgnsc(i),1,5)
    in('24900','24901','24910','24911','24920','24921','24930','24931',
    '24940','24950','24951','24960','24961','24970','24971',
    '24980','24990','24991',
    '25000','25001','25002','25003','25010','25011','25012','25013',
    '25020','25021','25022','25023',
    '25030','25031','25032','25033','25040','25041','25042','25043',
    '25050','25051','25052','25053',
    '25060','25061','25062','25063','25070','25071','25072','25073',
    '25080','25081','25082','25083',
    '25090','25091','25092','25093','36201','36202','36641') then dia_&clmtyp=1;
    /* copd */
    if substn(dgnsc(i),1,3)='496'
    or substn(dgnsc(i),1,4)
    in('4910','4911','4918','4919','4920','4928','4940','4941') then copd_&clmtyp=1;
    or substn(dgnsc(i),1,5) in('49120','49121','49122') then copd_&clmtyp=1;
  end;
  if dia_&clmtyp= drop dia_&clmtyp=-1;
run;
%mend clm_loop;
%clm_loop(clmtyp=ip);
%clm_loop(clmtyp=sn);
%clm_loop(clmtyp=op);
%clm_loop(clmtyp=hh);
```

In Step 9.2, we continue the process of identifying the presence of diagnosis codes used to identify diabetes or COPD, this time using our carriers data set. We output only those records where the flag variables are equal to 1. In Step 9.3 and 9.4, we will use these records to proof that the beneficiary has diabetes or COPD.

In Step 9.3, we use a macro to loop through all claims processed, summarizing the flags for each beneficiary by claim type, outputting one file for each relevant claim type (CC_NT_IP, CC_NT_SN, CC_NT_OP, CC_NT_HH, and CC_NT_CARR).
