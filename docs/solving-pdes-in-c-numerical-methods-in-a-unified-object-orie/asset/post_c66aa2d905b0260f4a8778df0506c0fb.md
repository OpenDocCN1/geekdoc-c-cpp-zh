
is defined only relatively in terms of other people only and his emotions towards them. Indeed, from birth, the baby identifies with his/her mother, and later on with his/her father too. Furthermore, throughout life, the human sees not himself/herself but rather other people only, and always seeks their attention, appreciation, assurance, acceptance, and recognition of his/her valuable contribution to and place in society. In this view, each individual human mind exists only relatively as a reference or a mirror to others. Only two or more mirrors can share and reflect information and form a system or a society. An isolated mirror, on the other hand, would remain in the dark forever. As indicated in Zen Buddhism, a single hand can never clap.

It is particularly interesting to see how the above observations are also relevant in perhaps the most abstract form of language: poetry.

## 4.6 Verlaine: Impressionism in Poetry

Poetry, as well as other kinds of art, may indeed be viewed as the interface between the obscured ideas and impressions deep in one’s mind and the more explicit thoughts that can be expressed in any human language. The rhythm of poetry and the pauses and blank spaces used in between the lines are what communicates emotions, impressions, and ideas that cannot be said in any words. As the great poet Paul Verlaine advises poets in his poem “The Art of Poetry” (free interpretation of an interpretation by Aminadav Dikman):

> The words you use  
> you have to choose  
> with some degree of disregard.  
>
> Remember:  
> treasure points at which  
> implicit raw ideas meet  
> the well defined.  
>
> And all the rest—  
> is only prose.

In fact, Verlaine even recommends here to use inaccurate words. After all, there is no chance to express the pure idea or the deep emotion properly, so why bother to use precise words? It makes much more sense to go about the original idea and only indicate or hint it. To make the correct impression, the dramatic words and rhythm used in poetry are particularly suitable. All the rest is now up to the reader.

## 4.7 Enzensberger and Rilke

Here are two more poems by the German poet Hans Magnus Enzensberger that show us how poor and inexact words can be in expressing deep, delicate emotions and feelings (free interpretation of an interpretation by Asher Raich):

> For to say “I’m happy”  
> doesn’t mean you’re happy.  
>
> For the thirsty man  
> doesn’t know he is thirsty.  
> For the desperate man  
> doesn’t say he is desperate.  
> For climax and climax  
> are two different things.  
> For the dying man  
> only says quiet words  
> that we can’t hear.

Here is yet another poem:

My son,  
don’t read poetry.  
Read railway lists,  
they are more precise.  
Read topographic maps,  
before it is too late.  
Open your eyes;  
write no poem.

Furthermore, in his poem “Dante,” Enzensberger also wrote about identity. He says that nobody can determine your identity for you. Only you can decide what your identity really is, even though this may disagree with what everybody else expects from you.

In his book *The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge* (1910), Rainer Maria Rilke writes that memories must be well processed and integrated in one’s mind before they can come out in the form of a poem. This is why only at old age can one interpret his/her entire life experience into at most one good poem. Furthermore, the entire history of the entirety of mankind is not as important as the life of one poet. Indeed, as Aristotle said, poetry is a greater import than history. After all, history can tell the past, but poetry may capture the unique moment of present.

## 4.8 Whitman and His Followers

In the above poems, one can find some general truth or observation rather than mere private introspection. The poet not only reveals to us readers his/her own psychological state of mind, but also reflects the entire natural or social landscape. This is indeed what characterizes valuable poetry, such as that of the great poet Walt Whitman and his followers, including the Israeli poets Avoth Yeshurun and Nadav Liniel.

The young poet Nadav Liniel is particularly influenced not only by Whitman but also by Aristotle and Wittgenstein. Indeed, it seems that Liniel realizes that there are no words that can express true, deep, raw emotion (free interpretation):

Iron remembers rain  
with a touch of rust.  
Rock misses the rain  
that breaks it down.

Is there any way  
to remember your name?  
Is there any name  
for a soul  
with no body?  
Love is love.

Furthermore, in his poem “Forward and Backward,” Liniel describes in a rather vivid way how he walks around in his own home, and how every ray of light from a car that passes in the street and every kind of food in the refrigerator remind him of the presence of his lover who sleeps alone in the other room. The metaphors that Liniel uses for this purpose indeed produce a rather realistic erotic and lyric atmosphere that cannot be described in any prose.

Moreover, in his letter to Whitman, entitled “Thing,” Liniel uses both time and space scales to show how modest one should be in observing nature and in testifying about it:

I follow you  
to learn how things  
can pop out from their names  
to be sent off.  
Trees send a message from earth to sky  
and sky answers with rain.  
Valleys bear tons of air  
and thunders thunder.  
Nothing is measured in people here.  
And I? I can tell you nothing.  
Trees remember their birth  
no tree grows in one day.  
I don’t know the name of the green.  
Many things that I can’t tell  
are written in those trees  
that will turn to pages as you did.  
Before opening the door  
to see that you’re gone,  
I make one more attempt  
to tell one thing from all.

## 4.9 What Is Poetry?

In prose or classical poetry, words often refer to general concepts that are more or less known to every reader. Still, the precise meaning of each word may be subjective and depend on the private background of each individual reader.

In modern poetry, on the other hand, words may refer not only to specific concepts but also to vague ideas. The reader has thus a more active role in interpreting the words according to his/her own world of associations. Furthermore, the reader is free to use his/her own imagination to combine words into phrases and to produce the overall emotional atmosphere.

This is probably why poetry may have such a strong effect on the reader: it may reflect his/her own emotional state through words that really touch.

Poetry may thus be defined as any text that may give the reader a sense of aesthetics and insight. This definition is of course subjective: some text may seem as poetry to some readers and as mere prose to other readers. In fact, this may be relevant not only for texts written in a natural human language but also for texts written in a formal language such as a programming language or mathematics. For example, some readers may well see the beauty in a well-written scientific theory, mathematical proof, or computer program and consider it as poetry.

## 4.10 Dreams and the Subconsciousness—Freud’s Theory

Poetry may well touch the reader as strongly and deeply as a dream. In fact, as in Freud’s theory, a dream too may give the dreamer a short glance into his/her own subconsciousness. The views seen in a dream have no obvious verbal representation. Still, once the dream is told by the dreamer to someone else, the words used for this purpose may reveal secrets from the very subconsciousness of the dreamer that have been completely unknown to him/her before.

Thus, the words used to tell the dream come from the same source as the dream itself: that is, subconsciousness. In fact, these words refer to the original memories in the original background of the dreamer that may have been locked up for years. These words may therefore be considered as subjective pointers or references to subjective memories in one’s mind.

## 4.11 Natural Human Languages

As discussed above, general objects and concepts used in natural human languages are so vague that no word can describe them precisely. Words can at best refer to or indicate the original concepts, so that they can be combined with other words in subsentences and sentences. These indications, however, are not objective but rather subjective: they depend strongly on the background of the person who uses them and on his/her personal associations and memories.

Fortunately, most speakers of a particular language have rather similar backgrounds, so they assign rather the same meaning to each word. This way, they can indeed communicate with each other and understand each other.
