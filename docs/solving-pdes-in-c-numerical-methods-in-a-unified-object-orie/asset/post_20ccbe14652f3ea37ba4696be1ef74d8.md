
Thinking about an object may also result in naming it in more accurate words that may be relevant in different contexts. This is why a natural language is also referred to often as a context-sensitive language.

The thinking process may also lead to realizing the influence of the object on other objects in different circumstances. This may lead to characterizing the original object not only in terms of its original properties but also in terms of its potential effects.

Still, although the thinking process may give the object better and better characterization in terms of properties or effects that may be relevant in different contexts, it may never lead to a completely objective definition. Indeed, every definition must use words, which are vague and imprecise as well. Thus, thinking about an object may at best improve one’s own private subjective interpretation, but never form a universal objective context-free interpretation.

Below we introduce a model to attempt to interpret the human mind and show how it is related to language.

## 4.13 Inductive Models for Talking, Listening, and Thinking

The above discussion may be used to introduce a model for the activity of one’s mind. In this model, one’s memories and experience lie within the private background of his/her mind, whereas one’s vocabulary lies within the foreground of one’s mind. When one talks, his/her private memories in the background of his/her mind translate into words in the foreground for others to hear and interpret.

Still, because words are inherently inaccurate, the translation (or projection, or mapping) from background to foreground is not one-to-one: two different ideas (or concepts, or thoughts) may be so close or similar to each other that both are expressed by the same word. Therefore, the mapping from background to foreground that produces one’s words is not always reversible: it may be impossible to recover one’s original thoughts from his/her own words. This is probably why one may often feel the need to use more and more words to form more and more sentences to make himself/herself clearer and his/her original ideas better understood by others and by oneself as well.

Thus, the person who listens to the words can never tell what really is behind them. Indeed, he/she can never know for sure the original intention of the speaker. At best, the listener can only interpret each particular word according to its context in the sentence and to his/her own private background and current state of mind.

In fact, the speaker is also a listener to his/her own words. After all, he/she has the opportunity to examine his/her own words when they are about to be said and check whether they indeed fit and reflect well his/her own original thoughts and intentions.

For example, when the speaker tells his/her own dream to someone else, he/she also listens to his/her own words, which in turn invoke memories that may have been locked deep within his/her own subconsciousness. Because these memories come from the same subconscious origin as the dream itself, they may indeed reveal the true deep emotions behind the dream and the correct interpretation of the symbols in it.

The above model for using and interpreting words is inductive in the sense that a word can be comprehended only by a concrete example. For example, the term "bed" may remind one of the original feeling of lying down on his/her original childhood bed. Using induction, one can then extend this concrete memory to the abstract concept of the general bed. Furthermore, using deduction, one can then check whether another object is a bed as well: in fact, if it is indeed possible to lie down on it and have the same feeling, then it must be a bed as well.

Thinking may be modeled in a similar way as well. In this model, however, the projection is no longer from background to foreground but rather from subconsciousness to consciousness. For example, one often sees an object from the corner of the eye without noticing it or being aware of its existence. The view of such an object may then be considered as mere useless noise. Only once the presence of the object becomes sufficiently important or relevant does it become conscious: a brain cell is allocated to store its image for further use. In other words, the image that is viewed is projected from subconsciousness to consciousness for practical (rational) use. Once this image is no longer needed, it is removed from the brain and forgotten, to release the brain cell for future use.

This is probably why introducing new words may help one to think. After all, once a concept is named by a specific word, it is transferred from subconsciousness right into consciousness and placed in a particular brain cell to be used later to indicate the original concept whenever needed. This is how even a vague concept such as beauty or glory becomes explicit and ready to use: a concrete feeling or memory that represents it well is stored in the brain cell that is referred to by the word “beauty” or “glory.” This is indeed an inductive process. Deduction is then used to check whether a given object is beautiful or not: if it invokes the same feeling as in the brain cell named “beauty,” then it must indeed be considered as beautiful.

The above discussion may also explain why art is so important to the human mind. After all, it lets us have a rare glimpse of pure (positive) emotions that may be hidden in the subconsciousness and raise them for a short while to the consciousness. This is how one may gain new insight about his/her own personality, particularly the part in it that may appreciate and enjoy the sense of aesthetics.

The brain can thus be modeled in terms of a mathematical graph, in which each node stands for a brain cell. This way, the process of thinking may be considered as a discrete flow in this graph. This discrete flow of information is likely to satisfy the same laws as does a standard continuous physical flow: namely, conservation laws, the laws of thermodynamics, the increasing-entropy condition, and the flow equations in their discrete version.

## 4.14 Objects in Kant’s Theory

The above ideas are also related to Kant’s theory about objects and their interpretation. Indeed, in Kant’s theory, an object can be comprehended or recognized by us humans not in term of any inherent property or characteristic but only in terms of its functions that are relevant to us. For example, the term “chair” is interpreted as a piece of furniture used to sit upon. After all, this is the only purpose of a chair, or its only useful function.

This observation is particularly helpful in mathematical objects in formal mathematical languages. Indeed, as discussed below, mathematical objects are best described, comprehended, and indeed implemented not in terms of data or storage but rather in terms of their functions that characterize them and make them what they really are and relate them to other objects as well. This is indeed why object-oriented programming is so useful not only to implement complex mathematical objects but also to have a better understanding of their deep nature.

Thus, we are now ready to extend the points made so far about natural human languages to formal languages as well. This is indeed the start of object-oriented programming.

## 4.15 Formal Languages

Unlike natural languages, formal languages (such as programming languages or mathematics) are completely objective and have no room for any personal interpretation. Indeed, formal objects are defined uniquely in mathematical terms, possibly using preliminary axioms or lower-level objects that have already been defined earlier. This inductive definition method is discussed below in some more detail.

Although a formal object can be interpreted intuitively from a few different points of view, they must all coincide mathematically and give mathematically equivalent properties and functions. Indeed, the object may be implemented on the computer in several different ways, some more efficient than others. Still, all these potential implementations must be mathematically equivalent to each other and satisfy the original mathematical axioms and properties that make the object what it is.

Thus, to think about a formal object means to realize its mathematical nature: the properties and functions that make it what it is. The best way to do this is to implement the object on the computer. Indeed, this way one may realize the formal structure of the object, including the data it requires and the relations and interactions among them. Choosing a suitable data structure may be particularly helpful to interpret the nature of the object correctly. Furthermore, the properties of the object are best understood once implemented in efficient computer functions, which form an integral part of the implementation of the object.

In summary, thanks to their objective mathematical nature, formal languages are much more stable than natural human languages. Still, even formal languages may require some “atom” objects that are defined only implicitly in terms of elementary axioms. ## 4.16 Inductive Definition and Interpretation

In both natural languages and formal languages, arbitrarily complex objects can be defined and interpreted using lower-level objects that have already been defined earlier or will be defined shortly. This process actually uses mathematical induction. Indeed, to define a highly complex object, one assumes that all less-complex objects have already been defined properly. This way, the definition of the present object may well use any lower-level object.

The above approach is actually the basis for every language, may it be formal or natural. Indeed, the dictionary definition of any word uses words that are assumed to be already well defined and understood by the reader.

The above assumption can actually be viewed as an induction hypothesis. Indeed, assuming that lower-level objects are well understood, they can indeed be used to produce a rather clear and sensible interpretation of the higher-level object that is currently defined.

Thanks to the above assumption or hypothesis, the definition takes a rather transparent and elegant form. Indeed, there is no need to bother with the detailed definitions of the lower-level objects. After all, they are assumed to be well understood. Therefore, they can be used as complete (diamond) objects, allowing full attention to be given to the features of the currently defined object.
