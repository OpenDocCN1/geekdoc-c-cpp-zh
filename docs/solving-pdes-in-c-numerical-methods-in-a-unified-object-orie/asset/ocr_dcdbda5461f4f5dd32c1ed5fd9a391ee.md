# ### 4.21 Lacan: Mind Is Language

Lacan’s theory proposes that the mind of a human being is actually identical to the language that he/she uses. Indeed, in order to think about an object, one must first give it a name. Conversely, in order to give an object a name, one must first have some idea about it. Thus, the terminology used to refer to the object actually makes it present in one’s mind. For example, to be able to think about a general chair (rather than a concrete chair), one must introduce the word “chair.” Conversely, to understand the word “chair,” one must be familiar with the general concept of chair. This is why an animal, which lacks language, can think only of a concrete chair rather than the general concept of chair.

The above makes sense not only in natural languages but also in formal languages such as mathematics. Here, however, the name that is given to the object must also have a mathematical meaning to characterize the object in terms of relations or axioms. For example, the term “vector” means that arithmetic operations can be carried out with commutative and distributive laws.

In Lacan’s theory, one can think about an abstract concept only once he/she has a word for it. For example, one can think about freedom only once he/she has the word “freedom” in his/her vocabulary. Similarly, the mathematician can think about a vector thanks to his/her familiarity with the term “vector,” or with the mathematical properties that characterize it uniquely and make it what it is: an object that can undergo linear arithmetic operations. The object-oriented implementation may also contribute to a better understanding of the mathematical object by giving the properties and axioms that characterize it a more concrete form in terms of a data structure and computer functions.

# ### 4.22 Language and Mind

In Wittgenstein’s theory, language only contains subjective pointers or references to the ideas and concepts in one’s mind. Furthermore, language may refer only to the rational and abstract part in consciousness. The rest of the human mind, including the subconsciousness, can be referred to only by symbolic views as in dreams rather than by words.

In this respect, a word in a natural human language can be compared to a piece of classical music or modern (rather than realistic) art. Indeed, just as a piece of modern art may introduce a new insight or uncover a hidden emotion, a word can also invoke a memory that is well-embedded in deep consciousness or even subconsciousness. For example, even a prosaic word like “table” that refers to the abstract notion of the general table must highlight the subjective memory of some concrete table from early childhood.

In Lacan’s theory, on the other hand, language is mind. More precisely, language is the rational and abstract part of consciousness. In fact, an abstract notion in one’s consciousness not only can, but also must, be represented by some word to enable one to think about it or to relate it to other concepts as well.
