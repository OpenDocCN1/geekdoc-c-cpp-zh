In the beginning of the chapter there is an attempt to highlight the relationship between programming languages and natural human languages and to show how the development of computer languages reflects the development over the centuries of a better understanding of the human mind and how it works.

Indeed, programming languages did not develop out of nowhere; although their development was motivated by technological advances, the way they emerged and developed is intrinsically related to the way our minds work, to the way we communicate to each other and to ourselves, to the way we learn about the world, and to the way we use our own minds and memory to obtain, process, and retrieve information.

For this purpose, the discussion below contains an attempt to give some philosophical background for vague terms such as mind, soul, and language. In particular, it is argued that the soul of a human being is just the collection of his/her desires, as was indeed indicated by Spinoza. Furthermore, the relation between mind and language is described in terms of a projection from mind onto language, as was indeed indicated by Wittgenstein. With this interpretation, the difference between formal and natural languages is clearer: words in a formal language are interpreted inductively using lower-level words that have already been interpreted, whereas words in a natural language are interpreted intuitively from one’s associations and memories.

## 4.1 Wittgenstein: Limits of Human Language

The great philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein studied the limits of any natural human language. He realized that there are ideas that can’t be expressed in words. For example, the most accurate report about the topography of a landscape would never express its beauty. This is why Wittgenstein said that there are things about which nothing can or should be said.

Words are inherently vague. Indeed, to explain or define or interpret a word, one must use other, more elementary words. Furthermore, to explain these words, one must in turn use yet more elementary words. By continuing this process, one must eventually use extremely elementary words that bear no explanation whatsoever. All that can be said about these “atom” words is that they give some indications or hints, and are therefore useful to explain more complex words.

In Aristotle’s theory, an atom word only stands for the nature of an object: the properties or relations (or functions, or axioms) that characterize it and make it what it is. This is indeed the approach used in this book. In Plato’s theory, on the other hand, each atom word must have a “god” to represent it objectively and give it meaning and reasoning. In monotheism, on the other hand, people may believe in one word that is the source of all the atom words. They refer to this word as “God.”

Below we discuss the relation of Wittgenstein’s theory to Plato’s and Aristotle’s theories in some more detail.

## 4.2 Wittgenstein, Plato, and Aristotle

In Plato’s theory, a general abstract object must have a “god” to represent it. For example, the general stone is represented by the god of stones. After all, there is no general stone; only individual concrete stones exist. In fact, the god of stone only stands for the idea of the general stone.

In Aristotle’s theory, on the other hand, a general abstract object is defined in terms of its nature or the features that characterize it and make it what it is. For example, the nature of stone is to be heavy. The nature of the machine is to do work required by man. The nature of the computer is to perform calculations required by man. The nature of a soldier is to obey orders from his commander. By following the spirit of this theory, one could also say that the nature of man is to be free and to take moral orders from God only, such as the Ten Commandments.

In Wittgenstein’s theory too, a general abstract concept cannot be interpreted exactly in words. After all, the only exact interpretation of the concept is in the very idea (or “god,” or nature) behind it, which cannot be expressed in words.

The modern novelist Clarice Lispector realized that words can never capture the sense of being at a unique moment in the present:

> “I have only words to give... active words to form sentences with an extra hidden meaning... Don’t read my words one by one—just float above them to read between the lines... My book contains no words—it is just a question or a silent photograph... Reality is beyond me... I’m in love with facts, but the only fact I know is to feel, think, or write... Truth is an internal touch... Life has no word to tell its meaning... My book is incomplete—the answer is still missing...” (free interpretation).

Below we discuss Wittgenstein’s theory from yet another point of view. The observations made below will be used later not only in the discussion about human languages but also in the discussion about formal programming languages (including object-oriented languages) as well.

## 4.3 Deduction–Induction

To explain an abstract concept, one may want to use concrete examples. Unfortunately, an example is relevant to a special case only and can’t express the original concept in its most general form.

The process of constructing concrete examples from the original concept is called deduction. The process of using these examples to illustrate the original concept and have a better idea about it is called induction.

The entire deduction–induction process may help in understanding the original concept better. Unfortunately, since each example is limited to a special case only, this process is inherently inaccurate and incomplete. Still, the deduction–induction process may be the best way to have some idea about highly abstract concepts.

In summary, any interpretation is inherently inaccurate, because it must use either vague words or limited examples. The only way to refer to a concept is to say the unique word that refers to it in the given language. Any synonym or interpretation would be inaccurate. In fact, due to the lack of a complete interpretation, even this very word is only a symbol that indicates or hints at the original concept.

It is only an illusion that we understand the word that we are saying. The truth is that at best we only get from it some indication or impression about the concept that it is supposed to stand for. In fact, this indication is highly subjective and relative: it depends on the person who uses the word. This is why different people may have different interpretations of the same word, based on their different backgrounds. This is also why misunderstandings often occur between people with different backgrounds, even when they do speak the same language.

## 4.4 Words

A word is only a tool to raise memories that are associated with it from one’s subconsciousness to one’s consciousness. A word is, thus, a mere “pointer” to data stored deep in one’s mind. It is only a trigger to invoke emotions or memories from one’s background that are related to it or indicated by it.

Thus, speaking and indeed thinking are actually emotional processes. Each word or thought invokes certain emotions, which stem from one’s reaction to it. This is why thoughts, including interpretation of words, are highly personal and subjective.

Once said and/or interpreted in one’s mind, the word draws one’s attention to a subset of memories and changes his/her emotional state.

This is why a computer can never talk or write or use any human language: it has no emotions and lacks any drive to survive. A human, on the other hand, is controlled by the drive to live and survive, including the desire to love and be loved (Darwin’s theory). Therefore, each thought that makes sense gives him/her satisfaction and peace of mind and makes him/her happy. This, however, is only an illusion. The sense of happiness or pleasure only means a better chance to survive.

Below we use the above discussion to explain what a human being really is in philosophical terms.

## 4.5 Human

A human is thus a collection of memories that are stored in the subconscious part of his/her mind and are sometimes raised to the consciousness by thoughts or words. The thought may change one’s emotional state in a way that is helpful in terms of survival. For example, it may cause a sense of satisfaction if it makes sense and improves the chances of survival. This mechanism is what one might call soul.

In fact, a human being is more his/her soul than his/her body. Indeed, the mechanism of thoughts is what makes the brain work, even in sleep. Once there are no thoughts, the human being is dead. Thus, a human is his/her soul, or flow of thoughts.

In summary, the human is just the collection of his/her desires (as was indeed indicated by the philosopher Baruch Spinoza). This is why, throughout his/her life, one always wants something. Only when he/she dies, one wants nothing more.
