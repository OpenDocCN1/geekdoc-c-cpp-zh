In this chapter, we present the concept of object-oriented programming and use it in the graph-coloring and triangle-coloring problems. In fact, the concept of object-oriented programming proves to be most helpful in the implementation of mathematical objects required in numerical methods, including graphs, sparse matrices, and unstructured meshes.

For this purpose, however, we first introduce some philosophical ideas about natural human languages, which may prove to be most relevant to formal languages such as programming languages and mathematics as well.

In the beginning of the chapter there is an attempt to highlight the relationship between programming languages and natural human languages and to show how the development of computer languages reflects the development over the centuries of a better understanding of the human mind and how it works.

Indeed, programming languages did not develop out of nowhere; although their development was motivated by technological advances, the way they emerged and developed is intrinsically related to the way our minds work, to the way we communicate to each other and to ourselves, to the way we learn about the world, and to the way we use our own minds and memory to obtain, process, and retrieve information.

For this purpose, the discussion below contains an attempt to give some philosophical background for vague terms such as mind, soul, and language. In particular, it is argued that the soul of a human being is just the collection of his/her desires, as was indeed indicated by Spinoza. Furthermore, the relation between mind and language is described in terms of a projection from mind onto language, as was indeed indicated by Wittgenstein. With this interpretation, the difference between formal and natural languages is clearer: words in a formal language are interpreted inductively using lower-level words that have already been interpreted, whereas words in a natural language are interpreted intuitively from one’s associations and memories.

# 4.1 Wittgenstein: Limits of Human Language

The great philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein studied the limits of any natural human language. He realized that there are ideas that can’t be expressed in words. For example, the most accurate report about the topography of a landscape would never express its beauty. This is why Wittgenstein said that there are things about which nothing can or should be said.

Words are inherently vague. Indeed, to explain or define or interpret a word, one must use other, more elementary words. Furthermore, to explain these words, one must in turn use yet more elementary words. By continuing this process, one must eventually use extremely elementary words that bear no explanation whatsoever. All that can be said about these “atom” words is that they give some indications or hints, and are therefore useful to explain more complex words.

In Aristotle’s theory, an atom word only stands for the nature of an object: the properties or relations (or functions, or axioms) that characterize it and make it what it is. This is indeed the approach used in this book. In Plato’s theory, on the other hand, each atom word must have a “god” to represent it objectively and give it meaning and reasoning. In monotheism, on the other hand, people may believe in one word that is the source of all the atom words. They refer to this word as “God.”
