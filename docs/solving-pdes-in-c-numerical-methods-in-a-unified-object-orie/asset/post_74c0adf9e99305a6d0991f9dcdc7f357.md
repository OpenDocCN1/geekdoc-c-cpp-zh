
The above formula tells us how the elements $B_{i,j}$ should be defined. Indeed, the $i$-th degree of freedom of $p_q$, $p_q^{(i)}$, is obtained from a partial derivative of $p_q$ at one of the corners (or edge midpoints, or side midpoints) of the reference tetrahedron $T$. Because a partial derivative is a linear operator, it is actually applied to each individual monomial in $p_q$, and evaluated at the relevant corner (or edge midpoint, or side midpoint) of $T$. Then, the contributions from the different monomials are multiplied by the corresponding (unknown) coefficients in $\tilde{x}$ and summed up. Thus, an element in $B$ must be a particular partial derivative of a particular monomial, evaluated at a particular corner, edge midpoint, or side midpoint of $T$.

More precisely, for $0 \le j < 56$, let us substitute
$j \leftarrow \hat{j}_{i,j,k}$,
where $(i, j, k) \in T^{(5)}$. Furthermore, for the first 10 equations in the above linear system ($0 \le i < 10$), let us substitute
$i \leftarrow \hat{l}_{l,m,n}$,
where $(l, m, n) \in T^{(2)}$. This way, the degree of freedom $p_q^{(\hat{l}_{l,m,n})}$ is the value of the $(n, m, l)$th partial derivative of $p_q$ at the origin $(0,0,0)$. Thus, $B_{\hat{l}_{l,m,n}, \hat{j}_{i,j,k}}$ must be the value of the $(n, m, l)$th partial derivative of the monomial $x^k y^j z^i$ at the origin:

$$
B_{\hat{l}_{l,m,n}, \hat{j}_{i,j,k}} \equiv \begin{cases} i! j! k! & \text{if } i = l \text{ and } j = m \text{ and } k = n, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Furthermore, in the next 10 equations in the above linear system, the partial derivatives are evaluated at the corner $(1,0,0)$ rather than at the origin. Therefore, the degree of freedom $p_q^{(10+\hat{l}_{l,m,n})}$ is the $(n, m, l)$th partial derivative of $p_q$ at $(1,0,0)$, so $B_{10+\hat{l}_{l,m,n}, \hat{j}_{i,j,k}}$ must be defined as the $(n, m, l)$th partial derivative of the monomial $x^k y^j z^i$ at $(1,0,0)$:

$$
B_{10+\hat{l}_{l,m,n}, \hat{j}_{i,j,k}} \equiv \begin{cases} i! j! \cdot \frac{k!}{(k-n)!} & \text{if } i = l \text{ and } j = m \text{ and } k \ge n, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Similarly, in the next 10 equations, the partial derivatives are evaluated at $(0,1,0)$, yielding the definitions

$$
B_{20+\hat{l}_{l,m,n}, \hat{j}_{i,j,k}} \equiv \begin{cases} i! k! \cdot \frac{j!}{(j-m)!} & \text{if } i = l \text{ and } j \ge m \text{ and } k = n, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Similarly, in the next 10 equations, the partial derivatives are evaluated at $(0,0,1)$, yielding the definitions

$$
B_{30+\hat{l}_{l,m,n}, \hat{j}_{i,j,k}} \equiv \begin{cases} j! k! \cdot \frac{i!}{(i-l)!} & \text{if } i \ge l \text{ and } j = m \text{ and } k = n, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

This completes the definition of the first 40 rows in $B$, indexed from 0 to 39.

The next two equations are obtained from the two normal derivatives (the $y$- and $z$-partial derivatives) at the edge midpoint $(1/2,0,0)$:

$$
B_{40,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 2^{-k} & \text{if } j = 1 \text{ and } i = 0, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

$$
B_{41,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 2^{-k} & \text{if } j = 0 \text{ and } i = 1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Similarly, the next two equations are obtained from the two normal derivatives (the $x$- and $z$-partial derivatives) at the edge midpoint $(0,1/2,0)$:

$$
B_{42,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 2^{-j} & \text{if } k = 1 \text{ and } i = 0, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

$$
B_{43,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 2^{-j} & \text{if } k = 0 \text{ and } i = 1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Similarly, the next two equations are obtained from the two normal derivatives (the $x$- and $y$-partial derivatives) at the edge midpoint $(0,0,1/2)$:

$$
B_{44,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 2^{-i} & \text{if } k = 1 \text{ and } j = 0, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

$$
B_{45,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 2^{-i} & \text{if } k = 0 \text{ and } j = 1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Furthermore, the next two equations are obtained from the two normal derivatives (the $z$-partial derivative and the sum of the $x$- and $y$-partial derivatives, divided by $\sqrt{2}$) at the edge midpoint $(1/2,1/2,0)$:

$$
B_{46,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 2^{-j-k} & \text{if } i = 1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

$$
B_{47,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} \frac{(j + k)2^{-j-k+1}}{\sqrt{2}} & \text{if } i = 0, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Similarly, the next two equations are obtained from the two normal derivatives (the $y$-partial derivative and the sum of the $x$- and $z$-partial derivatives, divided by $\sqrt{2}$) at the edge midpoint $(1/2,0,1/2)$:

$$
B_{48,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 2^{-i-k} & \text{if } j = 1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

$$
B_{49,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} \frac{(i + k)2^{-i-k+1}}{\sqrt{2}} & \text{if } j = 0, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Similarly, the next two equations are obtained from the two normal derivatives (the $x$-partial derivative and the sum of the $y$- and $z$-partial derivatives, divided by $\sqrt{2}$) at the edge midpoint $(0,1/2,1/2)$:

$$
B_{50,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 2^{-i-j} & \text{if } k = 1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

$$
B_{51,\hat{i},j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} \frac{(i + j)2^{-i-j+1}}{\sqrt{2}} & \text{if } k = 0, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

The final four equations are obtained from evaluating the normal derivative at the side midpoints. The next equation is obtained from the normal derivative (the $z$-partial derivative) at the side midpoint $(1/3,1/3,0)$:

$$
B_{52,\hat{j},i,j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 3^{j-k} & \text{if } i=1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Similarly, the next equation is obtained from the normal derivative (the $y$-partial derivative) at the side midpoint $(1/3,0,1/3)$:

$$
B_{53,\hat{j},i,j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 3^{i-k} & \text{if } j=1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Similarly, the next equation is obtained from the normal derivative (the $x$-partial derivative) at the side midpoint $(0,1/3,1/3)$:

$$
B_{54,\hat{j},i,j,k} \equiv \begin{cases} 3^{i-j} & \text{if } k=1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

The final equation is obtained from the normal derivative (the sum of the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-partial derivatives, divided by $\sqrt{3}$) at the side midpoint $(1/3,1/3,1/3)$:

$$
B_{55,\hat{j},i,j,k} \equiv \frac{(i + j + k) 3^{-i-j-k+1}}{\sqrt{3}}.
$$

This completes the definition of the matrix $B$.

In order to solve the above linear system for the unknown vector $\tilde{x}$, one can use three possible approaches. The direct approach is to calculate the inverse matrix $B^{-1}$ explicitly (as in Section 3.20) to obtain $\tilde{x}$ as the $q$th column in $B^{-1}$. A more indirect approach is to solve the original linear system iteratively by a Krylov-subspace method, such as GMRES [62]. Finally, one may also multiply the original linear system by the transpose matrix $B^t$ to obtain the so-called normal equations

$$
B^t B \tilde{x} = B^t I^{(q)},
$$

which can be solved by preconditioned conjugate gradients [56].

## 28.3 Functions in the Mesh

Consider now a tetrahedron $t$ in the three-dimensional mesh $\mathcal{M}$, with vertices (or corners) denoted by $\vec{k}$, $\vec{l}$, $\vec{m}$, and $\vec{n}$ in the three-dimensional Cartesian space. In this case, we write

$$
t = (\vec{k}, \vec{l}, \vec{m}, \vec{n}),
$$

where the order of vertices in this notation is determined arbitrarily in advance.

A function in $t$ has 56 degrees of freedom: its value and the values of its first and second partial derivatives at the four corners, its two normal derivatives at the six edge midpoints, and its normal derivatives at the four side midpoints. The values of these degrees of freedom determine the function uniquely. In a basis function in $t$, for example, only one of these degrees of freedom has a nonzero value: the value 1.

The indexing of these degrees of freedom is as in the reference tetrahedron $T$ above. More precisely, it is as in the rows of the matrix $B$ in Section 28.2. In particular, the index of a degree of freedom corresponding to the value of the function at a corner of $t$ is denoted as the “modulus” of that corner:

| Corner | Modulus |
| :--- | :--- |
| $|\vec{k}|$ | 0 |
| $|\vec{l}|$ | 10 |
| $|\vec{m}|$ | 20 |
| $|\vec{n}|$ | 30 |

Let $D_t$ be the affine transformation that maps $T$ onto $t$ and let $S_t$ be its Jacobian (as in Section 27.1). The basis functions $p_i$ defined in the reference tetrahedron $T$ in Section 28.2 can now be composed with $D_t^{-1}$ to form the following functions in $t$:

$$
r_{i,t} \equiv p_i \circ D_t^{-1}, \quad (0 \le i < 56).
$$

Unfortunately, these functions are not basis functions, as they may have more than one nonzero partial derivative at a corner of $t$ or normal derivative at an edge midpoint in $t$. Still, they may be used to form the required basis functions in $t$.

## 28.4 Corner Functions

From the above definitions and the chain rule, it follows that if the gradient of $p_i$ vanishes at some point $(x, y, z) \in T$, then the gradient of $r_{i,t}$ vanishes at $D_t(x, y, z) \in t$. In particular, if the gradient of $p_i$ vanishes at some corner of $T$, then the gradient of $r_{i,t}$ vanishes at the corresponding corner of $t$. Furthermore, the same is true for the Hessian (the $3 \times 3$ symmetric matrix of second partial derivatives). As a consequence, we can define the so-called “corner” functions in $t$:

$$
R_{i,t} \equiv r_{i,t}, \quad i = 0, 10, 20, 30.
$$

Note that these functions have the value 1 at one corner and 0 at the other three corners. Furthermore, their gradient and Hessian vanish at all four corners. Still, they are not basis functions, because their normal derivatives do not necessarily vanish at edge and side midpoints that lie by the corner at which they don’t vanish. Later on, they’ll be modified to be proper basis functions.

## 28.5 Gradient-Related Corner Functions

Furthermore, let us define the corner functions associated with the first partial derivatives in $t$. For this purpose, let $\bar{i}$ be some corner of $t$, and define the so-called gradient-related corner functions by

\begin{pmatrix} R_{\bar{i}+\hat{i}_{0,0,1},t} \\ R_{\bar{i}+\hat{i}_{0,1,0},t} \\ R_{\bar{i}+\hat{i}_{1,0,0},t} \end{pmatrix} \equiv S_t \begin{pmatrix} r_{\bar{i}+\hat{i}_{0,0,1},t} \\ r_{\bar{i}+\hat{i}_{0,1,0},t} \\ r_{\bar{i}+\hat{i}_{1,0,0},t} \end{pmatrix}
$$
