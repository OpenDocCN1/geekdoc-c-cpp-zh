## The Helmholtz Equation: Error Estimate

Error estimates for the Helmholtz equation in a simple model case show that the standard finite-difference scheme may be too expensive in terms of time and storage, particularly in the three-dimensional case. This result may be interpreted to indicate that the finite-difference scheme is indeed inferior to adaptive finite-element algorithms.

The nonlinear Helmholtz equation is particularly useful to model the backscattering phenomenon in wave propagation. This has all sorts of applications in laser technology, such as laser surgery, laser pulses sent towards the atmosphere and outer space, etc.

```
|u|^2u = (ℜ²u + ℑ²u)u.
```

where ℜu is the real part of the unknown function u, and ℑ is the imaginary part. Furthermore, by rewriting the original PDE as a system of PDEs in terms of the real unknown functions ℜu and ℑu, one can go ahead and discretize to obtain a differentiable algebraic system of equations whose Jacobian can be calculated and used in the Newton iteration (Section 9.14). This approach is introduced in [4] in the one-dimensional case with a finite-difference discretization, and also detailed in Section 27.5 below in the three-dimensional case with the finite-element discretization.

As a matter of fact, the three-dimensional case is essentially more difficult than the one-dimensional case. Indeed, as discussed in [31], the one-dimensional case is subcritical, so the equation can actually be well-approximated by the timelike Schrödinger equation. The multidimensional case, on the other hand, is essentially different: the two-dimensional case is critical, and the three-dimensional case is even supercritical, yielding a much more challenging problem. Still, the one-dimensional case is worth studying to develop error estimates that are relevant in the three-dimensional case as well.

### 25.2 The One-Dimensional Case

In one dimension, the nonlinear Helmholtz equation is actually an ODE:

−u′′(x)+Ku(x)+K1|u(x)|2u(x)=0

in the unit interval

x∈(0,1),

where K and K1 are given complex parameters with nonnegative real parts. (Note that the parameter K used here plays the role of the coefficient −4π2K2 used in Chapter 22, Section 22.2.) To make the problem well posed, mixed complex boundary conditions are imposed at the endpoints x=0 and x=1:

−u′(0)+√Ku(0)=2√K and u′(1)+√Ku(1)=0.

Note that, when K is real and negative, √K=i√|K|.

The above boundary-value problem in the finite interval [0,1] is obtained in [4] from a problem defined in the semi-infinite interval [0,∞) by truncating it at 1 and imposing artificial boundary conditions at 1 to allow a periodic solution. In the original problem in [0,∞), the boundary condition at ∞ is that u remains bounded. However, this problem can also be viewed as an initial-value problem in the timelike variable x≥0. This way, one can use the method of Section 6.15 above to find a suitable second initial condition about the initial derivative u′(0) with which u remains bounded for as long as possible and is therefore either asymptotic in the sense that it converges (to 0) as x→∞ or at least periodic as x→∞.

Still, here we stick to a boundary-value problem in a finite interval as in [4], which can be extended to a PDE in a finite two-dimensional (and indeed three-dimensional) domain as in [3] and in subsequent chapters in this book.

The linear case K1=0 in the above boundary-value problem in the interval [0,1] has the unique solution

u(x)=exp(−√Kx).

In the nonlinear case K1≠0, on the other hand, as well as for problems with non-homogeneous boundary conditions or right-hand side, no closed-form solution is available. In such cases, a discretization scheme must be used to solve the equation numerically rather than analytically. The original ODE is then approximated in the form of the algebraic system

A(v)=b,

where N is the number of points in the discrete grid, b is a given N-dimensional (possibly complex-valued) vector, v is the N-dimensional (possibly complex-valued) vector of unknowns, and A is a given operator. For example, in the linear case K1=0, A is an N×N (possibly complex-valued) matrix.

### 25.3 The Error and the Truncation Error

Let w be the N-dimensional vector obtained by sampling u(x) at the grid points. The error is then defined as the N-dimensional vector that is the difference between w and v:

error=w−v.

The truncation error, on the other hand, is the N-dimensional vector obtained from calculating the residual at w:

truncation error = A(w) − b,

or, in the linear case K₁ = 0,

truncation error = A w − b = A(w − v) = A · error.

### 25.4 Finite Differences

It is common to use a uniform grid of meshsize h = 1/(N − 1):

{0, h, 2h, 3h, …, (N − 1)h = 1}.

For example, in the symmetric second-order difference scheme, the second derivative of u(x) is approximated by a finite difference over the three consecutive gird points j − i, j, and j + 1:

−u''(j h) ≐ h⁻²(2uⱼ − uⱼ₋₁ − uⱼ₊₁).

At the endpoints j = 0 and j = N − 1, the dummy grid points j − 1 = −1 and j + 1 = N are used only momentarily; indeed, they are immediately eliminated by also using a symmetric second-order discrete approximation of the boundary conditions:

(u_N − u_{N−2})/(2h) + √K u_{N−1} = 0

and

−(u₁ − u₋₁)/(2h) + √K u₀ = 2√K.

Thus, the coefficient matrix A takes the tridiagonal form

h⁻² ⎛ 2 + K h² + 2h√K   −2
      −1               2 + K h²   −1
                         −1      2 + K h²   −1
                                      ⋱   ⋱   ⋱
                                               −1  2 + K h²   −1
                                                      −2  2 + K h² + 2h√K ⎞

Furthermore, upon dividing the first and last equations in the above linear system by 2, A takes the form of the following complex-symmetric matrix:

h⁻² ⎛ 1 + K h²/2 + h√K   −1
      −1                2 + K h²   −1
                         −1      2 + K h²   −1
                                      ⋱   ⋱   ⋱
                                               −1  2 + K h²   −1
                                                      −1  1 + K h²/2 + h√K ⎞

In [3], [4], [31], and [32], a fourth-order compact difference scheme is introduced. However, it is not obvious how to extend this scheme to the three-dimensional case later on. This is why we continue our study of the second-order difference scheme and assess its accuracy.
