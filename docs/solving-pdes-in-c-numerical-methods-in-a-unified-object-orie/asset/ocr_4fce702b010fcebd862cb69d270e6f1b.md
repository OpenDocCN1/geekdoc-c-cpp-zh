# 4.2 Wittgenstein, Plato, and Aristotle

In Plato’s theory, a general abstract object must have a "god" to represent it. For example, the general stone is represented by the god of stones. After all, there is no general stone; only individual concrete stones exist. In fact, the god of stone only stands for the idea of the general stone.

In Aristotle’s theory, on the other hand, a general abstract object is defined in terms of its nature or the features that characterize it and make it what it is. For example, the nature of stone is to be heavy. The nature of the machine is to do work required by man. The nature of the computer is to perform calculations required by man. The nature of a soldier is to obey orders from his commander. By following the spirit of this theory, one could also say that the nature of man is to be free and to take moral orders from God only, such as the Ten Commandments.

In Wittgenstein’s theory too, a general abstract concept cannot be interpreted exactly in words. After all, the only exact interpretation of the concept is in the very idea (or "god," or nature) behind it, which cannot be expressed in words.

The modern novelist Clarice Lispector realized that words can never capture the sense of being at a unique moment in the present:

> “I have only words to give... active words to form sentences with an extra hidden meaning... Don’t read my words one by one—just float above them to read between the lines... My book contains no words—it is just a question or a silent photograph... Reality is beyond me... I’m in love with facts, but the only fact I know is to feel, think, or write... Truth is an internal touch... Life has no word to tell its meaning... My book is incomplete—the answer is still missing...” (free interpretation).

Below we discuss Wittgenstein’s theory from yet another point of view. The observations made below will be used later not only in the discussion about human languages but also in the discussion about formal programming languages (including object-oriented languages) as well.

# 4.3 Deduction–Induction

To explain an abstract concept, one may want to use concrete examples. Unfortunately, an example is relevant to a special case only and can’t express the original concept in its most general form.

The process of constructing concrete examples from the original concept is called deduction. The process of using these examples to illustrate the original concept and have a better idea about it is called induction.

The entire deduction–induction process may help in understanding the original concept better. Unfortunately, since each example is limited to a special case only, this process is inherently inaccurate and incomplete. Still, the deduction–induction process may be the best way to have some idea about highly abstract concepts.
