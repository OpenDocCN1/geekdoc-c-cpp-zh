bed. Furthermore, using deduction, one can then check whether another object is a bed as well: in fact, if it is indeed possible to lie down on it and have the same feeling, then it must be a bed as well.

Thinking may be modeled in a similar way as well. In this model, however, the projection is no longer from background to foreground but rather from subconsciousness to consciousness. For example, one often sees an object from the corner of the eye without noticing it or being aware of its existence. The view of such an object may then be considered as mere useless noise. Only once the presence of the object becomes sufficiently important or relevant does it become conscious: a brain cell is allocated to store its image for further use. In other words, the image that is viewed is projected from subconsciousness to consciousness for practical (rational) use. Once this image is no longer needed, it is removed from the brain and forgotten, to release the brain cell for future use.

This is probably why introducing new words may help one to think. After all, once a concept is named by a specific word, it is transferred from subconsciousness right into consciousness and placed in a particular brain cell to be used later to indicate the original concept whenever needed. This is how even a vague concept such as beauty or glory becomes explicit and ready to use: a concrete feeling or memory that represents it well is stored in the brain cell that is referred to by the word “beauty” or “glory.” This is indeed an inductive process. Deduction is then used to check whether a given object is beautiful or not: if it invokes the same feeling as in the brain cell named “beauty,” then it must indeed be considered as beautiful.

The above discussion may also explain why art is so important to the human mind. After all, it lets us have a rare glimpse of pure (positive) emotions that may be hidden in the subconsciousness and raise them for a short while to the consciousness. This is how one may gain new insight about his/her own personality, particularly the part in it that may appreciate and enjoy the sense of aesthetics.

The brain can thus be modeled in terms of a mathematical graph, in which each node stands for a brain cell. This way, the process of thinking may be considered as a discrete flow in this graph. This discrete flow of information is likely to satisfy the same laws as does a standard continuous physical flow: namely, conservation laws, the laws of thermodynamics, the increasing-entropy condition, and the flow equations in their discrete version.

# 4.14  Objects in Kant’s Theory

The above ideas are also related to Kant’s theory about objects and their interpretation. Indeed, in Kant’s theory, an object can be comprehended or recognized by us humans not in term of any inherent property or characteristic but only in terms of its functions that are relevant to us. For example, the term “chair” is interpreted as a piece of furniture used to sit upon. After all, this is the only purpose of a chair, or its only useful function.

This observation is particularly helpful in mathematical objects in formal mathematical languages. Indeed, as discussed below, mathematical objects are best described, comprehended, and indeed implemented not in terms of data or storage but rather in terms of their functions that characterize them and make them what they really are and relate them to other objects as well. This is indeed why object-oriented programming is so useful not only to implement complex mathematical objects but also to have a better understanding of their deep nature.

Thus, we are now ready to extend the points made so far about natural human languages to formal languages as well. This is indeed the start of object-oriented programming.
