### 27.8 Numerical Results

It turns out that the adaptive-refinement algorithm in Section 26.8 is particularly efficient. Indeed, each level uses only a couple of thousands of degrees of freedom (nodes), even in the finest mesh.

In each level, linear finite elements are used to assemble the stiffness and mass matrices and form the coefficient matrix used in the Newton iteration in Section 27.7. Furthermore, in each level, 100 GMRES iterations (with the multigrid preconditioner) are used to provide the discrete solution $u$ on the current mesh. It turns out that the convergence factor of the multigrid preconditioner is about 0.85 on average; that is, the average factor by which the $l_2$-norm of the preconditioned residual is multiplied in each GMRES iteration is 0.85 or so.

We start with a few levels of adaptive refinement to form a fine mesh on which the linear problem ($K = -40, K_1 = 0$) is well approximated. Indeed, thanks to the fact that the solution is available in a closed form, the error is available as well. This error (in each level) is shown in Table 27.1.

In the next couple of levels, we turn to the nonlinear case. Indeed, $|K_1|$ increases from level to level (see Table 27.2). Furthermore, in this nonlinear case, we also replace the homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions at $y = 0, y = 1, z = 0,$ and $z = 1$ by mixed complex boundary conditions, as advised in [32]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_y + \sqrt{K}\,u &= 0 &&\text{if } y=1,\\
-u_y + \sqrt{K}\,u &= 0 &&\text{if } y=0,\\
u_z + \sqrt{K}\,u &= 0 &&\text{if } z=1,\\
-u_z + \sqrt{K}\,u &= 0 &&\text{if } z=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

In each of these levels, a few outer Newton iterations (Section 27.7) are performed. In each Newton iteration, 100 inner GMRES iterations (with the multigrid preconditioner) are used to reduce the $l_2$-norm of the preconditioned residual by at least six orders of magnitude.

## High-Order Finite Elements in Three Spatial Dimensions

So far, we have implemented linear finite elements only. The basis functions used in this scheme are linear in each individual finite element. In fact, they take the value 1 at most at one corner of each tetrahedron in the mesh, and vanish at all the other corners. This is why they are also called nodal basis functions: they take the value 1 at only one node in the mesh, and vanish at all the other nodes.

In this chapter, on the other hand, the solution of the PDE is approximated by piecewise nonlinear functions. In fact, the functions are polynomials of degree up to five in each individual tetrahedron, resulting in a much more accurate numerical scheme. However, the scheme is also much more complicated to formulate and implement. Fortunately, the present object-oriented framework proves once again to be most helpful in simplifying the programming considerably and in producing an elegant and useful code.

### 28.1 Polynomials of Degree Five

A polynomial of degree five in three variables can be written as

$$
p(x, y, z) = \sum_{0 \le i, j, k, i + j + k \le 5} a_{i, j, k} x^k y^j z^i
$$

where the $a_{i, j, k}$'s are some prescribed scalars. In order to specify this polynomial uniquely, it is sufficient to specify 56 degrees of freedom, say the value of $p$ and its first and second partial derivatives at the four corners of the reference tetrahedron ($10 \cdot 4 = 40$ degrees of freedom), the value of the two normal derivatives of $p$ at the six edge midpoints ($2 \cdot 6 = 12$ more degrees of freedom), and the value of the normal derivative of $p$ at the side midpoints (4 more degrees of freedom).

A basis function in the reference tetrahedron $T$ (Figure 27.1) is a polynomial $p_i(x, y, z)$ of degree five ($0 \le i < 56$) whose $i$-th degree of freedom is equal to 1, whereas all the other degrees of freedom vanish. Every polynomial of degree five $p(x, y, z)$ can be written uniquely as a linear combination of the basis functions:

$$
p(x, y, z) = \sum_{i=0}^{55} p^{(i)} p_i(x, y, z)
$$

or

$$
p \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{55} p^{(i)} p_i
$$

where the scalar $p^{(i)}$ is the $i$-th degree of freedom of $p$. In particular, the $i$-th basis function, $p_i$, has only one nonzero degree of freedom, $p_i^{(i)} = 1$:

$$
p_i^{(j)} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } j = i, \\ 0 & \text{if } j \neq i \end{cases}
$$

($0 \le j < 56$).

### 28.2 Basis Functions in the Reference Tetrahedron

The triplets of the form $(i, j, k)$ used to index the coefficients $a_{i, j, k}$ of a polynomial of degree 5, $p(x, y, z)$, form the discrete tetrahedron

$$
T^{(5)} \equiv \{(i, j, k) \mid 0 \le i, j, k, i + j + k \le 5\}.
$$

Let us order these triplets in the lexicographic order; that is, $(i, j, k)$ is prior to $(l, m, n)$ if either (a) $i < l$ or (b) $i = l$ and $j < m$ or (c) $i = l$ and $j = m$ and $k < n$. Let us use the index $0 \le \hat{i}_{i, j, k} < 56$ to index these triplets in this order. Using this index, we can form the 56-dimensional vector of coefficients $\bar{x}$:

$$
\bar{x}_{\hat{i}_{i, j, k}} \equiv a_{i, j, k}, \quad (i, j, k) \in T^{(5)}.
$$

The same approach can be used to order the triplets in the smaller discrete tetrahedron

$$
T^{(2)} \equiv \{(l, m, n) \mid 0 \le l, m, n, l + m + n \le 2\}.
$$

In this case, once the triplets of the form $(l, m, n)$ are ordered in the lexicographic order, the index $0 \le \hat{i}_{l, m, n} < 10$ is used to index them in this order.

To represent a basis function $p_q$ for some integer $0 \le q < 56$, we need to specify its coefficients $a_{i, j, k}$, or the components of $\bar{x}$. Unfortunately, these coefficients are rarely available explicitly. In order to find them, we must use every piece of information we may have about $p_q$, including its available degrees of freedom. More precisely, we must solve a linear system of algebraic equations, in which $\bar{x}$ is the vector of unknowns:

$$
B \bar{x} = I^{(q)}
$$

where $I^{(q)}$ is the $q$-th column of the identity matrix of order 56, and $B$ is the $56 \times 56$ matrix defined by the relations

$$
p_q^{(i)} = \sum_{j=0}^{55} B_{i, j} \bar{x}_j = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = q, \\ 0 & \text{if } i \neq q \end{cases}
$$

$0 \le i, q < 56$.
