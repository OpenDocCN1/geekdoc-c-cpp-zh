The result of this linearization is actually a convection-diffusion equation, with convection coefficient C(t, x) as in Figures 9.1–9.3. This problem may serve as an interesting model for the first linearized problem in the above Newton iteration.

As discussed at the end of Chapter 8, Section 4, large truncation errors may appear at grid points where the solution u(t, x) exhibits large variation. This problem is particularly relevant when adequacy is considered, because in this case the diffusion coefficient ε goes to zero together with the meshsize h and Δt, so there is no sufficient diffusion to smooth sharp variation. Therefore, the truncation error at these places can be as large as (Δt)⁻¹ + h⁻¹.

Fortunately, when Dirichlet, Neumann, or mixed boundary conditions are used, the Jordan matrix J used in Chapter 8, Section 3, is practically diagonal, so applying it to a vector is equivalent to multiplying its components by the corresponding main-diagonal elements. Furthermore, these main-diagonal elements are particularly small at grid points where u(t, x) exhibits large variation, such as shock fronts. Thus, one can show that the discretization error is small at least at grid points where u(t, x) is rather smooth.

Indeed, it can be seen in Section A.12 of the Appendix that J can be decomposed as the product

J = R E

of the orthogonal (norm-preserving) matrix R and the diagonal matrix E. Assume first that the convection coefficient C(t, x) is a shock wave as in Figure 9.3. Then, the main-diagonal elements in E have the small value e_min at grid points at the shock front and grow exponentially as one gets farther away from the shock front. Moreover, when one gets farther and farther away from the shock front, the solution u(t, x) approaches a constant value even more rapidly, so u_t(t, x) and u_x(t, x) (and, hence, also the truncation error) approach zero faster than the growth in the corresponding main-diagonal elements in E (see [67]).

Let E_i be the block in E that corresponds to the i th time level. This way, J_i = R_i E_i is the Jordan matrix for A_i, the matrix that contains the discrete spatial derivatives at the i th time level. Assume also that

ε = O(h) = O(Δt)

(that is, ε approaches zero at least as rapidly as h, and h approaches zero at least as rapidly as Δt). As discussed at the end of Section A.12 of the Appendix, in this case Δt E_i A_i E_i⁻¹ is strictly diagonally dominant, so its eigenvalues are well away from the origin, and the stability estimate in Chapter 8, Section 3, improves to read

‖ J A⁻¹ J⁻¹ ‖ = O(Δt)

(that is, ‖ J A⁻¹ J⁻¹ ‖ approaches zero at least as rapidly as Δt). By denoting the numerical solution by ũ and the solution of the linearized problem (in the grid) by u (as in Chapter 8, Section 4), we thus have
