Thinking about an object may also result in naming it in more accurate words that may be relevant in different contexts. This is why a natural language is also referred to often as a context-sensitive language.

The thinking process may also lead to realizing the influence of the object on other objects in different circumstances. This may lead to characterizing the original object not only in terms of its original properties but also in terms of its potential effects.

Still, although the thinking process may give the object better and better characterization in terms of properties or effects that may be relevant in different contexts, it may never lead to a completely objective definition. Indeed, every definition must use words, which are vague and imprecise as well. Thus, thinking about an object may at best improve one’s own private subjective interpretation, but never form a universal objective context-free interpretation.

# 4.13 Inductive Models for Talking, Listening, and Thinking

The above discussion may be used to introduce a model for the activity of one’s mind. In this model, one’s memories and experience lie within the private background of his/her mind, whereas one’s vocabulary lies within the foreground of one’s mind. When one talks, his/her private memories in the background of his/her mind translate into words in the foreground for others to hear and interpret.

Still, because words are inherently inaccurate, the translation (or projection, or mapping) from background to foreground is not one-to-one: two different ideas (or concepts, or thoughts) may be so close or similar to each other that both are expressed by the same word. Therefore, the mapping from background to foreground that produces one’s words is not always reversible: it may be impossible to recover one’s original thoughts from his/her own words. This is probably why one may often feel the need to use more and more words to form more and more sentences to make himself/herself clearer and his/her original ideas better understood by others and by oneself as well.

Thus, the person who listens to the words can never tell what really is behind them. Indeed, he/she can never know for sure the original intention of the speaker. At best, the listener can only interpret each particular word according to its context in the sentence and to his/her own private background and current state of mind.

In fact, the speaker is also a listener to his/her own words. After all, he/she has the opportunity to examine his/her own words when they are about to be said and check whether they indeed fit and reflect well his/her own original thoughts and intentions.

For example, when the speaker tells his/her own dream to someone else, he/she also listens to his/her own words, which in turn invoke memories that may have been locked deep within his/her own subconsciousness. Because these memories come from the same subconscious origin as the dream itself, they may indeed reveal the true deep emotions behind the dream and the correct interpretation of the symbols in it.

The above model for using and interpreting words is inductive in the sense that a word can be comprehended only by a concrete example. For example, the term "bed" may remind one of the original feeling of lying down on his/her original childhood bed.
