# SUMMARY

Increased glucose metabolism and uptake are characteristic of many tumors and use molecules to clinically diagnose and monitor cancer progression. In addition to cancer cells, the tumor microenvironment (TME) encompasses a wide range of stromal, innate, and adaptive immune cells. Cooperation and competition between these populations support tumor proliferation, progression, metastasis, and immune evasion. Cellular heterogeneity leads to metabolic heterogeneity where tumors are dependent not only on the TME cellular composition but also on cell states, location, and nutrient availability. In addition driving metabolic plasticity of cancer cells, altered nutrients and signals in the TME can lead to metabolic suppression of effector cells and regulate immune cells. Here we discuss how metabolic programming of cells within the TME promotes proliferation, progression, and metastasis. We discuss how metabolic heterogeneity may offer therapeutic opportunities to overcome immune suppression and augment immunotherapies.

# INTRODUCTION

Tumors require an abundance of energy for growth, proliferation, and metastasis. To sustain these processes, oncogenic genes drive diverse metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and other pathways that generate essential energy and biosynthetic precursors. It is apparent, however, that these pathways are not only for cell growth but for proliferative cells, including pro-tumor and anti-tumor immune cells. This shared demand for nutrients creates a competitive tumor microenvironment and immune suppression.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is composed of many cells that support or resist treatment. As the TME includes a diversity of cells, it is metabolically and highly active (Figure 1). Cells and resulting tumors populate various regions of the TME, in part through support cell populations seeking oxygen and nutrient supply in poorly vascularized or highly metabolically active areas. The TME is also shaped and altered by accumulation of waste products and inflammation, where nutrients are limited. Because nutrients are abundant on systemic and whole-body factors, type, location of the tumor within the primary tissue, host diet and nutritional scale will affect nutrient availability within the TME. Additionally, metabolic plasticity may occur as cells within the TME adapt to different nutrient depending availability. Metabolism within the TME supports proliferation of tumor cells but also in metastatic disease because metabolic processes influence each stage of the metabolic cycle within cancer cells and in the TME as normal cells escape and survive.

This complex landscape can be detrimental for treatment because targeting the growth or metastasis of cancer cells can also inhibit or enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapies. Conversely, new approaches must specifically target the immune cells in turn by interfering with their metabolic pathways. While, immunotherapies blocking PD-1 or CTLA-4 can activate anti-T cells in multiple cancer cell types, resistance to some therapies often develops after an initial response. The mechanisms of resistance are not well understood, but metabolic pathways are fundamentally involved in cell fate and cell differentiation, and immunotherapy.

Improvingotherapotherap and reducing resistance is of high priority, but it requires understanding how cells interact and interact with cancer cells in the TME, in regard to nutrient shared and resource utilization, to overcome metabolic suppression in the TME.

## METABOLIC HETEROGENEITY

Proliferating cells require abundant and diverse nutrients to support bioenergetic demands, growth and while maintaining cell functions or migratory properties. The fuels for these cells come from glucose, glycolysis, and lipolysis. Increased glucose uptake that occurs in hypoxia despite the presence of oxygen is referred to the “Warburg effect” or aerobic glycolysis. Indeed, glucose uptake in high tumors can cause extracellular levels of glucose reduced and elevated. Many factors contribute to the turning of tumors to undergo aerobic glycolysis, including oncogenic signaling or loss of tumor such as Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) or p53 mitochondrial alterations, regulation of glycolytic enzymes, or hypoxia signaling. These factors are caused by oxygen deprivation driving phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3Ks) and myelomethyl oxygen homolog (MYC), directly cell insulin-like growth factors that promote glycolysis. In addition hypoxia occurs during tumorigenesis or loss of VHL, the activation of glycolytic enzymes including GLUT1, hexokinase 1 (HK1), hexokinase 2 (HK2), and pyruvate kinase domain 1 (PKM2), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDHA) also reduces metabolic activity and ROS, which are exported regulated by oxidative stress pathways.

Beyond aerobic glycolysis, anaerobic metabolism requires a wide range of nutrients. The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) is essential for generation of adenine dinucleotide (NADPH) and ribose sugars which are needed for nucleotide synthesis, ATP production, and elimination of oxidative stress. Lipid and cholesterol are also used by cells to support tumors, which can be either taken up in the cells from free fatty acids or produced by fatty acid synthesis and de novo cholesterol synthesis.
