
One of the most basic theorems in classical recursion theory is the Friedberg-Mučnik Theorem ([28] and [109]) which asserts the existence of two incomparable r.e. Turing degrees. Since $\Pi^1_1$-sets are seen as analogs of r.e. sets in the higher setting, the following result shows that there is a fundamental difference between the two notions of reducibility.

**Theorem 2.4.4 (Spector [153]).** If $x, y \in \Pi^1_1$ and $y \notin \text{HYP}$, then $x \le_h y$.

Proof. We show that $\mathcal{O} \le_h y$. By Theorem 1.5.1, there is a recursive function $f$ such that $n \in \mathcal{O} \Leftrightarrow f(n) \in \mathcal{O}$. Since $y \notin \text{HYP}$, by Lemma 2.2.4, $\sup\{|f(n)|_y : n \in y\}$ is $\omega_1^{CK}$. Then
$n \in \mathcal{O} \Leftrightarrow (\exists m)(m \in y \land n \in \mathcal{O}_{f(m)})$
and
$n \notin \mathcal{O} \Leftrightarrow (\forall m)(m \in y \rightarrow n \notin \mathcal{O}_{f(m)})$.
By Lemma 2.2.3, $\mathcal{O} \le_h y$.

**Remark 2.4.5.** Theorem 2.4.4 prompted Kreisel to suggest that $h$-reduction was not the “correct” notion of reducibility for higher recursion theory. This led to the introduction of the notion of metarecursive reducibility in metarecursion theory. It is shown in Sacks [118] that there exist two $\Pi^1_1$-sets with incomparable metadegrees.

There is a tight link between $\omega_1^x$ and the $h$-degree of $x$, as illustrated in the following proposition:

**Proposition 2.4.6 (Spector [153]).** $x \le_h y \implies \omega_1^x \le \omega_1^y$.

Proof. Since $x \le_h y$, every $x$-recursive well-ordering relation $R \le \omega^2$ is $\Delta^1_1$ in $y$. By relativizing Proposition 1.5.6 to $y$, $R$ has order type $< \omega_1^y$. Hence $\omega_1^x \le \omega_1^y$.

The converse of Proposition 2.4.6 does not hold in general. However, there is an important partial result which characterizes $\omega_1^{CK}$.

**Theorem 2.4.7 (Sacks).** $\omega_1^x > \omega_1^{CK}$ if and only if $x \ge_h \mathcal{O}$.

We decompose the proof into two lemmas, with the following left as an exercise:

**Lemma 2.4.8 (Sacks).** Suppose $x \le_T y$. Then there is a recursive function $f$ such that (i) $(\forall n)(\forall m)(n <_O m \rightarrow f(n) < f(m))$, (ii) $(\forall n)(n \in \mathcal{O}^x \rightarrow |n|_x = |f(n)|_y)$.

**Lemma 2.4.9 (Spector [153]).** (i) $\mathcal{O}^x \le_h y \implies \omega_1^x < \omega_1^y$. (ii) $\omega_1^x < \omega_1^y \land x \le_h y \implies \mathcal{O}^x \le_h y$.

Proof. (i) $<_O$ is $\Pi^1_1(x)$ and hence is recursive in $\mathcal{O}^x$. Thus by Proposition 2.4.6, $\omega_1^x < \omega_1^{\mathcal{O}^x} \le \omega_1^y$.

(ii) If \( x \le_h y \), then \( x \le_T H_e^y \) for some \( e \in \mathcal{O}^y \). Let \( f \) be as in Lemma 2.4.8. Since \( \omega_1^x < \omega_1^y \), by Proposition 2.4.6, \( \omega_1^x < \omega_1^{H_e^y} \). Hence there is a number \( m \in \mathcal{O}^{H_e^y} \) such that for all \( i \), \( i \in \mathcal{O}^x \leftrightarrow i \in \mathcal{O}_m^{H_e^y} \). Thus \( x \le_h H_e^y \le_h y \).

Proof of Theorem 2.4.7. If \( \omega_1^x > \omega_1^{CK} \), then by (ii) of Lemma 2.4.9, \( \mathcal{O} \le_h x \). If \( \mathcal{O} \le_h x \), then by (i) of Lemma 2.4.9, \( \omega_1^x > \omega_1^{CK} \).

**Corollary 2.4.10.** \( x \not\le_h \emptyset \leftrightarrow \mathcal{O}^x \le_h x \not\le_h \emptyset \).

Proof. If \( \mathcal{O}^x \not\le_h x \not\le_h \emptyset \), then \( x <_h \emptyset' \not\le_h x \not\le_h \emptyset \). Conversely, if \( x \not\le_h \emptyset \), then by Theorem 2.4.7, \( \omega_1^x = \omega_1^{CK} < \omega_1^{\mathcal{O}^x} \). By (i) of Lemma 2.4.9, \( \mathcal{O}^x \le_h x \not\le_h \emptyset \).

**Corollary 2.4.11.** The set \(\{x \mid \omega_1^x = \omega_1^{CK}\}\) is \(\Sigma_1^1\).

Proof. By Proposition 1.2.5, \(\mathcal{O}\) is a \(\Pi_1^1\)-singleton. Then \( x \ge_h \mathcal{O} \) if and only if \( (\exists n \in \mathcal{O}^x)(\exists e)(\Theta_e^{H_e^x} \in \{\mathcal{O}\}) \), which is a \(\Pi_1^1\)-statement. By Theorem 2.4.7, the set \(\{x \mid \omega_1^x = \omega_1^{CK}\}\) is \(\Sigma_1^1\).

## 2.5 Some basis theorems and their applications

### 2.5.1 Gandy's Basis Theorem

A set of reals \( A \) is a *basis* for a collection of sets \(\mathbf{P}\) of reals if for any nonempty \( P \in \mathbf{P} \), \( P \cap A \neq \emptyset \). Basis theorems are very important in recursion theory. They are used against other things to pin down the complexity of predicates. For example, let \(\{T_i\}_{i \in \omega}\) be an effective enumeration of recursive trees in \( 2^{<\omega} \). The index set \( N = \{i \mid T_i \text{ contains an infinite path}\} \) appears to be \(\Sigma_1^1\). But one can show that every recursive tree in \( 2^{<\omega} \) containing an infinite path must contain an infinite path recursive in \( \emptyset' \) (see Exercise 1.5.3). So \( N \) is an arithmetical set.

**Lemma 2.5.1.** Suppose that \( T \subseteq \omega^{<\omega} \) is a recursive tree with an infinite path. There is an infinite path \( x \in [T] \) recursive in \( \emptyset \).

Proof. Suppose that \( T \subseteq \omega^{<\omega} \) is a recursive tree with an infinite path. Note that the set \( S = \{ \sigma \in T \mid (\exists y > \sigma)(\forall n)(y \mid n \in T) \} \subseteq T \) is a nonempty \(\Sigma^1_1\) tree without dead ends and so recursive in \( \emptyset \). Since \( T \) has an infinite path, so does \( S \). Hence, the leftmost infinite path \( x \) in \( S \) is recursive in \( \emptyset \).

Thus the collection of reals recursive in \( \emptyset \) is a basis for recursive trees in \(\omega^{<\omega}\), while the set of hyperarithmetic reals is not:

**Proposition 2.5.2.** There is a recursive tree \( S \subseteq \omega^{<\omega} \) with uncountably many infinite paths but without a hyperarithmetic infinite path.

Proof. By Corollary 2.2.2, the set \( A \) of non-hyperarithmetic reals is \(\Sigma^1_1\). Then there is a recursive tree \( T \subseteq 2^{<\omega} \times \omega^{<\omega} \) such that for any \( x \in 2^\omega \), \( x \in A \) if and only if there exists a \( y \) satisfying \( (\forall n)((x \mid n, y \mid n) \in T) \). Let \( S \subseteq \omega^{<\omega} \) be a recursive tree such that \( \sigma \in S \) if and only if \( (\sigma_0, \sigma_1) \in T \) where \( \sigma_0(n) = \sigma(2n) \) and \( \sigma_1(n) = \sigma(2n + 1) \) for all \( n \) which makes sense for the \( \sigma \)’s. Then for any infinite path \( x \in [S] \), \( x = x_0 \oplus x_1 \) so that \( x_0 \in A \). In other words, every infinite path in \( S \) Turing computes a nonhyperarithmetic real. Moreover, by the definition of \( A \), every nonhyperarithmetic real is recursive in an infinite path in \( S \). Hence \( S \) contains uncountably many infinite paths.

**Theorem 2.5.3 (Gandy [33]).** If \( A \) is \(\Sigma^1_1\) and nonempty, then \( A \) contains a real \( x \) such that \( \omega_1^x = \omega_1^{CK} \) and \( x \le_T \emptyset \).

Proof. By Corollary 2.2.2, the set \( B = \{ x \oplus y \mid y \not\le_h x \wedge x \in A \} \) is also a \(\Sigma^1_1\) nonempty set. By Lemma 2.5.1, there is a real \( x \oplus y \in B \) and \( x \oplus y \le_T \emptyset \). Hence \( x \in A \) is recursive in \( \emptyset \). Since \( y \not\le_h x \), we have \( x \not\ge_h \emptyset \). By Theorem 2.4.7, \( \omega_1^x = \omega_1^{CK} \).

Theorem 2.5.3 is known as the Gandy Basis Theorem.

### 2.5.2 Incomparable hyperdegrees

We apply the Gandy Basis Theorem to construct a pair of incomparable hyperdegrees.

**Lemma 2.5.4.** If \( A \) is a \(\Sigma^1_1\)-set containing a nonhyperarithmetic real, then \( A \) is uncountable.

Proof. Suppose that \( A \) is a countable \(\Sigma^1_1\)-set containing a nonhyperarithmetic real. Then the set \( B = \{ x \in A \mid x \not\le_h \emptyset \} \) is a nonempty \(\Sigma^1_1\)-subset of \( A \). Hence there is a recursive tree \( T \) such that \( x \in B \) if and only if there exists a \( y \) satisfying for every \( n \), \( (x \mid n, y \mid n) \in T \). Let \( T' = \{ (\sigma, \tau) \mid (\exists x \supset \sigma)(\exists y \supset \tau)(\forall n)((x \mid n, y \mid n) \in T) \} \) be a subtree of \( T \). Obviously \( T' \) is \(\Sigma^1_1\)-tree. Moreover, for any \( (\sigma, \tau) \in T' \), there are \( \sigma_0, \sigma_1 > \sigma \) and \( \tau_0, \tau_1 > \tau \) with \( (\sigma_0, \sigma_1) \in T' \) for \( i \le 1 \). Otherwise, the \(\Sigma^1_1\)-set \( \{ x \mid (\exists y)((\forall n)(x \supset \sigma \wedge y \supset \tau, y \in T)) \} \subseteq B \) contains a unique real which must be hyperarithmetic, contradicting the definition of \( B \). Thus imitating the proof of Theorem 11.10, one shows that \( B \) contains a perfect subset.
