## 17.9 Markov Clustering

Markov Clustering (MCL), proposed by Stij van Dongen, clusters graph by simulation of stochastic processes on a graph [45]. MCL is based on iterative application of two operations on the transition probability matrix or stochastic matrix graph: *Expand and Inflate*. *Expand(M)* is simply a matrix–matrix multiplication as follows:

```
$M_{Expand} = M \times M
```

while Inflate(M,r) corresponds to raising each element of matrix $M$ to its $r$-th power and normalizing the columns to 1, $r$ is the *inflation parameter* ($r > 1$) and is typically set to 2.

```
$M_{Inflate}(i,j) = \frac{M(i,j)^r}{\sum_k M(k,j)^r}$
```

Remaining values are normalized to make the columns of the matrix sum to 1, and hence, the matrix is a stochastic matrix. Starting with the initial matrix, the whole process is iterated until convergence. Expand step flows out to new vertices. This enhances the intruster flows as there are more paths within the same cluster than between different clusters. Nonlinearity is brought into the process through the Inflate operation that strengthens the intruster flow and weakens the intercluster flow. The process sets up a positive feedback loop forcing all nodes within a tightly linked group to flow to one node [31]. MCL is particularly popular in the bioinformatics community because of its effectiveness in clustering protein–protein interactions [92]. The pseudocode for the algorithm is as follows:

```
Algorithm Markov Clustering, MCL
A := I // Add self to the vertices
M := AD⁻¹ // M is the canonical flow matrix
repeat
  M := Expand(M)
  M := Inflate(M,r)
  M := Prune(M)
until M converges
Interpret M as the resulting clustering
```

MCL has two major shortcomings [48]. First, it is slow because it involves matrix–matrix multiplication. Especially, during the first few iterations when flow matrix is dense, the Expand step becomes time consuming. Second, it has a tendency to produce imbalanced clusters, e.g., singleton clusters with few nodes, or produce one big cluster.

## 17.9.1 Regularized MCL (RMCL): Improvement over MCL

Recently there have been variants of MCL in an effort to address major drawbacks of MCL with respect to scalability and imbalanced clustering. Regularized MCL (RMCL) [48, 50] has succeeded in solving the problem associated with imbalanced clustering, especially the problem relating to producing singleton clusters. Parasarathy [48] observed that the reason behind MCL producing many clusters is the fact that it allows columns of pairs of neighbors in flow matrix to diverge significantly. MCL uses the adjacency matrix of input graph only at the start to initialize the flow matrix, which allows MCL to “overfit.” RMCL addresses the problem by regularizing (or smoothing) the flow distributions with respect to neighbors, i.e., by taking into account neighborhood structure in each step. Essentially it changes the Expand (M := M) step of MCL into a Regularize (M := $M_G$) step where $M_G$ is the canonical transition matrix of the graph. That is,

> $Regularize = M \times M_G$

The pseudocode of the algorithm is given in Algorithm 43.

> **Algorithm 43** Regularized Markov Clustering, RMCL [48]
> ```
> A := A+I // Add self-loop to the vertices
> M := AD⁻¹ // M is the flow matrix
> repeat
>   M := M_{Regularize} := M \times M_G
>   M := M_{Inflate} := Inflate(M,r)
>   M := Prune(M)
> until M converges
> Interpret M as the resulting clustering
> ```

## 17.10 Multilevel Partitioning

The multilevel approach has been successfully applied to a variety of problems [65] and has resulted in fast and high quality results. This powerful framework can also be applied to graph partitioning and often produces fast, accurate and quality partitions. The basic idea is to coarse the graph successively to get a small graph, partition the small graph, and use the result to successfully project the partition back to the original graph [31]. A schematic of the multilevel graph framework is given in Figure 17.1. Many well-known graph partitioning algorithms use multilevel partitioning techniques. Some of them are multilevel spectral clustering [13], Metis (optimizing K/L objective function) [86], Graculus (optimizing normalized and other weighted cuts) [42] and MLR-MCL [48].

There are three main components of any multilevel graph partitioning approach. They are briefly discussed below:

- 1. Coarsening: The goal of coarsening is to get a smaller graph that retains the most important characteristics of the original graph.

![Figure 17.1: Multilevel graph partitioning.](

- 2. Partitioning the Coars Graph: Often this is applied repeatedly until the resultant graph reduces to a manageable “small enough” size. As a consequence, partitioning is applied on the small graph very efficiently. A popular method is to construct a maximal matching on the graph [5]. A matching is a subset of edges (M ⊆ E) such that no two edges share the same endpoint. A matching is maximal if no edge can be added to the matching. Once a matching has been constructed, vertices at the ends of each edge are collapsed into a super node in the coarsened graph. Specifically, for $e_{ij}\in M$, vertices $v_i$ and $v_j$ are collapsed into a single vertex. The weight of the new vertex $v$ is $w(v_i)+w(v_j)$ in [5].

Different techniques exist for construction of a maximal matching. One can use randomized methods for coarsening graphs quickly. A simple randomized matching (RM) is given in [7] which randomly selects edges until the matching is maximal. A slight modification of RM which is known as heavy edge matching (HEM) has been proposed in [86]. The basic idea is to reduce the total weight of the coarsened graph which is reduced in cut-size as well as the original graph. However, one problem associated with this approach is that it can miss heavy edges as pointed out in [5]. Another modification was proposed in [72] by Gupta who suggests sorting the edges by weight and then choosing the heaviest edge. This is called heaviest matching. Sorting is an additional cost for this approach.

- 3. Uncoarsening and Projecting Up: In this step the coarsened graph is used to initialize partitions on finer (bigger) graph. Since each vertex in finer graph can be traced to a vertex in the coarsened graph, assigning the vertices of finer graph to appropriate partitions is simple once we have the partition on the coarsened graph. This is called “coarsening up”. This step is continued until the original graph has been reached.

Metis [86] supports different matching schemes for the coarsening step including RM and HEM. For partitioning the coarsened graph, Metis implements four schemes: based on graph heuristics and on spectral bisection. During the uncoarsening process, Metis uses the K/L algorithm [57] as partition refinement. Since K/L can take many iterations before converging, Metis implements Greedy Refinement (GR) which performs only a single iteration and a Boundary Kernighan-Lin Refinement (BKLR) that allows only swapping vertices that are along the boundary of the bisection. Detailed discussion about Metis can be found in [86].

Graculus [42] generalizes the coarsening process of Metis to a max-cut coarsening to make it effective for a wider range of objectives. The three class approaches are explored—the extremely efficient region-grouping algorithm of Metis [86], the spectral clustering, and the bisection method. In the refinement step, Graculus uses weighted k-means. A more detailed discussion about the algorithm can be found in [42].
