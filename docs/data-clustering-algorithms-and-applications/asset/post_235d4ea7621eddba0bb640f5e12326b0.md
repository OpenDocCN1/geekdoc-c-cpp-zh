## 4.3.2.3 Minimum Spanning Tree-Based Clustering

In a weighted graph, a minimum spanning tree is an acyclic subgraph that covers all the vertices with the minimum weight. Prim's and Kruskal's algorithms are used for finding the minimum spanning tree (MST) in a weighted graph. In Euclidean minimum spanning tree (EMST), the data points represent the vertices and the edges are computed using the Euclidean distance between two points. Using this EMST a divisive clustering method can be developed which removes the largest edge to get two clusters and subsequently removes the next edge to get three clusters so on. This process of removing edges from EMST gives rise to an effective divisive method. The major advantage of this method is that it is able to detect clusters with spherical shapes effectively. A basic EMST clustering algorithm proceeds by taking the parameter K where the edges in the graph are sorted in descending order. This is followed by removing the edges one by one until K components are obtained. This is similar to the process of divisive clustering where further partitions are obtained after each split. Subsequently we can use EMST to build a clustering algorithm which continuously prunes the edges in the graph. An inconsistent edge is one whose weight is much higher than the average weight of the edges in the neighbourhood of that edge. Algorithm 22 describes a minimum spanning tree-based clustering algorithm that was originally proposed by Zahn [5].

**Algorithm 22 Minimum Spanning Tree-Based Divisive Clustering**
1: Create the EMST using Prim's/Kruskal's algorithm on all the data points.
2: repeat
3: Remove edge with highest inconsistency measure.
4: until no edges can be removed.

# Data Clustering: Algorithms and Applications

## 4.3.3 Other Hierarchical Clustering

The agglomerative and divisive clustering methods are successful in capturing clusters. As mentioned above, agglomerative methods, especially single link and complete link, suffer from the "chaining problem" and are ineffective at arbitrarily shaped clusters. Hence, to capture arbitrarily shaped clusters such as CURE [1] and CHAMEON [2] have been proposed in the literature. Some of the popular extensions of hierarchical clustering are discussed below.

- 1. CURE (Cluster Using Representatives) [1] is an algorithm which incorporates a novel feature of representing cluster using a set of well-centered representative points. The distance between two clusters is calculated by looking at the minimum distance between the representative points chosen. In addition, CURE features both of the single link and complete link hierarchical clustering methods. CURE employs a shrinking factor α as shrinks points towards the centroid. This makes CURE more robust to outliers.

- 2. CHAMEON [2] is a clustering algorithm which uses graph partitioning methods on the K-nearest graph of the data. These initial partitions are then used as seeds for the agglomerative hierarchical clustering process. This algorithm uses two metrics based on local-connectivity and relative closeness of clusters to merge the clusters. These metrics capture the local influence of clusters during the clustering thus enabling this algorithm to behave like a dynamic framework. CHAMEON is one of the best hierarchical clustering algorithms and is extremely effective in capturing arbitrarily shaped clusters primarily due to the dynamic behavior. A detailed comparison between the results of CURE and CHAMEON for synthetic datasets with clusters of varying shapes can also be found in [2].

- 3. COBWEB [3]: This is a conceptual clustering algorithm that works incrementally by updating the clusters by object. Probabilistically described clusters are arranged as a tree to form hierarchical clustering known as probabilistic categorization. It handles uncertainty with categorical attributes in clustering through a probabilistic framework that is similar to Naive Bayes. The dendrogram in this algorithm is also a classification tree and the nodes are referred to as concepts.

- 4. Self-Organizing Maps (SOM) [4] were developed on the same lines of Artificial Neural Networks for hierarchical representation. It is an efficient data visualization technique. Similar to K-means, data are assigned to their closest centroids. The differences in the centroid update step, where a centroid is updated, those in its neighborhood close to this centroid are also updated. The final output is an SOM neural network which can be explored to understand the relationships between different objects in the clustering.

## 4.4 Discussion and Summary

A major advantage of partitional clustering algorithms is that they can gradually improve the clustering quality through an iterative optimization process [3]. However, standard hierarchical clustering using the dendrogram cannot revisit the merges (or splits) that were already completed. Partitional algorithms are also effective in detecting compact spherical-shaped clusters and easy to implement and use in practice [4]. K-means is a computationally efficient algorithm compared to hierarchical clustering. There is no consensus, it is believed that K-means is better than hierarchical clustering algorithms [5] in terms of the quality of the final clustering solution.

Hierarchical clustering methods can potentially overcome some of the critical problems associated with flat (partitional) clustering methods. One of the major advantages of hierarchical algorithms is the generation of the visual diagrams which can assist the end-users during the clustering process. In such applications, generally a user will label the clusters to understand more about them. This is also called labeling. Hierarchical methods are also deterministic compared to the nonparametric behavior with the basic K-means algorithm.

Despite these advantages, it is observed that hierarchical clustering methods the merge or split decisions at any given level in the hierarchy cannot be undone [3]. This is considered to be a problem for hierarchical algorithms since [6] proposes an iterative strategy that keeps modifying the dendrogram until the optimal solution is found. The runtime complexity of these hierarchical algorithms is quadratic which is not especially for large-scale problems. Parallel hierarchical methods [7] have also been proposed to reduce the complexity to linear.

In spite of the numerous advances in the field of data clustering over the past decades, both partitional and hierarchical algorithms form a solid foundation for data clustering. Many of the newly proposed clustering algorithms (to be discussed in the next chapters) typically compare their performance to these fundamental algorithms. In addition, due to their simplicity and ease of use, these algorithms are heavily used in several application domains as bioinformatics, information retrieval, text mining, imaging, climate science, and astronomy. The development of new variants of both partitional and hierarchical clustering remains an active area of research.
