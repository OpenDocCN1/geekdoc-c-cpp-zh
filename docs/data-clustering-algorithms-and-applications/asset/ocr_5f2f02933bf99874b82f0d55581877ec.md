Finally, to account for documents of different lengths, the length of each document vector is normalized to unit length ($||d_i,d_j|| = 1$); that is, each document is a vector in the unit hypersphere.

To maximize term co-occurrence in text, words can be reduced to a base form, through stemming or lemmatization. Stemming [85] is a heuristic process that works on individual words, removing derivational affixes and in general cutting the word ending in matches with other forms of the same word. Lemmatization uses dictionaries and morphological analysis, aiming to return to the root of the word [73]. It analyzes words in context and is more demanding than stemming. Synonym of a word can be replaced by a common form using lexical databases [50]. Some attempts have been made to capture order and sentence structure in the VSM by using text word or character n-grams (sequences of two or more items) [19, 75].

**Similarity in vector space.** The cosine similarity is the most used measure to compute similarity between two documents in the vector space. Given vectors $d_1$ and $d_2$, it is defined

```
$cos(d_1,d_2) = \frac{d_1 \cdot d_2}{||d_1|| \times ||d_2||}$
```

where “...” represents the vector dot product operation. This formula can be simplified to $cos(d_1,d_2) = d_1 \cdot d_2$ for vectors of unit length.

Let **D** be the $N \times M$ document-document matrix, whose rows are the term frequency vectors. The pairwise similarities of all documents in the collection can be computed directly from **D** as

```
$SIM = L^{-1/2}DL^{-1/2}$
```

where **X = $DD^T$** and **L** is an $N \times N$ diagonal matrix whose diagonal elements are the diagonal elements of X. The left and right multiplication of $X$ by $L^{-1/2}$ scales the document to unit length. The formula reduces to $SIM = DD^T$ if the documents are already unit length.

Other popular measures for comparing documents include the Euclidean, Manhattan, and Chebyshev distances, and the Jaccard coefficient similarity. The Euclidean distance, also known as the $l^2$ norm, is simply the geometric distance in the $M$-dimensional space of the vectors, defined by

```
$dist(d_1,d_2) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{M} (d_1^i - d_2^i)^2}$
```

where $d_1^i$ is the $i$th element in the $d_1$ document vector. The Manhattan (also known as the city-block distance or the $l^1$ norm) and the Chebyshev (also known as the Chebyshev board or the $l^{\infty}$ norm) distances are similarly defined as

```
$dist_1(d_1,d_2) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} |d_1^i - d_2^i|$
```

```
$dist_{\max}(d_1,d_2) = \max_{i=1}^{M} |d_1^i - d_2^i|$
```

The Jaccard coefficient is a set similarity metric. It can be applied to a feature vector by considering its nonzero elements as members. Using this logic, the Jaccard coefficient measures commonality, represented by the intersection of the two documents normalized by their union:

```
$J(D_1,D_2) = \frac{|D_1 \cap D_2|}{|D_1 \cup D_2|}$
```

where $D_1$ and $D_2$ are representations of $d_1$ and $d_2$, respectively.
