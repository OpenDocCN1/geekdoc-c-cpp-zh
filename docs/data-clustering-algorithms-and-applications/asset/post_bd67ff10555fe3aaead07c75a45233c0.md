While most indices, such as DB, XB, and SDb, consider both of the evaluation criteria (compactness and separation) in the way of ratio or summation sum, as RMSDT, RS, and I consider only one. The 12 measures are briefly introduced as follows.

The root-mean standard deviation (RMSSTD) is the root square of the pooled variance of all the formed clusters. R-squared (RS²) is the ratio of sum of squares of clusters to the total sum of squares of the whole data set. It measures the degree of difference between clusters [51, 19]. The Modified Huber G statistic [25] evaluates the differences between clusters by counting the disagreements of pairs of objects in two partitions.

The Calinski-Harabasz index (CHI) [7] evaluates the cluster validity on the average between- and within-cluster sum. Index I (I) [41] measures separation by the maximum distance between cluster centers, and measures compactness on the sum of distances between objects and their cluster center. Dunn’s index (DI) [111] uses the minimum pairwise distance between objects in different clusters as the intercluster separation and the maximum diameter among all clusters as the intercluster compactness. These three indices take a form of Index=(a-Separation)/(b+Compactness), where a and b are weights. The optimal cluster number is determined by maximizing the value of these indices.

The Silhouette index (S) [49] validates the clustering performance on the pairwise difference of between- and within-cluster distances. In addition, the optimal cluster number is determined by maximizing the value of index.

The Davies-Bouldin index (DB) [9] is calculated as follows. For each cluster C, the similarities DB and all other clusters are computed, and the highest value is assigned to C as it represents cluster similarity. The smaller the index, the better the clustering is. By minimizing this index, clusters are the most distinct from each other, and therefore, achieve the best partition. The Xie-Ben index (XB) [6] defines the compactness as the minimum mean square distance between each object and its cluster center. The optimal cluster number is reached when the minimum of XB is found. Kan and Ramakrishnan [32] proposed DB* and XF* in 2005 as improvements of DB and XB. The two improved measures will be used in this study.

The idea of SD index (SD) [22] is based on the concepts of average scattering and the total sum of clusters. The first term evaluates compactness based on variances of cluster objects, and the second term evaluates separation difference based on distance between cluster centers. The SD index is the sum of these two terms, and the optimal number of clusters can be obtained by minimizing the value of SD.

The SDb index [20] takes density into account to measure the intercluster separation. The basic idea is that for each pair of clusters, at least one of their densities should be larger than the density of their midpoint. The intracluster compactness is the same as it is in SD. Similarly, the index is the summation of these terms and the minimum value of SDb indicates the optimal cluster.

Different from the existing measures, the Clustering Validation index based on Nearest Neighbors (CVNN) [38] evaluates the intercluster separation on objects that capture the geometrical information of each cluster. Sharing the same idea with KNN consensus uses multiple objects as representatives for different clusters in different situations when measuring the intercluster separation. If an object is located in the center of a cluster and is surrounded by objects in the same cluster, it is separated from other clusters and contributes little to the intercluster separation. If an object is located at the edge of a cluster and is surrounded mostly by objects in other clusters, it connects to other clusters tightly and contributes a lot to the intercluster separation. CVNN also employs the average pairwise distance between objects in the same cluster as the measurement of intracluster compactness. Finally, the CVNN index takes a form of measurement of the intercluster separation and the intracluster compactness after the normalization of both of them.

There are some other internal validation measures in the literature [50, 21, 58, 35]. However, some have poor performance while some are designed for data sets with specific structures. Take Composed Density between within clusters (CDbw) and Symmetry-based index (Sym-index) for examples. It is hard for CDbw to find representatives for each cluster, which makes the result of CDbw unstable. On the other hand, Sym-index can handle only data sets which are symmetrical. A focused study on the mentioned 12 validation measures and acronyms for these measures will be used.

## 23.3 Understanding of Internal Validation Measures

In this section, a study of 12 internal validation measures mentioned in Section 23.1 is presented to investigate properties of different validation measures in different aspects, which can be helpful for index selection. If not mentioned, K-means [39] (implemented by CLUTO [27]) is used as the clustering algorithm, and the parameter k is set to 10 for CVNN.

### 23.3.1 The Impact of Monotonicity

The monotonicity of different validation indices is studied in this subsection to get clustering results for different numbers of clusters. As shown in Figure 23.7, Wellseparated is a synthetic data set of 1000 objects, which are well separated into five clusters. As the results show in Table 23.1, the first three indices monotonically increase or decrease as NC equals the true number. There are certain reasons the remaining nine indices reach their maximum or minimum value as NC increases. Specifically, RMSDTD = $\sqrt{SSE/(n-NC)}$, and $n-NC$ can be viewed as a constant. Therefore, RMSDTD decreases. Also $RS = (TSS - SSE)/TSS$ (Total Sum of Squares and $TSS = SSE + SSB$, SSB-Between group Sum of Squares) which is a constant for a certain set data. Thus $RS$ increases as $NC$ increases.

From the generation of $G$, only objects within clusters will be counted in the equation. As a result, if the data set is divided into two equal clusters, each cluster will have $n/2$ objects, and $n^2/4$ pairs of distances will be counted. If the data set is divided into three equal clusters, each cluster will have $n/3$ objects, and $n^2/3$ pairs of distances will be counted. Therefore, with the increasing of the cluster number $NC$, more pairs of distances are counted, which makes $\Gamma$ increase.

Looking further into these issues, one can discover that they either take separation, or compactness into account ($RS$ and $\Gamma$ consider only separation, and $RMSSTD$ considers only compactness). As the property of monotonicity, the curves of $RMSSTD$, $RS$, and $\Gamma$ will be either upward or downward. It is claimed that the optimal cluster number is reached at the shift point of the curves, which since the shift point is very subjective and hard to determine, here measures are excluded from future studies.
