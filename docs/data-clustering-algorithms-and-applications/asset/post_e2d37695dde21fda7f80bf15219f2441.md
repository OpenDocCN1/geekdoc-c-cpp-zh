**Subspace Clustering.** Subspace clustering in a narrower sense pursues the goal of finding all clusters in all subspaces of the feature space. This goal obviously is deified to the bottom-up technique used by these approaches, based on some monotonic property of clusters allowing the application of the APRIORI search. However, the relationship between the definition of subspace clustering (find all clusters in all subspaces) and the subspace search (bottom-up, applying an APRIORI search) is historical and hence rather than essential. We consider the problem as guiding category, regardless of the approach. Nevertheless, the majority of approaches consists of some adaptation of the APRIORI search method.

The pioneer approach for finding all clusters in all subspaces called the “subspace clustering” for this specific task has been CLIQUE [14], using a grid-based clustering method. The data is partitioned by an axis-parallel grid into equi-sized units of width $\delta$. Only units which contain at least $\tau$ points are considered dense. A cluster is defined as a maximal set of dense units. Since dense units satisfy the downward closure property, subspace clusters can be explored efficiently in a bottom-up way. With all 1-dimensional units, (k+1)-dimensional dense units are computed from the k-dimensional units in an APRIORI-like procedure. If a (k+1)-dimensional unit contains a projection onto a k-dimensional unit that is not dense, then the (k+1)-dimensional unit cannot be dense. Furthermore, a heuristic that is based on the minimum description length principle is introduced to discard units within less interesting subspaces (i.e., subspaces that contain only a small number of dense units). This way, the efficiency of the algorithm is enhanced but at the cost of incomplete results (i.e., possibly some true clusters are lost).

There are some variants of CLIQUE; let us consider three well-known examples. The method ENCLUS [34] also relies on a fixed grid for subspaces that potentially contain one or more clusters than than dense units. Three quality criteria for subspaces are introduced, one of them replaces the downward closure property. The generation of subspace clusters is similar to CLIQUE. Another variant of CLIQUE called MAFIA [111] uses an adaptive grid over overlapping windows of width $\delta$ as 1-dimensional units of the grid.

In summary, all grid-based methods use a simple but rather efficient cluster model. The shape of each resulting cluster corresponds to a polygon with axis-parallel lines in the corresponding subspace. Obviously, the accuracy and the efficiency of CLIQUE and its variants primarily depend on the granularity and the positioning of the grid. A higher grid granularity results in higher runtime-requirements but will likely produce more accurate results.

Subspace clustering aims at finding all clusters in all subspaces. As a consequence, each data point can belong to many clusters simultaneously (i.e., the clusters can substantially overlap in different subspaces). This approach usually results in redundant clusters when the retrieved clustering.

Many approaches in the literature do not clearly fit in one of these traditional categories and the tendency of opinion in research is that more suitable problem descriptions should lie somewhere between these archetypal categories, allowing for overlap of clusters, but restricting the redundancy of clusters. Eventually, a suitable clustering should not be defined by the algorithm but by the domain-specific requirements.

Note that these categories are not (or coincidentally in the early approaches) related to the categories of subspace research (top-down vs. bottom-up).

SUBCLU [84] uses the DBSCAN cluster model of density-connected sets [46]. It is shown that density-connected sets enables DBSCAN to search for density-based clusters in subspaces in APRIORI-like style. The resulting clusters may include arbitrary shape and size in the corresponding subspaces. In fact, for each subspace SUBCLU puts clusters that have been found by DBSCAN applied to that subspace. Compared to the grid-based approaches, SUBCLU achieves a better clustering quality which requires a higher runtime.

It has been observed that a bias towards a certain density threshold, as used by SUBCLU and the grid-based approaches, leads to a certain similarity (recall Aspect 5 of dimensionality). On the one hand, a tighter threshold is needed to separate clusters from noise in low-dimensional subspaces and lose clusters in higher-dimensional subspaces. On the other hand, a more relaxed threshold which is to detect high-dimensional clusters will produce an excessive amount of low-dimensional clusters. Motivation by this, the problem uncertainty is addressed by a cluster model called DISC [18] (and Sim et al. [19, 70, 100]) has been proposed. DISC is based on a density measure that is adaptive to the dimensionality. A major drawback of this approach is lack of antimonotonic properties and, thus, the search space is not possible. A "weak density" is thus defined as a remedy, providing antitotonic properties. This remedy, however, introduces a global density threshold again, which renders the intended solution of the motivating problem ineffective. As this example shows, the curse of dimensionality should always be expected to keep haunting the searcher.

**Hybrid Approaches.** The majority of approaches do not use a partitioning but pursue some hybrid [117, 136]. This way, hierarchies of subspace clusters can be discovered (i.e., the information of a lower-dimensional cluster is composed of a subset of the attributes of a higher-dimensional cluster). DISH uses an algorithm that is inspired by the density-based clustering algorithm OPTICS. HARP [133] is a hierarchical clustering algorithm similar to single-link clustering [126, 121] but does not produce a hierarchy of subspace clusters. Starting with singleton clusters, HARP iteratively merges clusters as long as the resulting cluster has a minimum number of relevant attributes. A relevance score is introduced for attributes based on a threshold that has harsh value and is progressively decreased when clusters grow in size. By design, HARP has problems finding low-dimensional clusters. The dendrogram can be cut at any level in order to produce a unique assignment of points to clusters.
