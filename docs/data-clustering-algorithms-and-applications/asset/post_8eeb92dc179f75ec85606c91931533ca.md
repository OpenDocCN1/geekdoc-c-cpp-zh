### 13.3.3 Adjacency-Based Algorithms

The document-text matrix naturally represents the adjacency between documents and words in a collection and can be interpreted as a graph. Spectral clustering finds cuts within the induced document-text matrix graph that provides optimal clusters. Z et al. [120] partition the graph by minimizing a normalized sum of edge weights between vertex pairs in the graph. Spectral Recursive Embedding (SRE) provides an approximate solution to the problem by computing a partial singular value decomposition of a scaled document-text frequency matrix. Spectral clustering is covered in more detail in Chapter 8 of this book.

The optimal solution to the graph partitioning problem is NP-complete. Relaxations of this problem often lead to a generalized eigenvalue problem, which makes spectral clustering suitable for only datasets with limited feature vectors. Ding et al. [28] induce the Mcut algorithm, which solves a relaxed version of the optimization of the min-max cut objective. They show that it produces more balanced partitions than other cuts, including the normalized cut.

Similar documents are defined by a shared vocabulary. Co-clustering allows clusters in a column to identify documents, and vice-versa. Co-clustering, also known as clustering, to find blocks of related words and documents in the text, i.e., related rows and columns in the document-matrix. Dhillon et al. [26] take an information-theoretic approach to solving the problem. In their solution, the optimal co-clustering maximizes the mutual information between documents and random variables, where the document-matrix represents an empirical probability distribution of the two random variables. Equivalently, the optimal co-clustering minimizes the information loss between the original random and the clustered random variables. Dhillon et al. formulate the problem as optimizing this loss. At each iteration, the algorithm recovers cluster prototypes by using clustering information and recovers prototypes by using row clustering. They show the algorithm monotonically decreases the objective function and is guaranteed to reach a local minimum in a finite number of steps.

Co-clustering can also be solved by graph-theoretic approaches. Rege et al. [89] propose the Isometric Co-clustering Algorithm (ICA) which partitions the bipartite graph formed by documents and terms. It is done by heuristically minimizing the ratio of the partition perimeter and area, given an appropriate definition of graph-theoretic area. The advantage of ICA over spectral clustering approaches is that SVP provides a solution to a system of linear equations, which is computationally less expensive. Ge and Zhou [38] propose a Dual Regularized Co-clustering (DRCC) with documents and terms to be discrete samples from separate manifolds. They construct two graphs that allow them to see the geometric structure of the two manifolds. They ensure that both documents and terms are smooth with respect to their individual manifolds by regularizing the two graphs, enabling DRCC to utilize the encoded geometric information. The partitioning is then accomplished via semi-nonnegative tri-factorization with two graph regulators.

### 13.3.4 Generative Algorithms

While previous methods focus on the current picture of data, generative algorithms try to find how documents arrived at their current state. Documents are made up of words that must be connected in patterns to form comprehensible language. If the generative models, the language factors, of documents could be identified, documents from the same models would use similar language and thus be considered similar. Generative algorithms can be presented as a mixture of probability distributions over the collection of terms [47, 105, 15, 13, 124, 61]. For example, Probabilistic Latent Semantic Analysis (PLSA) [47, 46], a probabilistic extension of the dimensionality reduction approach based on LSA [24] (Section 13.2.4), defines a statistical model in which the conditional probability between documents and is modeled as a latent variable. An unobserved class variable is assigned to each observation (e.g., the occurrence of a term in a given document), since each document is created by a mixture of distributions.

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) [13] considers mixture models that express the so-called “exchangeability” of both terms and documents. In LDA, the generative process consists of three levels that involve the whole corpus, the documents, and the terms of each document. The algorithm selects a single topic for each document, a distribution over collection from a Dirichlet distribution. Finally, each term is sampled from a multinomial distribution over terms specific to the sampled topic. In this way, LDA defines a more sophisticated generative model for a document collection, whereas PLSA generates a model for each individual document. The LDA generation process, shown graphically through plate notation (Section 13.2.1) in Figure 13.3, is detailed below.

- 1. For each topic, generate a multinomial distribution over terms, $\beta_k \sim Dir_M(\eta)$, $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$
- 2. For each document $d_i$, $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$
   a. Generate a multinomial distribution over topics, $\theta_i \sim Dir_K(\alpha)$
   b. For each $w_{ij}$ in document $d_i$:
      i. Choose the topic $z_{ij}$ from the distribution in step a, e.g., $z_{ij} \sim Multi(\theta_i)$
      ii. Choose $w_{ij}$ from topic $z_{ij}$, e.g., $w_{ij} \sim Multi(\beta_{z_{ij}})$

Many extensions to the initial probabilistic clustering algorithms have been developed. Chemudugenta et al. [20] propose a model that combines-topic and word-level modeling of documents. To address the uncorrelated words assumption made in LDA, Walla [108] provides a bigram topic model that incorporates a notion of word order. Bayesian nonparametric methods [104, 12] find the number of topics exhibited in the collection as part of the inference, rather than requiring the user to provide it. Rosen-Ziti et al. [90] use a two-stage stochastic process to model the author-topic relationship. B [111] provides a general overview and several research directions for probabilistic topic models. Chapter 3 in this book provides an in-depth look at probabilistic models for clustering.

**Similarity in probabilistic space.** Since generative models represent documents as probability distributions, a number of information distance metrics have been proposed for comparing two such documents. Let $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_R\}, x_i \in \mathbb{R}, R \in [1..R]$ and PMFs $p = \{p_1, \dots, p_R\}, q = \{q_1, \dots, q_R\}$ for variable-the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence measures the proximity of $p$ to $q$.

$$KL(p,q) = \sum_{i=1}^R p_i \log_2 \frac{p_i}{q_i}$$

## Document Clustering: The Next Frontier

### 13.4 Clustering Documents

Long documents often discuss multiple subjects. This presented added challenge to general purpose clustering algorithms that intend to associate a document with a single topic. The idea to solving this problem is to consider the document as being made up of topologically blocks, *segments*. Segments can be identified independent of or concurrent to the clustering procedure.

#### 13.4.1 Document Segmentation

Text segmentation is concerned with the fragmentation of text into smaller units (e.g., paragraphs) each discussing a single topic. Regardless of the presence of logical structure in the document, linguistic criteria and statistical measures have been mainly used to identify thematically coherent, contiguous blocks [44, 10, 21].

The *TextTiling* algorithm [44] is the exemplary block-based method for text segmentation. TextTiling is able to subdivide text into multiparagrous, contiguous and disjoint blocks that represent topics, or subtitles. More precisely, TextTiling detects boundaries by analyzing patterns of local co-occurrence and distribution in the text. That a topic tends to co-occur locally. A switch to a new subtopic is detected when the co-occurrence of a set of terms ends the co-occurrence of another set terms.

Recent segmentation techniques have taken advantage of advances in generative topic modeling which were specifically designed to identify topic within text. Brants et al. [15] use PLSA to capture word-topic distributions, fold in those at the block level (if block cases are segments), and then select segmentation points based on the similarity values of adjacent block pairs. Sun [102] use LDA on corpora of segments, segment similarities via a Fisher kernel, and optimize segmentation via dynamic programming. Misra et al [76] use a document-level LDA model, represent segments and predict their LDA models

**Modeling segmentation.** The Segmented Topic Model (STM) [29] assumes that each segmented document has a certain mixture of latent topics and each segment within the document also has a mixture over the same latent topics as the documents. The shared latent topic provides a way to correlate documents and segments.

The basic idea of the LDA model is that documents can be represented as random mixtures over topics, word-topic distributions $\theta$, where topics are distributions over words.
