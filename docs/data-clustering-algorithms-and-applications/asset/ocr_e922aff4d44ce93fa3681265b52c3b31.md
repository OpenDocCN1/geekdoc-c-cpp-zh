# 19.1 Introduction

Clustering algorithms group data objects together by some notion of distance or similarity. This resembles visual tasks that are easy: spotting a cluster of stars in the night sky or identifying a cluster of old houses within a modern city. The human visual system “has evolved to facilitate quick and considered consideration of the visually like and unlike a wide variety of cues — e.g. location and relative, shape, colour, and matching against predetermined patterns. Consequently, visualization is a natural and powerful resource for cluster analysis; it is especially useful in identifying unanticipated structure“[33].

Many algorithms have been proposed to formulate the concept of a cluster and to automatically detect clusters in large sets of data objects. When the notion of Euclidean distance extends to vector spaces with more than three dimensions, clustering by proximity becomes formerly possible for more complex data objects. Complex data objects are often described by a large fixed set of features. Furthermore, they are considered as multidimensional vectors.
