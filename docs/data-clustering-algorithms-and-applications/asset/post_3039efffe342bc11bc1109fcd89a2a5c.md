Hu et al. [51] combine original features with semantic features derived from external knowledge-bases to support the clustering task. Applying standard NLP techniques, they model the short input text into a parsing-like structure to support the extraction of redundant seed phrases, which they use to generate external semantic features. More specifically, a naive pau-segmentation of the text facilitates a shallow parsing pass which identifies seed phrases. To avoid redundancy, each phrase is compared with all the others in the segment, and the phrase with the highest Wikipedia-based similarity is removed. The remaining seed phrases are used to retrieve external text content, from either Wikipedia or WordNet, depending on the presence of words in the phrase. External features are extracted from titles and link text in the phrase. Document features are finally selected by tf-idf weights of original and external features. An additional parameter is introduced to control the concurrent use of multiple types of external features in the feature space. Hu et al. demonstrate the concurrent use of external knowledge bases, with internal semantics, to improve clustering of short texts.

## 13.5.3 Clustering Web Snippets

Document clustering research has traditionally focused on Web documents as a way to facilitate users' ability to quickly browse search results. Web documents could be clustered off-line, with a general purpose clustering algorithm. However, this approach ineffective [37, 16], because it is based on features that the entire collection is irrelevant to the particular query. Intent, query-specific, online, postretrieval clustering results, i.e., clustering search results, was shown to produce superior results [45]. A search result is generally composed of a title and a snippet, short often containing phrases from the document to the search query. As such, clustering results uses a subset of the collection concentrated around the query terms.

Unlike the clustering task, the primary focus of search result clustering is not to produce optimal clusters [109, 16, 5]. Rather, search result clustering is a highly user-centric task as users are unwilling to wait longer than a few seconds for search results. Clusters must exhibit identifying query subsets or facets from the user perspective. Finally, clusters must be assigned informative, meaningful and concise labels.

Scatter-Gamer [82, 45] is an early cluster-based browsing method that addressed the speed requirement by postretrieval clustering on top-ranked documents returned from a traditional information retrieval system. Zamir and Etzioni demonstrated the well-known Suffix Tree Clustering (STS) [118] algorithm, which creates intuitive clusters based on phrases shared between documents. It follows the assumption that retrieved phrases imply topics of interest within the search result. STS treats a phrase as a string of words, builds a suffix tree or traverses the tree to base clusters. The algorithm then uses a similarity measure based on overlap of documents to create a base cluster. In this graph, each node represents a group of snippets sharing a phrase. The final solution is obtained by finding the connected components in the graph. Zamir and Etzioni also showed that using snippets for clustering is as effective as using documents.

Addressing the meaningful and concise label research of search result clustering, Anastasi et al. [5] employ a strategy that generates labels for clusters. They first identify phrases within a set of search results using a suffix tree in linear time by Ukkonen's algorithm [106]. Then they select labels from the frequent phrases using a greedy set heuristic, where at each step a phrase covering the most uncovered search results is selected until the whole cluster is covered or no further phrases remain. Results are then assigned to a label if they contain the terms in the label, unlabeled results placed in a special cluster named Other. Osiński et al. [79] also follow a label-before-cluster approach. They use dimensionality reduction techniques to induce cluster labels. Then, treating each cluster as a query for the snippet-set in the information retrieval sense, they populate the clusters with the retrieved results for the queries. This has inspired many other phrase-based hierarchical methods for Clustering snippets.

Common phrases naturally describe clusters. Kummura et al. [65] develop a monolithic clustering algorithm with the goal of automatically generating a hierarchy, where concepts are terms or phrases. At each level of the hierarchy being constructed, the algorithm aggressively identifies topics that the distinctiveness of the monhetic phrases describing the clusters is maximized. Li and Wu [67] first used a phrase-based method of extracting phrases from snippets. The clustering method starts with all extracted phrases belonging to individual clusters and chooses the most clusters according to the constructed index. Each cluster is finally identified by a distinct phrase. The snippets whose phrases belong to the same cluster are grouped together, while the remaining snippets are clustered based on their k-nearest neighbors. Zeng et al. [119] map the clustering problem to a phrase ranking problem, in which a regression model is first trained to rank the n-grams for a specified keyword. The model is then used to extract relevant phrases which the snippets are finally clustered.

## 13.5.4 Clustering Microblogs

The recent popularity of social networks has led to increasing demand for robust clustering algorithms for microblog data, or tweets. General purpose document clustering algorithms do not work well with this data due to the lack of occurrence terms and context in the short "documents". Researchers have tried to solve this by altering existing techniques or creating specialized models. The most promising research direction relies on aligning or augmenting the short texts with external information.

Liu et al [69] rely on an incremental similarity-threshold based clustering to identify similar tweets for the task of semantic role labeling. In a related problem of classifying tweets to a predefined set of generic classes, Sriram et al. [98] compare the bag of words model with others based on short-text specific features such as shortened words or slang, time-event phrases, opinions or user names. In their evaluation, they find that non-bag of words outperform the bag of words ones. Park et al. [180] propose a hybrid approach that exploits external information from search to deal with the extraction of phrases from a blog. A set of candidate terms with highly-distinct values is initially extracted from all posts of a blog, and then fed to a Web-based search engine. The resulting output for each candidate term is grouped into a hierarchy of clusters, and each of these clusters is compared with the blog posts covering to determine how many topics are covered by the blog.

Topic modeling has also been shown in the microblog domain. Range et al. propose LDA [88], a version of LDA that enables available supervision, and use it Twitter data [87] to characterize content, trends, and recommend users to follow. Wen et al. [113] propose a PageRank-type algorithm for measuring influence of microblogs. They use LDA to decode latent topics and compute transition probabilities contingent on the similarity of users. Hong and Davison [48] study how to train topic models on microblog data to be used in standard text mining applications. They find that model based features can be very useful, while the length of the documents can greatly affect the effectiveness of trained topic models. Specifically, aggregating short messages leads to better models.
