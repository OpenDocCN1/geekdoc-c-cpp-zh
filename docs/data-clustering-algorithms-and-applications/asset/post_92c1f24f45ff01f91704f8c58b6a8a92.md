## 11.11 Local Partitioning Algorithms

Local algorithms solve the partitioning problem for a given vertex or a set of vertices without looking at the whole graph. These algorithms are important in the context of large graphs when one is interested in a few vertices as opposed to all the vertices in the graph. They are interesting because the complexity of the algorithms, to a large extent, no longer depends on the size of the graph, but rather on the size of the solution. The intuition being that random walks from inside a well-connected group of nodes will not mix enough as a bottleneck that prevents the probability of easily getting out of the cluster [131].

Such local clustering using random walks has been described in [156, 154]. Let $p_u$ be the probability distribution of a $t$-step random walk starting at $u$. Let $\Gamma$ be the permutation of the vertices of the graph according to degree-normalized probability for each $t$. That is,

```
$p_t(v) = \frac{d(v)}{d(\Gamma_t)}$
```

Let us define sweep set $S^t_i = \Gamma_1, \dots, \Gamma_t$. Given all random walks converge to the stationary distribution [131], let $\Psi_u$ be the final distribution of the random walk. The main theoretical result shows that the difference between $p^t(S^t_i)$ and $\Psi_u(S^t_i)$ is small or there exists a cut with low conductance. By checking conductance of sweep sets $S^t_i$ at each step $t$, one can discover clusters of low conductance. This work was extended for seeded sets as opposed to was proposed by Andersen and Lang [6]. It has been shown that the local clustering approach can recover the original community for large datasets with a set of vertices belonging to a known community as seeds.

Spielman and Teng [156] was improved by Andersen et al. [5] by the simulation of random walks with restarts (Personalized PageRank). Another local clustering algorithm is Nibble [155].

**Improving partitions by Flow-Based Postprocessing:** A partition of a graph can be improved using algorithms for approximate maximum flow in networks. Blake [58] used a crawler to identify approximate community and set up a max-flow/min-cut problem that produces the actual community in order to discover web communities. A strategy for improving any arbitrary cut was proposed by Lang and Rao [97]. For a cut $(C, C')$ of a graph, their algorithm finds the best improvement among all cuts $(C', C)$ where $C'$ is a strict subset of $C$. They formulate a new instance of the max-flow problem such that the polynomial solution to this problem can be used to find $C'$ with lowest conductance among all subsets of $C$. This method is referred to as Max-Flow-Quotient Improvement (MQI). In a step, Metis is first used to bipart the input graph. And then MQI is used to improve the partition. MQI can improve partitions by local clustering as well [6].

**Community Discovery via Shing:** Web documents can be clustered using shingles and fingerprints (also known as sketches) as proposed in [18]. A length-s shingle of an object is s off parts of the object. For instance, a length-s shingle of a document is a contiguous sequence of length s contained in the document. A sketch is a fixed-size subset of shingles with a specific length. The property that makes an object's fingerprint is that the similarity between two objects' sketches is approximately equivalent to the similarity between the objects themselves. Here, the similarity to Jaccard similarity, i.e.,

```
Sim(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}
```

Shingling has been applied for extracting dense communities from large graphs [65]. In this work, each node's outgoing links are used to get the first-level shingle where each vertex v is associated with a number of shingles, each of which points to v. After this inverted index is built that contains each first-level shingle and a list of vertices associated with v. Second-level shingles (also known as meta-shingles) are built from first-level. Two first-level shingles are considered as relevant if they share at least one meta-shingle. A graph is constructed where nodes stand for first-level shingles and edges indicate meta-shingle. Clusters of first-level shingles are free to connect back to original nodes and the algorithm is bipartite as well as undirected graphs and is very efficient and scalable [131].

## 17.12 Hypergraph Partitioning

A hypergraph is a generalization of a graph where an edge, called hyperedge, can connect more than two vertices. Hypergraphs appear naturally in many problems such as Boolean satisfiability and circuit layout. Formally, a hypergraph is defined as $H=(\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{L})$ where $\mathcal{V}$ is the set of vertices and $\mathcal{L}$ is the set of nodes (hyperedges) among those vertices. Weights can be easily associated with the vertices of the graph. Every $n_j \in \mathcal{N}$ is a subset of vertices in the graph. That is, $n_j \subset \mathcal{V}$. Vertices in a net are called pins.

A k-way partitioning of a hypergraph partitions the vertices into $k$ disjoint partitions. Typically to the problem tries to minimize a cost function. One standard cost function is *net cut* which is simply the sum of the weights of the edges that span more than one partition. Constraints are usually added to the partitioning problem. Where certain vertices are fixed in their partitions and balance constraint where the total vertex weight in each partition is balanced [129]. Optimal hypergraph partitioning with *balanced constraint* is known to be NP-hard [63]. However, heuristic algorithms with non-linear runtime have been developed in [22, 57, 90] and some refinements are proposed in [104, 47, 4, 88].

*Clustering in hypergraph*, generally refers to the process of finding a coarser hypergraph from the input hypergraph by merging vertices into larger groups (clusters). Weight of a cluster is the sum of vertices within the cluster [129].

Performance is always a big concern for partitioning algorithms because in many of their applications the size of input graph increases every year. For instance, in VLSI design the number of transistors grows exponentially following Moore's law, and thus, the algorithms must also scale for the inputs to be used effectively. In the following section, we provide a brief review of different partitioning schemes.

**Exhaustive Search:** These methods produce optimal partitions, but with an exponential complexity. Evaluation of each solution is the bottleneck and can be sped up by incrementally evaluating the cost. Iteratively updating the cut of a solution when a vertex is moved is straightforward [25]. However, the complexity grows exponentially in the number of vertices and makes it impossible for large, practical cases.

**Branch and Bound:** Intelligent pruning of the search space can be added to exhaustive methods to improve their scalability. This technique is known as Branch and Bound (B&B). It performs a recursive depth first search on the tree of assignment (vertices to partitions) and finds the best partition for each unassigned vertex. It can search the entire solution space in exponential time but maintains optimality by bounding suboptimal results. Different constraints can be used for pruning and bounding [129].

**Fidia-Mattheys Heuristic:** Even B&B methods take an impractical amount of time to produce the optimal output for realistic instances. The Fiduccia (FM) heuristic is an adaptive linear-time heuristic for fast improvement of hypergraph partitioning. The FM algorithms prioritize by gain. Here more is the change of assignment of a vertex to a partition and the gain is the corresponding change to the cost function. The algorithm runs in passes where each vertex is moved exactly once. The initial solution is usually produced by some randomized algorithm and then passes continue until convergence.

**Multilevel FM Framework:** The multilevel framework provides the best known results for large hypergraphs [129]. It involves three major steps as: coarsening, partitioning, and unarsening. MLFM is one of the best techniques for practical sized hypergraphs.

**Different Definitions of Communities:** Community in a graph is most commonly defined as a subset of vertices connected internally and loosely connected to the rest of the graph. But there have been alternative characterizations and definitions of community in the literature. Research has been proposed by Aor and Parthasarathy in [8]. They define viewpoints as a group of influential and subset of nodes. Viewpoint neighborhood provides a new and exciting tool for analysis of large graphs.

Another class of communities, as proposed by Lesovec et al. [101], is whiskers which is a group of nodes that are connected to the rest of the graph by only one or two edges. They find that to the measure of conductance, some of communities in a variety of real-world networks are whiskers.

**Other techniques:** Several techniques have been proposed in the literature for partitioning hypergraphs [44, 50, 174]; they have some drawbacks that make it impractical to use them for real applications. Meta-heuristics such as simulated annealing and tabu search often produce better quality results at the cost of an impractical increase in runtime [50]. Other techniques have some constraints associated with them that limit their applicability in practical cases. One example is the spectral techniques [44]. Yang and Wong's [174] method relies on min-cut-flow algorithms and efficient network flow algorithms. These are polynomial-time algorithms that cannot take the balance constraint into account which are costly trial-and-error in flow-based partitioning [129].
