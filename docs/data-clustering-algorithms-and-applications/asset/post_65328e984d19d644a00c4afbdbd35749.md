**Accounting for segment correlation.** LDCC inherently assumes that documents are composed of single-topic segments. Consecutive segments often pertain to the same subject, in the same way that paragraphs in a chapter may cover different aspects of the topic discussed. In their follow-up paper, Shafiei and Miloski [96] extended LDCC to potentially identify coherent segments in text. The proposed model, LDSEG, assumes a high likelihood that the segment has the same distribution as the previous segment in the document and models this using a Markov process on the segment-topic distribution. A binary variable for the topic of each segment indicates whether it is the same as that of the previous segment. If it is not, a new topic is sampled for the current segment. The list of states for this switching variable also defines a segmentation in each document. Figure 13.6 (b) depicts the plate notation of the LDSEG model, whose generation process for each document is detailed below.

1. Choose the number of segments for document $d_i$, $S_i \sim Poisson(\phi)$
2. Generate topic proportions, $\mu_i \sim Dir(\alpha)$
3. For each segment $s_{ij}$ in document $d_i$
  a. Choose $y_{ij}=y_{ij-1}$ with probability $Pr(c_{ij}=1) = \pi$
  b. Otherwise, choose a random topic for the segment, $y_{ij} \sim Multi_k(\mu_i)$
  c. Choose number of words for the segment, $N_{ij} \sim Poisson(E)$
  d. Generate segment topic proportions, $\theta_{ij} \sim Dir_K(\alpha, \gamma_{ij})$
  e. For each $w_{ij}$ in segment $s_{ij}$
    i. Choose a topic $z_{ij} \sim Multi_k(\theta_{ij})$
    ii. Choose word $w_{ij}$ from $Pr(w_{ij}|z_{ij}, \beta)$

**A framework for generative clustering.** Tompi and Tagarelli recognize the segment topic coherence assumption and provide a topic-based framework for clustering multi-documents using generative models [83]. Instead of assigning documents to clusters, Tompi and Tagarelli cluster documents based on their topic distributions. The proposed framework executes three steps. First, the documents are processed using standard preprocessing techniques to obtain the term matrix. Then, a generative model is applied to represent the documents in a topic latent space. The output of this step is a probability matrix expressing the topic mixture underlying the documents. In the final step, documents are clustered based on their topic mixtures, using an information theory PMF distance metric to compare documents. The clustering algorithm is a centroid-based linkage agglomerative hierarchical algorithm, like the one used by Ponti et al. in [84], which was described earlier.

## 13.5 Clustering Short Documents

Clustering short documents faces additional challenges above those of general purpose document clustering. Short documents normally address a single topic, yet they may do so with completely orthogonal vocabulary. Nucleus, contracted forms of words, and slang are prevalent in short texts. In this section, we will first discuss general methods for clustering short documents and focus on methods designed specifically for clustering Web documents and microblogs.

### 13.5.1 General Methods for Short Document Clustering

There has been a relatively large corpus of online approaches to the clustering of short texts. Wang et al. [111] propose a frequent-based parallel clustering algorithm specifically designed to handle large collections of short texts. The algorithm uses an information-inference mechanism to build a semantic text feature graph which by a k-NN-classification method to control the degree of cluster overlapping. Pepinto et al. [81] resort to information-theory field and define a symmetric KL divergence to control short documents for clustering purposes. Since the KL distance computation relies on the estimation of probabilities using term frequency, a special type of back-off scheme is implemented to avoid the issue of zero frequency due to the sparsity of text. Carullo et al. [18] describe an incremental clustering algorithm that utilizes a generalized Dice coefficient as a document similarity measure. The algorithm requires two thresholds: one to control the minimum accepted similarity and another to define the maximum similarity of a document that can still cluster to a definition.

Particle-swarm optimization techniques and bio-inspired clustering algorithms have also been proposed for short text data. Ingaramo et al. [52] develop a partitioning algorithm to handle short texts of arbitrary size. The key aspect of that is the adaptation of the AntTree algorithm [42], which integrates the "attraction of a cluster" and the Silhouette Coefficient concepts to detecting clusters. Each ant represents a single data object as it moves in the clustering structure according to its similarity to other already connected to the tree structure. Starting from an artificial support, all ants are incrementally connected, either to that support or to already connected ants. This process continues until all ants are connected to the structure, i.e., all objects are clustered.

Following the TermCut strategy, Ni et al. [78] propose two algorithms. The first tries to bisect clusters until the desired number of clusters is reached. The second takes a *minimal RMcut decrease threshold* as input, and as name suggests, continues the bisection process until the decrease in RMcut falls below the given threshold. While the idea behind the RMcut criterion is very similar to using the CLUTO cluster functions defined in Equations 13.5 and 13.6 of Table 13.2, Ni et al. show that their bisecting strategy outperforms CLUTO for a number of short text datasets.

### 13.5.2 Clustering with Knowledge Infusion

Motivated by the lack of common vocabulary in short documents, many document clustering algorithms first enrich or complement the statistical vector representation of terms with external knowledge, such as WordNet or Wikipedia. Banerjee et al. [8] propose to enrich the original term-feature space of search results with the titles of the Wikipedia articles retrieved as two queries for each result. The first query is based on the result title, while the other is based on the result description, snippet. Scaiaella et al. [93] propose a "graph of topics" model to represent each snippet, in which vertices correspond to Wikipedia pages that are identified by topic annotators, and the edges are weighted to determine the semantic relationship between the linked topics. An online spectral clustering is used on an induced graph consisting of two types of vertices, topics and snippets, where the weighted edges express either similar topics or topic-snippet matches.

User actions have also been useful in identifying short document clusters. Wang and Zhai [109] exploit information in log data produced by a real search engine to cluster result snippets. Carpineto and Romano [17] introduce a meta-clustering strategy that clusters snippets by integrating partitions separately as a result of analyzing the *tf-document* term-matrix with SVD, NMF, and generalized trees. They also define an evaluation measure that takes into account the behavior of Web users in the time spent to satisfy their search needs.
