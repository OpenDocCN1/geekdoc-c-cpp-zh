With respect to spatial queries, the observation that the intrinsic dimensionality of a data set is usually lower than the embedding dimensionality (based on interdependencies among attributes) has often been to overcoming the curse of dimensionality [48, 24, 113, 85]. Durrant and Kabán [45] show that the correlation between attributes is such an important event for avoiding the concentration of distances. Correlated attributes will probably also result in a intrinsic dimensionality that is considerably lower than the representational (embedding) dimensionality. This led to confronting the curse of dimensionality with the self-similarity blessing [85]. As opposed to the conjecture of Beyer et al. [27] and Francois et al. [55], however Durrant and Kabán [45] also show that low intrinsic dimensionality does not suffice to avoid the concentration, the correlation between attributes needs to be enough. Let us note that, different aspects such as a strong cluster-structure of data [25] and low intrinsic dimensionality [85] may be merely properties of a strong, though possibly latent, correlation between attributes. Durant and Kabán also identify relevant dimensions as the core of the problem of the curse. In essence, the ratio between noise (e.g., irrelevant attributes or additive noise masking in relevant attributes) and (latent) correlation in the attributes of a dataset will determine whether asking for the "nearest neighbor" is actually meaningful or not.

# Intrinsic Dimensionality vs. Embedding Dimensionality

An important implication of a strong correlation between several attributes is that the intrinsic dimensionality of a data set can be considerably lower than the so-called embedding dimensionality, the number of features of the data set. In short, the intrinsic dimensionality (or expansion dimension), as opposed to the embedding dimensionality, describes how much space is used by the data. These concepts have been discussed, e.g., by Gupta et al. [65].

# Aspect 5: Varying Relative Volume of an ε-Hypersphere

The relative volume of an ε-hypersphere (i.e., for the $L2$-norm as query distance) as compared to the volume of the data space varies extremely with the change of dimensionality. The data space is usually seen as a hypercube. Here, the maximally occurring distance (along the diagonal) is growing with the square root of the dimensionality. If we choose ε big enough to cover the complete data space in one or two dimensions, but steadily the corners of the data space will grow the range of the hypersphere. Figuratively, we could imagine the high-dimensional data space almost consisting of only corners.

As a consequence, what may seem a reasonable value of ε for a 3-dimensional query space may in a 20-dimensional space be extremely unlikely to contain any point of all, or versa, choosing ε big enough to cover some reasonable value in 20-dimensional space would engulf 3-dimensional subspace completely. This is an effect to keep in mind when designing subspace clustering algorithms. This effect (among others) has been discussed in more detail, although with respect to outlier detection in high-dimensional data, by Zimek et al. [142].
