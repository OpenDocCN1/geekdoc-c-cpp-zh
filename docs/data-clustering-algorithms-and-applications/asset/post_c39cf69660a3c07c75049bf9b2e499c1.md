### 24.1 Introduction

Since data clustering is a vast area, it is impossible to cover all material. Therefore, this chapter will summarize the key resources available. In general, these can be divided into (i) books, (ii) survey papers, and (iii) software.

This chapter is organized as follows: Section 24.2 presents educational resources. This section itself is divided into two subsections. The key books are discussed in 24.2.1, whereas the survey papers on data clustering are discussed in 24.2.2. Finally, the software on data clustering will be discussed in 24.3. Section 24.4 presents the conclusions.

### 24.2 Educational Resources

Educational resources are generic in the form of books or more focused on research. Books are generally more useful from a practical perspective, whereas survey articles are more useful for an academic perspective.

#### 24.2.1 Books on Data Clustering

Some of earlier books on data clustering are by Anderberg [3], Duran [9], and Hartigan [15]. Much of the developments in data clustering occurred after the writing of these books. Furthermore, these books were written before the advent of the modern age. Therefore, the perspective in these books is not necessarily optimized toward the computational design of algorithms.

Two of the earliest and well-known books after the advent of the modern age were written by Jain and Dubois [18] and by Kaufman and Rousseau [21]. These books discuss most of the classical clustering literature, in a way which is easy to understand and comprehend. In particular, the book by Kaufman and Rousseau, written in 2005, was updated to the most recent developments at time. Some of the recent books on data clustering, for example, Xu and Wusch [33], Mirkin [26] addresses the topic from the perspective of data recovery.

With the advent of database technology and the large amounts of data in the nineties, the issue of scalability has become increasingly important. Therefore, a number of algorithms have been proposed over the last 15 years, which have been designed to improve the scalability of the approach. The work in [14] is one of the recent books, which addresses many of the algorithms developed by the database community. Nevertheless, the work over the last 15 years is much broader and addresses many domains of data, along with scalability issues. To the best of our knowledge, there is no single book addressing all these issues. This book is therefore an attempt to fill the void in the field.
