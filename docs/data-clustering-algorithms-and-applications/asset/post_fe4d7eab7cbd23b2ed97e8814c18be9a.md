```
$O = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} W_{ij} \cdot (y_i - y_j)^2 \quad (1.4)$
```

The objective function $O$ can be rewritten in terms of the Laplacian matrix $W$. The Laplacian matrix is defined as $D - W$, where $D$ is a diagonal matrix satisfying $D_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} W_{ij}$. Let $\mathfrak{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_n)$. The objective function can be rewritten as:

```
$O = 2 \cdot \mathfrak{y}^T \cdot D \cdot \mathfrak{y} \quad (1.5)$
```

We need to consider a scaling constraint in order to ensure the trivial value of $y_i = 0$ for all $i$ is not selected by the problem. A possible scaling constraint is as follows:

```
$\mathfrak{y}^T \cdot D \cdot \mathfrak{y} = 1 \quad (1.66)$
```

Note that the matrix $D$ provides different weights to the data items, because it is assumed that nodes with greater similarity to different data items are more involved in the clustering process. This optimization formula is in generalized eigenvector format, and the value of $\mathfrak{y}$ is optimized by selecting the smallest eigenvalue for which the generalized eigenvector relationship $L\cdot\mathfrak{y} = \lambda\cdot D\cdot\mathfrak{y}$ is satisfied. In practice, the smallest eigenvalue corresponds to the trivial solution, where $\mathfrak{y}$ is the (normalized) unit vector. This trivial eigenvector is noninformative. Therefore, it can be discarded, and it is not used in the analysis. The second-smallest eigenvalue provides an optimal solution, which is more informative.

This model can be generalized to determining all the eigenvectors in ascending order of value. Such directions correspond to directions in the data space, which result in the best mapping. The set of $n$ eigenvectors $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$ (of which the first is trivial), with corresponding eigenvectors representing the vector representation of the data points along each eigenvector denoted by $v^1, \dots, v^n$. What do the large and small magnitude eigenvectors intuitively represent in the transformed space? By using the order of the data items along a small magnitude eigenvector to create a cut, the weight of the edges across the cut is likely to be small. Thus, this represents a cluster of the data items. At the same time, if $k$ largest eigenvectors are picked, the then representations $v^1, \dots, v^k$ preserve the maximum amount of information. Thus, spectral methods can be used to simultaneously perform clustering and dimensionality reduction (possibly nonlinear) shape of the data, which arguably at a rather high cost [78]. ## 1.2.6 The High Dimensional Scenario

The high dimensional scenario is particularly challenging for clustering because of the large variations in the behavior of the data attributes over different parts of the data. This leads to numerous challenges in data mining problems such as clustering, nearest neighbor search, and outlier analysis. It should be noted that many of the algorithms for these problems are dependent upon the use of distances as an important subroutine. However, with increasing dimensionality, the distances seem to increasingly lose their effectiveness and statistical significance because irrelevant attributes. The problem is that a smaller fraction of the attributes remains relevant with increasing dimensionality, which leads to the blurring of the data and increasing concentration effects, because of the averaging behavior of the irrelevant attributes. Concentration effects refer to the fact when many features are noisy and uncorrelated, their additive effects lead to all pairwise distances between data points becoming similar. The noise and concentration effects are problematic in two ways for distance-based clustering (and many others):

- An increasing noise from irrelevant attributes may cause errors in the data representation, so that it no longer properly represents the intrinsic distance between data objects.

- The concentration effects from the irrelevant dimensions lead to a reduction in the statistical significance of the results from distance-based algorithms, if used directly with distances that have not been properly denoted.

The combination of these issues above leads to questions about whether full-dimensional attributes are truly meaningful [8, 21]. While the natural solution to such problems is to use feature selection, the problem that different attributes may be relevant to different localities of the data. As is stated in [43]: "One of the problems of current nearest neighbor search is that they give equal weight to all features (dimensions), which are not of equal importance." Furthermore, the importance of a given dimension may not even be a problematic symptom. This has led to significant efforts to redesign data mining algorithms such as clustering, which are dependent on the notion of distances [4]. In particular, it would seem that data mining algorithms behave poorly with decreasing dimensionality from a qualitative perspective. It is well known that conventional distance measures were designed for many kinds of low-dimensional applications that are not suitable for the high-dimensional case. While high-dimensional data contain more information, they are also more complex. Therefore methods will do well with increasing dimensionality because of the noise effects of irrelevant attributes. Carefully designed functions can leverage the greater information in these dimensions and show improvement with dimensionality [4, 11] at least for few applications. Proposed clustering considered a local form of feature selection, or dimensionality reduction, in which the feature selection or transformation is performed to different localities of the data. Some of the earliest methods refer to these methods to dimensionality reduction [23] in order to emphasize the local feature selection effect. Thus, a projected cluster is defined as a set of clusters $C_1...C_n$, along with a representing of $L_1...L_n$, such that the clusters $C_i$ in the subset are represented by $L_i$. Thus, these methods are a local form of feature selection, which can be used to determine the relevant clusters in the underlying data. Note that the subset $L_i$ could represent a subset of the original attributes, or it could represent a transformed system in which the clusters are defined on a small set of orthogonal attributes. Some of the earliest projected clustering methods are discussed in [6, 9, 10]. The literature on this has grown significantly since 2000, and surveys on the area are being made in [67, 54]. An overview of algorithms for high-dimensional data will be provided in Chapter 9. Therefore, even when there is significant noise and concentration of the data, there will always be some of consistent variations over the similarity matrix because of the impact of the consistent attributes.
