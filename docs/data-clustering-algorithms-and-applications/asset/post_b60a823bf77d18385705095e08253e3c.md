# 18.1 Introduction

Many data sets are collected often have uncertainty built into them. In many cases, the underlying uncertainty can be easily measured and collected. When this is the case, it is possible to use the uncertainty in order to improve the results of data mining algorithms. This is because the uncertainty provides a probabilistic measure of the relative importance of different attributes in data mining algorithms. The use of such information can enhance the effectiveness of data mining algorithms, because the uncertainty provides a guidance in the use of different attributes during the mining process. Some examples of real applications in which may be used are as follows:

- Imprecise instruments and hardware are sometimes used in order to collect the data. In such cases, the level of uncertainty can be measured by prior experimentation. A classic example such hardware is sensors, in the measurements are often imprecise.

- The data may be input by statistical methods, such as forecasting. In such cases, the uncertainty may be inferred from the methodology used in order to perform the function.

- Many privacy-preserving data mining techniques use probabilistic perturbations [11] in order to reduce the fidelity of the underlying data. In such cases, the uncertainty may be unavailable as a result of the privacy-preservation process. Recent work [5] has explicitly connected the problem of privacy-preservation with uncertain data mining and has proposed a method which generates data, which is friendly to the use of uncertain data mining methods.

The problem of uncertainty has been studied in the traditional database literature [14, 43], though the issue has seen a revival in recent years [3, 5, 15, 19, 22, 40, 49, 51, 52]. The driving force behind this revival is the evolution of new techniques such as sensors which collect the data in a completely accurate way. In many cases, it becomes possible to collect the uncertainty along the data.

The problem of clustering is a well-known and important one in the data mining and management. The problem has been widely explored in the context of deterministic data. Details of a variety of clustering algorithms may be found in [38, 34].

Uncertainty modeling is very relevant in the context of a number of different applications. An example is illustrated in [42] where uncertainty was incorporated into the clustering process in the context of sales merchandising. Since the problem of data clustering is closely related to that of classification, the methods for uncertain data clustering can also be used for related data mining problems like outlier detection [9] and classification [3].

The presence of uncertainty significantly affects the behavior of the underlying clusters because the presence of uncertainty along a particular attribute may affect the expected distance between the data point and attribute. In real applications, there is considerable variation in the uncertainty behavior across different attributes. The incorporation of uncertainty into the clustering behavior can significantly affect the quality of the results.

The problem of data clustering is often confused with that of fuzzy clustering [50]. In case of uncertain data clustering, the uncertainty belongs to the representation of the source objects which are clustering, and the actual clustering model may be probabilistic or deterministic. In case of fuzzy clustering [50], the source objects are typically deterministic, and the membership of objects to clusters is probabilistic. In other words, each object has a degree of belonging to the different clusters, which is "fuzzy" or probabilistic in nature.

In this chapter, we will provide a survey of clustering algorithms for uncertain data. The main classes of algorithms for uncertain data are as follows:

- Mixture-Modeling Algorithms: Mixture clustering techniques use probabilistic models for clustering uncertain data. A classic example of an approach is given in [33], which uses an EM-approach [23] for the clustering process.
