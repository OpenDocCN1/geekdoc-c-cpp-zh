Shingling has been applied for extracting dense communities from large graphs [65]. In this work, each node's outgoing links are used to get the first-level shingle where each vertex v is associated with a number of shingles c, each of which includes n nodes selected from nodes to v points. After this inverted index is built that contains each first-level shingle and a list of vertices associated with the shingle. Second-level shingles (also known as meta-shingles) and sketches are built from first-level. Two first-level shingles are considered as relevant if they share at least one meta-shingle. A graph is constructed where nodes stand for first-level shingles and edges indicate the meta-shingle relationship. Clusters of first-level shingles are free to connect back to original nodes and include associated meta-shingles. The algorithm is bipartite and directed as well as undirected graphs and is very efficient and scalable [131].

**Different Definitions of Communities:** Community in a graph is most commonly defined as a subset of vertices connected internally and loosely connected to the rest of the graph. But there have been alternative characterizations and definitions of community in the literature.

One community research has been proposed by Aor and Parthasarathy in [8]. They define viewpoint neighborhoods as a group of influential and salient nodes from the viewpoint of a single node or subset of nodes in the graph. The viewpoint neighborhood basically clears the nodes local to the node or subset of nodes. These models use activation models in their algorithm are general enough to incorporate various notions of influence and silence. Viewpoint neighborhood provides a new and exciting tool for analysis of large graphs.

Another class of communities, as proposed by Lesovec et al. [101], is whiskers which is a group of nodes that are connected to the rest of the graph by only one edge (similarly, groups of nodes connected to the rest of the graph with two edges are called 2-whiskers). The authors find that to the measure of conductance, some of the communities in a variety of real-world networks are simply whiskers. They propose a core-and-whiskers model for structures of networks where most nodes have a core supported by whiskers to the rest of the graph by only one or two edges.

# 17.12 Hypergraph Partitioning

A hypergraph is a generalization of a graph where an edge, called hyperedge, can connect more than two vertices. Hypergraphs appear naturally in many problems such as Boolean satisfiability problem and circuit layout [129]. Hypergraphs are also popular in the workload partitioning parallel processing, restructuring sparse matrices [27], load balancing and scientific computing [28], scheduling of batch-shared I/O tasks in Grid [91] as well as in automatic management of memory hierarchies in global address space programming [94].
