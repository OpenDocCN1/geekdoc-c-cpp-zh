# 7.4.3.3 Objective Function vs. Clustering Performance

In clustering problems, when we have the external structure information (i.e., the derived classes of the data points), performance measures such as purity, normalized mutual information (NMI), and accuracy [109, 53, 39] have been used as the performance measure. In practice, clusters of a dataset could have many different shapes and sizes. In addition, these clusters can overlap with each other and cannot be easily identified and separated. As a result, it is difficult to effectively capture the clusters using a single (even the "best if exists") clustering objective function.

Here hence generally a gap between the objective function and the clustering performance measure [33]. In many real applications, when the clustering objective is optimized, the quality of clustering in terms of accuracy or NMI is usually improved. The improvements work up to a certain point, and beyond, further optimization of the objective function will not improve the clustering performance or capture the structure. It is also possible that the objective function is optimized, the clustering performance (e.g., accuracy or NMI) can be degraded.

# 7.4.3.4 Scalability

Many prior efforts have been reported on scaling up matrix factorization methods through algorithm design. While efficient computation algorithms for matrix factorization have developed, they have primarily been in the context of scientific/engineering applications where sizes of matrices (tens of thousands by tens of thousands with millions of nonzero values) are typically smaller than for Web analysis (millions-billions matrices with billions of observations). We present several practical mechanisms for dealing large-scale datasets for NMF:

+   - Shrinking scheme: The scheme first "coarsens" the large scale problem into a small one (by level), then solves the coarsened optimization problem, and finally refines the solution to solution to approximate the solution to the original problem. A typical example of the shrinking scheme is the multi-level graph partitioning [31, 54, 57].
- Partitioning scheme: The scheme first divides the original problem into a series of small scales, and then solves small problems, and finally combines their solutions to approximate the original problem. Typical examples of this scheme includewise principal component analysis [87], canopy-based clustering [85], and K-means clustering [121].
- Online/incremental scheme: The scheme does not require the input dataset to reside in the memory and performs factorization in an incremental fashion (i.e., one sample or a chunk of samples per step). Examples of the scheme include incremental NMF [16], online NMF by updating the factor matrices [13], online NMF using stochastic approximation [122, 52, 120], and evolutionary NMF [125, 94].
