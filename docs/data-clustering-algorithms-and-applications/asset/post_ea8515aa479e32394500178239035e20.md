Clustering of time-series data, like clustering for all types of data, has the goal of producing clusters with high intracluster similarity and low-cluster similarity. Specifically, objects belonging to the same cluster must exhibit high similarity to each other, while objects belonging to different clusters must exhibit low similarity, high distance from each other. However, some specific properties that are part of the time-series data—such as the dimensionality, the presence of noise, and the high feature correlation [53, 25]—pose unique challenges for designing efficient and clustering algorithms. In time-series clustering it is crucial to decide what kind of similarity is important for the clustering application. An appropriate algorithm and appropriate distance should be chosen. For example, Euclidean distance reflects similarity in time, while dynamic time warping (DTW) reflects similarity in shape. Other approaches, like model-based clustering methods such as Hidden Markov Models (HMM) or ARMA processes [56] are followed similarity in change magnitude.

A difference between time-series and clustering of objects in Euclidean space is that the time to be clustered may not be equal in length. When this is not the case, all time series are of equal length, standard clustering techniques can be applied by representing each time series as a vector and using a traditional $L_p$-norm [18]. With such an approach, similarity in time can be exploited, while similarity in magnitude and change are disregarded. In this study we split the clustering process into two steps. The first one is the choice of the similarity that will be employed, and the second one the grouping algorithm that will be employed. In the following section we classify the approaches into two categories: for one-dimensional time series and for multidimensional time series. ## 15.2 The Diverse Formulations for Time-Series Clustering

Time-series clustering lends itself to diverse formulations, which are highly application-specific in nature. For example, do we wish to determine sets of time series with similar online trends, or do we wish to determine time series with similar shapes? The objective function in these cases are also clearly different. However, the application-specific goals in these cases are also quite different, and the corresponding similarity functions used in the analytical process are therefore also quite different.

Broadly speaking, two major formulations exist for time-series data clustering:

- **Correlation-based Online Clustering**: In this case, a bunch of time series are clustered in real time on the basis of the correlations among the different time series. Such applications are common in scenarios such as financial markets, where it is desirable to determine groups of stocks which move over time. Such methods can also be used to find time series which have similar trends, or least correlated trends, over time. In these cases, a short window of history is used for the clustering process, and the similarity functions across different series are based on interattribute correlations. These methods are also intimately related to the problems of time-series forecasting and regression. In fact, a few cluster-methods using as Selective Muscles [58] use clustering methods for stream selection and efficient clustering. Other methods in the literature use such techniques for sensor select [1, 2]. An important aspect of such methods is that they often need to be performed in real time, as streams are evolving over time. In many cases, it is also desirable to detect significant changes in the clustering behavior. This provides insights into how the stream correlation trends have changed significantly.

- **Shape-based Off-line Clustering**: In these cases, time series of similar shapes are determined from the data. The main challenge here is to define an appropriate shape-based similarity function. This can be extremely challenging because it requires the definition of similarity functions which can capture the similarities in the shapes of the time series. Depending upon the domain, the time series may be translated, scaled and warped. Therefore, numerous similarity functions such as the Euclidean function or dynamic time warping are used for time-series clustering. Clearly, the appropriateness of a similarity function depends upon the lying data domain. Furthermore, some of the similarities are computationally expensive, since they require the use of dynamic programming methods for computation. Depending upon the application, the exact values on the timestamps may sometimes be important, as long as the shapes of the time series are similar. For example, consider the case, where the EEG readings of different patients are used to create a database of multiple series. In such cases, the exact value of the timestamp when the EEG reading measured was not quite as important. This is very different from online correlation-based versions of shape-based clustering are also used for online-clustering, as the methods perform subsequence clustering.

Clearly, the precise choice of model and similarity function depends upon the specific problem domain. Therefore, this chapter will examine the formations in the context of either application domains.

The problem becomes more complex as one moves to the multivariate scenario. The simplest multivariate scenario is that of trajectory clustering, which can be considered a bivariate or trivariate time-series data, depending upon whether 2- or 3-dimensional trajectories are considered. The aforementioned cases also apply to the case of trajectories where it is desirable to determine which move together (online) or similar shape (off-line). In the latter case, the exact value of the timestamp may not be as important. This is very different from online correlation-based versions of shape-based clustering.
