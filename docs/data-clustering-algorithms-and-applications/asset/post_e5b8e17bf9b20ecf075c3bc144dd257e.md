
## Clustering High-Dimensional Data

This problem is particularly well known in the area of pattern recognition. At a general level, this problem relates to the clustering problem. Seeking a clustering of data set is assuming that the data are generated by several functions (statistical processes). Hence, a clustering model would enable the user to identify the functional dependencies in the data set at hand and, thus, to eventually find new interests in the domain of the data set (i.e., models of the statistical processes). These functions are more complex when several attributes contribute to the actual relationships.

## Aspect 2: Concentration Effect of $L_p$-Norms

The second aspect of the curve of dimensionality is the deterioration of expressiveness of the most commonly used measures, the $L_p$-norms, with increasing dimensionality. In their general form, for $x,y \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $L_p$-norms are given as

```
$||x-y||_p = \left(\sum_{i=1}^d |x_i - y_i|^p\right)^{1/p}. \quad (9.1)
```

The choice of $p$ is crucial in high-dimensional data according to several studies [27, 76, 11]. The key result of Beyer et al. [27] states the following: if the ratio of the length of the any point vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (denoted by $||x||$) to the length of the mean point vector (denoted by $E||x||$) converges to zero with increasing dimensionality, then the proportional difference between the farthest-point distance $D_{max}$ and the closest-point distance $D_{min}$ (the relative contrast) vanishes.

```
$\lim_{d\to\infty} \frac{||x||}{E||x||} = 0 \implies \frac{D_{max}-D_{min}}{D_{min}} \to 0. \quad (9.2)
```

The preconditioning has been described as being valid for a broad range of data distributions. Intuitively, the relative contrast of ($L_p$) distances diminish as the dimensionality increases. This concentration effect of the distance measure reduces the utility of the measure for discrimination.

## Distance Concentration and Clustering

The distance-concentration effect means that far and close neighbors have similar distances, if they belong to the same distribution (and some mild conditions apply on the nature of the distribution). As clustering is concerned with data from different distributions, the crucial question is whether these distributions are sufficiently separated. In case, distance concentration is not an issue for the separation of clusters.

The behavior of $L_p$-norms (i.e., $p \in \mathbb{N}$) in high-dimensional spaces has been studied by Hinneburg et al. [76]. The authors showed by means of an analytic argument that $L_1$ and $L_2$ are the only norms useful for high-dimensional spaces. In addition, they studied the use of projections for discrimination, the effectiveness of which depends on localized dissimilarity measures that do not satisfy the triangle inequality of distance metrics. Fractional $L_p$ distance measures (with $0 < p < 1$) have been studied in a similar context by Aggarwal et al. [11]. The authors observe that smaller values of $p$ offer better results in high-dimensional settings.

These studies are often quoted as a motivation of specialized approaches in high-dimensional data. However, all these studies generally assume that the complete data follow a single data distribution, subject to certain restrictions. When the data, is actually

## Aspect 3: Irrelevant Attributes

A third aspect is often confused with the attribute which is actually independent. In order to rules describing some occurring phenomena, in many domains gout of data where single entities are described with many related attributes. Among the features of a high dimensional data set, for any query point, attribute can be expected to be irrelevant to describing object. Irrelevant attributes can interfere with the performance of similarity queries for that object. The relevance of certain attributes may differ for different groups of objects within the same data set. Thus, groups of data are defined by some of the available attributes only, many irrelevant attributes may interfere with the efforts to find these groups. Irrelevant attributes are often referred to as "noise."

## Distance Concentration, Clustering, and Relevance of Attributes

There is an important interplay between Aspect 3 and Aspect 2, as many irrelevant attributes (noisy features) can obfuscate the separation of distributions in the high-dimensional space. This has been studied in detail Hou et al. [78]. Additional relevant attributes, i.e., attributes that carry information regarding the separation of different distributions are actually helpful rather than prone to obstacles. In that case, more dimensions will actually make the clustering easier instead of more difficult!

## Aspect 4: Correlated Attributes

In a data set containing many attributes, there may be some correlations among subsets of attributes. From the point of view of feature reduction, but all one of these attributes may be redundant. However, from the point of view of a domain scientist who collected these attributes in the first place, it may be an interesting new insight that there are far unknown connections between features [5]. As for the previous problem, this phenomenon may be differently relevant for different subgroups of a data set as correlations among attributes that are characteristic for a cluster will be different for other clusters of the data set.

With respect to spatial queries, the observation that the intrinsic dimensionality of a data set is usually lower than the embedding dimensionality (based on interdependencies among attributes) has often been to overcoming the curse of dimensionality [48, 24, 113, 85]. Durrant and Kabán [45] show that the correlation between attributes is such an important event for avoiding the concentration of distances. Correlated attributes will probably also result in a intrinsic dimensionality that is considerably lower than the representational (embedding) dimensionality. This led to confronting the curse of dimensionality with the self-similarity blessing [85]. As opposed to the conjecture of Beyer et al. [27] and Francois et al. [55], however Durrant and Kabán [45] also show that low intrinsic dimensionality does not suffice to avoid the concentration, the correlation between attributes needs to be enough. Let us note that, different aspects such as a strong cluster-structure of data [25] and low intrinsic dimensionality [85] may be merely properties of a strong, though possibly latent, correlation between attributes. Durant and Kabán also identify relevant dimensions as the core of the problem. In essence, the ratio between noise (e.g., irrelevant attributes or additive noise masking relevant attributes) and (latent) correlation in attributes will determine whether asking for the "nearest neighbor" is actually meaningful or not.

## Intrinsic Dimensionality vs. Embedding Dimensionality

An important implication of strong correlation between several attributes is that the intrinsic dimensionality of a data set can be considerably lower than the so-called embedding dimensionality, the number of features of the dataset. In short, the intrinsic dimensionality (or expansion dimension), as opposed to the embedding dimensionality, describes how much space is used by the data. These concepts have been discussed, e.g., by Gupta et al. [65].

However, Aspects 1, 2, and 3 could still apply, depending on the number and nature of the remaining attributes.

## Aspect 5: Varying Relative Volume of an ε-Hypersphere

The relative volume of an ε-hypersphere (i.e., for the $L2$-norm as query distance) as compared to the volume of the data space varies extremely with the change of dimensionality. The data space is usually seen as a hypercube. Here, the maximally occurring distance (along the diagonal) is growing with the square root of the dimensionality. If we choose ε big enough to cover the complete data space in one or two dimensions, but steadily the corners of the data space will grow the range of the hypersphere. Figuratively, we could imagine the high-dimensional data space almost consisting of only corners.

As a consequence, what may seem a reasonable value of ε for a 3-dimensional query space may in a 20-dimensional space be extremely unlikely to contain any point of all, or versa, choosing ε big enough to cover some reasonable value in 20-dimensional space would engulf 3-dimensional subspace completely. This is an effect to keep in mind when designing subspace clustering algorithms. This effect (among others) has been discussed in more detail, although with respect to outlier detection in high-dimensional data, by Zimek et al. [142].
