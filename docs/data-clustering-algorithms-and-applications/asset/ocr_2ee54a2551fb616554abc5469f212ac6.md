A k-way partitioning of a hypergraph partitions the vertices into $k$ disjoint partitions. Typically to the problem tries to minimize a cost function. One standard cost function is *net cut* which is simply the sum of the weights of the edges that span more than one partition. Constraints are usually added to the partitioning problem. Where certain vertices are fixed in their partitions and balance constraint where the total vertex weight in each partition is balanced [129]. Optimal hypergraph partitioning with *balanced constraint* is known to be NP-hard [63]. However, heuristic algorithms with non-linear runtime have been developed in [22, 57, 90] and some refinements are proposed in [104, 47, 4, 84, 88].

*Clustering in hypergraph*, generally refers to the process of finding a coarser hypergraph from the input hypergraph by merging vertices into larger groups (clusters). Weight of a cluster is the sum of vertices within the cluster [129].

Performance is always a big concern for partitioning algorithms because in many of their applications the size of input graph increases considerably every year. For instance, in VLSI design the number of transistors grows exponentially following Moore's law, and thus, the algorithms must also scale for the inputs to be used effectively. In the following section, we provide a brief review of different partitioning schemes.

**Exhaustive Search:** These methods produce optimal partitions, but with an exponential complexity. Evaluation of each solution is the bottleneck and can be sped up by incrementally evaluating the cost. Iteratively updating the cut of a solution when a vertex is moved is straightforward[25]. However, the complexity grows exponentially in the number of vertices and makes it impossible for large, practical cases.

**Branch and Bound:** Intelligent pruning of the search space can be added to exhaustive methods to improve their scalability. This technique is known as Branch and Bound (B&B). It performs a recursive depth first search on the tree of assignment (vertices to partitions) and finds the best partition for each unassigned vertex. It can search the entire solution space in exponential time but maintains optimality by bounding suboptimal results. Different constraints can be used for pruning and bounding [129].

**Fidia-Mattheys Heuristic:** Even B&B methods take an impractical amount of time to produce the optimal output for realistic instances. The Fiduccia (FM) heuristic is an adaptive linear-time heuristic for fast improvement of hypergraph partitioning. The FM algorithms prioritize by gain. Here more is the change of assignment of a vertex to a partition and the gain is the corresponding change to the cost function. The algorithm runs in passes where each vertex is moved exactly once. The initial solution is usually produced by some randomized algorithm and then passes continue until convergence.

**Multilevel FM Framework:** The multilevel framework provides the best known results for large hypergraphs [129]. It involves three major steps as: coarsening, partitioning, and unarsening. MLFM is one of the best techniques for practical sized hypergraphs.

**Other techniques:** Several techniques have been proposed in the literature for partitioning hypergraphs [44, 50, 174]; they have some drawbacks that make it impractical to use them for real applications. Meta-heuristics such as simulated annealing and tabu search often produce better quality results at the cost of an impractical increase in runtime [50]. Other techniques have some constraints associated with them that limit their applicability in practical cases. One example is the spectral techniques [44]. Yang and Wong's [174] method relies on min-cut-flow algorithms and efficient network flow algorithms. These are polynomial-time algorithms that cannot take the balance constraint into account which are costly trial-and-error in flow-based partitioning [129].
