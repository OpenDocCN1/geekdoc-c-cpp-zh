Clusters of data objects that are computed by automatic algorithms are influenced by two major factors. First, complex data objects are described by high-dimensional feature vectors. In nearly all cases these are lossy descriptions of the original objects that neglect some aspects. Second, the definitions of clusters used by different algorithms involve complex operations rendering the algorithm into a black box for end users. Therefore, there is in most cases no easy answer to simple questions like this: Why are two specific points put together the same cluster? Furthermore, all definitions of clusters include pathological configurations that are unwanted but sometimes difficult to detect automatically.

The consequences of the interplay of the two factors—high-dimensional feature vectors and definitions of clusters—are in different degrees not accessible to humans. Visual aids will enhance the understandability of cluster analysis. Visualization techniques have been devised to use four main concepts to enhance the understanding of clusters. The first idea is to cluster the data directly using visual and interactive tools. No automated clustering is assured by the user. Decisions about how to group the data objects into clusters are made by the user. Therefore, the results would be understandable. This avoids problems caused by the second factor—the definitions of clusters by a black box. However, the latter—understanding data sets of high-dimensional feature vectors—becomes more difficult to be visually communicated to the user.

The second approach is to use an automated clustering algorithm. However, the black box is opened and intermediate states of the algorithm are visually communicated to the user. This implies the hope that the user better understands the clusters, when the cluster construction itself is documented. Further, the user might interact with the algorithm to search toward wanted interpretable clusters.

The third approach puts the use of a model selector. Many clustering algorithms with different parameters and distance measures are run on the same data. The results consist the space of clusterings that is presented to the user. The visual representation of clusters helps the user to navigate through the clustering. Alternative clusterings can be compared and possible iterations can be explored. The structured space helps the user to reduce the number of pairwise comparisons of clusterings.

The last concept is to use visualization techniques that help to inspect the found clusters in the context of the application at hand. These approaches break with the visual metaphor of a cluster selector. Instead, data objects themselves, the cluster information, and the semantic context are visualized.

# 19.2 Direct Visual and Interactive Clustering

Automated clustering algorithms are difficult to understand for nonexperts. Therefore, it seems natural to ask whether the black box with the clustering algorithm can be replaced by an interactive and visual procedure to find clusters. Such procedure could be operated by a domain expert, who understands both, data and context. This would lift the burden of understanding the impact of choosing a clustering algorithm and finding parameters for it to produce understandable and meaningful clusters.

Tools towards this end have been developed in the context of statistics and information visualization. A major challenge is to present multi- and high-dimensional data to the user in a meaningful way that allows visual cluster detection by hand. High-dimensional vectors are not easily mapped to visual attributes in a direct way. We discuss methods based on scatterplots as well as parallel coordinates in the next two sections.

# 19.2 Scatterplots

Multidimensional vectors cannot be shown directly in a space when the dimensionality is larger than two or three. Therefore, several low-dimensional projections are shown as multiple scatterplots instead. Those low-dimensional projections are linked either spatially or temporally to visualize high-dimensional phenomena.

A typical example for spatially linked projections is a matrix of scatter plots [19, 10]. While presenting all two-dimensional projections of the high-dimensional data makes it difficult to recognize interesting structures in the data. Further, when well-separated clusters are spotted in one of the two-dimensional projections, it is difficult or impossible to tell whether clusters in different projections are aligned. By coloring a cluster in one projection, the selected points are also colored in the other projections. The pattern of the colored points in the other projections indicates whether two clusters in different projections are aligned.

An alternative to brushing is linking projections. A well-known example is Grand Tour [6] which cycles through all two-dimensional projections. Between two axis-parallel projections, the projections are linearly interpolated. When the two-axis projections show one variable, the transition from one to the other corresponds to a rotation around the shared variable axis. This is well understood for users. Through the continuously changing positions of all points in the projections, the clusters in both projections are visually recognizable without brushing and linking. However, this property is lost, when the two projections do not share a common variable or the two projections are consecutive in the Grand Tour.

Both visualization techniques, matrix of scatterplots and Grand Tour, effective means to navigate the space of two-dimensional projections. Each node in the RnavGraph represents a two-dimensional parallel projection onto two variables. An edge between two nodes if the two nodes share a variable. The RnavGraph tool [45] uses RnavGraphs together with scatter plots to facilitate the exploration of high-dimensional data. It allows one to interactively drag from one node to another edge. This translates to a rotation in a three-dimensional space spanned by the variables in nodes adjacent to the edge. Through the continuously changing positions of all points in the projections, the clusters in both projections are visually recognizable without brushing and linking. However, this property is lost, when the two projections do not share a common variable or the two projections are consecutive in the Grand Tour.

The navigational graph does not necessarily include all two-dimensional projections as nodes. With larger dimensionality it would require $k$ nodes, which would render such navigational graph unless an effective map for the space of projections. In [33] the use of scagnostic measures is suggested to pick two-dimensional projections with interesting data distributions. Scagnostics are briefly introduced by Tukey and Tukey [44]. These ideas are further developed and described more in detail in [49]. In nutshell, a number of $k$ measures are computed for distributions in the axis-parallel projections of a d-dimensional set to guide the selection of interesting projections. The measures are designed to quantify a wide range of characteristics that appear in two-dimensional distributions. To illustrate the measures, the two-dimensional projections of the mtcars-dataset [22] were quantified by the proposed $k=9$ measures [49]. A subset of produced measurement vectors, each with a dimensionality of one, is shown with the respective distributions in a biplot in Figure 19.2. A biplot [15] reduces the multidimensional vectors to two principal components and plots the reduced vectors together with the projected unit vectors of the original data space.
