### 13.1 Introduction

The proliferation of documents, on both the Web and in private systems, makes knowledge discovery in document collections arduous. Clustering has been long recognized as a useful tool for the task. It groups items together, maximizing intra-cluster similarity and inter-cluster distance. Clustering can provide insight into the make-up of a document collection and is often used as the initial step in data analysis.
While document clustering research has focused on moderate length single documents, real-life collections are made of very short or long documents. Short documents do not contain enough text to accurately compute similarities. Documents span multiple topics that general document similarity measures do not take into account. In this chapter, we will first give an overview of general purpose document clustering, and then focus on recent advancements in the next frontier in document clustering: long and short documents.

### 13.2 Modeling a Document

Unlike the traditional clustering task, document clustering faces several challenges. Corpora are high-dimensional with respect to words, documents are sparse, are of varying length, and can contain correlated terms [2]. Finding a document model, a set of features that can be used to discriminate between documents, is key to the clustering task. The clustering algorithm and the measure used to compute similarity between documents is highly dependent on the chosen model.

### 13.2.1 Preliminaries

For the problem of document clustering, we are given a collection of documents, or texts, $\mathcal{V}=\{w_1,\dots,w_M\}$ represents the vocabulary of $\mathcal{D}$. Each document $d\in\mathcal{D}$ is a sequence of $n_d$ words. We denote the term vector of document $d$ by $d$. At times, we may consider $d$ as made up of nonoverlapping chunks of text, called segments, which are composed of sentences and words. A set of segments, $S$ is called a segment-set. We denote the set of segment-sets from a document $d$ and $S=\{U_{d_i}D_i\}$ as $C=\{C_1,\dots,C_K\}$ of clusters. Table 13.1 lists names used throughout this chapter.
Probabilistic generative algorithms (cf. Section 13.3.4) learn a lower dimension (latent) feature space that accommodates hidden documents (unsupervised class variables) with word occurrences (observed data). The following notation applies to all classes of documents. Documents are represented as sequences (rather than sets) of words, $d=(w_1,\dots,w_{n_d})$. Words in a document are represented as unit-vectors, where $w$ is a vector of size $M$ with $w^i=1$ and $w^j=0$ for all indices $i\neq j$. If the document is segmented, segments are also considered sequences of words. However, a document can be either a sequence or a set of segments. Each $z\in\mathcal{Z}=\{z_1,\dots,z_K\}$, the latent topics, is a distribution over the vocabulary. Topic proportions, e.g., $\alpha$ and $\theta$, are distributions over the percentage of the document or segment that are drawn from each topic. Topic assignments $z$ and $y$ tell which topic was selected for choosing a term or document, respectively.
