
## 17.1 Introduction

Networks are ubiquitous—ranging from network of computers in the World Wide Web, to connections between users on social networks, from Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) networks to citation network among authors, from follower–follow network (on Twitter and similar networks) to dependency structure between constituent tasks of a large program. Recent advances in technology have resulted in a diverse range of generating network data. Some domains range from social [120, 169] to biological [81] from scientific [37, 119] to ecological [14, 153] and the like. Study of such networks can reveal important information about community structures over connection patterns, influence of nodes etc. [11, 12, 68, 124].

Clustering is one of the most important operations to apply on a network for valuable information from the network. Many other clustering algorithms on other data can be solved as a special case of network clustering. For instance relational data can be expressed as entity-relation graphs, general object-driven data can be represented with a (sparse-)similarity matrix nodes representing the different objects (spectral clustering of relational data is based on this) and so on. Some of the early algorithms (e.g., Chameleon [85]) on clustering generic data were designed as a special case of graph clustering. Graph clustering has been studied intensely for its practical applications [90, 57, 78]. Recent algorithms have been developed considering the structure and size of modern networks in mind. Examples include multigraph partitioning algorithms as Metis [86], Gracus [42], and MLR-MLC [148]. These algorithms are scalable and can deal with some of the biggest graphs [148]; with millions of vertices and edges of Spectral clustering methods form an important class of graph partitioning. They use weighted cuts [151] and are are very effective in terms of quality in the context of social networks. Another important class of graph clustering algorithms involve Markov Clustering (MC) [120] that is based on stochastic simulation. One drawback of the original MC, however, is the scalability. Improvements to the algorithm have been proposed and the limitations can be reduced while retaining the advantageous features [148, 150]. This has also been the use of hybrid algorithms as Metis-MQI.

Some of the applications of graph clustering ranging from analysis of social networks PPI networks [19], from VLSI design [46] to load balancing in distributing computing based on functions such as similarity and distance have become important aspects of large network analytics. Some additional applications include community discovery for proxy caches in World Web content and detection of link farms [20, 65], for personalized recommendation systems [141], for efficient routing in mobile-hoc networks [157], and so on. Other important applications are summarization of activities within network for a better understanding of the interaction between groups and how the network evolves [10], identification of influential nodes for marketing purposes [43, 100], and prediction of user rating for given items [93].

In recent years, many scalable algorithms have been proposed to efficiently produce high quality clusters from graphs. Networks can be perfectly represented with graph data structures and clustering networks, in general, similar to the problems of graph clustering. In this chapter, we will discuss various techniques proposed in the literature for clustering networks/graphs.

## 17.2 Background and Nomenclature

Before discussing various approaches in detail we like to clarify the terms clustering, graph partitioning, and community discovery, which are often used interchangeably in the literature. The term clustering is often described as a method by which assigns or organizes records, objects, or entities into groups (clusters) wherein members of each group are more similar to another (by some definition) and less to elements that lie outside of such group. Clustering may be further categorized as hard or soft—the former refers to the case where each element strictly belongs to at most one group, or partial—the latter refers to the case where all elements are placed into one or more groups, and balanced—the former refers to the case where the elements of groups are roughly similar, the latter when there is high skew among the candidates.

Graph partitioning is a term that is often associated with a hard form of clustering on graphs, that is, complete and perfectly balanced. This concept is natural in many applications such as VLSI design [46], and the partitioning of physical nodes within a cluster environment [17]. Community discovery is a term that refers to a clustering on graphs that yields tight clusters, typically from a topological standpoint, and hard or soft groupings depending on the needs of the domain. Although very similar to graph clustering, the term is frequently used in domains where one is interested only in finding the most densely connected components of the graph and not in the cluster assignment of the vertices. In such scenarios, a complete clustering of the whole network is not necessary. Rather, finding the most interesting groups often suffices. Lots of computations can be saved by clustering only the densely connected parts of the network [65].

## 17.3 Problem Definition

Networks are represented using graph data structures that contain sets of vertices and edges between vertices. In formal notations, a graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, E)$ consists of a set of $n$ vertices $\mathcal{V}=\{v_1, v_2, ..., v_n\}$ and a set of edges $E$. An edge $e_{ij}$ represents a connection between vertex $v_i$ and $v_j$. Both edges and vertices can have weights associated with them. A weighted edge represents not only a connection between the nodes, but also a measure of similarity, distance, between the two nodes. Graphs can be directed or undirected. Edges are symmetric ($e_{ij} = e_{ji}$) in undirected graphs while they are not in directed graphs ($e_{ij} \neq e_{ji}$).

Let $k$ be positive integer. The graph partitioning problem can be posed as a $k$-way partition of $G$ into $n$ nonempty subsets $C_1,...,C_k$ of $G$ such that $\bigcup_{i=1}^k C_i = G$. In general, the partitioning problem has two objectives: the graph should be clustered in such a way that each cluster contains roughly the same number of vertices and the number of edges between clusters is minimized thus other objectives can be defined based on application requirements.

Given the formulation of the general graph clustering problem, we first discuss commonly used evaluation criteria followed by a detailed discussion and analysis of different graph clustering algorithms proposed in the literature.

## 17.4 Common Evaluation Criteria

In this section, we give an overview of commonly used performance evaluation criteria for clustering algorithms. A variety of measures has been proposed in the literature to capture the goodness of a partition of the graph [131].

The simplest function to measure the quality of a partition is “cut” and the most direct way to construct the partition is to solve the mincut problem [105]. Given the number k of partitions, the mincut problem chooses a partition $C_1, \dots, C_k$ that minimizes

$cut(C_1, \dots, C_k) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^k W(C_i, \bar{C}_i)$

where $W(C_i, \bar{C}_i)$ is the cut. For k=2 mincut is relatively simple and an easy problem to solve [158]. But the partitions are not satisfactory. More specifically, the solution tends to separate one vertex from the rest of the graph [105]. In partitioning, each group should be large enough. There are two popular objective functions to include the aspect of a partition. RatioCut, proposed in [74], of a group of vertices is the sum of the weights connecting C to the rest of the graph normalized by its size. 

$RatioCut(C_1, \dots, C_k) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{W(C_i, \bar{C}_i)}{|C_i|}$

Probably the most popular quality for graph partitioning is normalized [151, 114]. The normalized cut of a group of vertices is the sum of the weights of the edges connecting C to the rest of the graph normalized by total edge weight of C and total edge weight of the rest of the graph. Mathematically, the normalized cut of a partition of the graph $\mathcal{V}'$ is denoted as

$Ncut(C_1, \dots, C_k) = \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{W(C_i, \bar{C}_i)}{d(C_i)}$

where $d(C)$ is the total degree of cluster C The intuition in both RatioCut and Normalized Cut is to make sure partitions are “balanced” [105]. Groups with low normalized represent good communities because they are well connected among themselves and sparsely connected to the rest of the graph. the conductance is defined in [83] and is closely related. It is defined as

$Conductance(C) = \frac{\sum_{i \in C} W(i,j)}{\min(d(C), d(\bar{C}))}$

The normalized cut or conductance of a partition of the graph into k clusters $C_1, \dots, C_k$ is the sum of the normalized cut or conductance of the individual partitions $C_i$ for $i=1, \dots, k$. Another popular measure of partitioning quality is the Modularity [125]. This measure is the sum of the differences, for each cluster, between fraction of internal edges and fraction of edges that are expected to be inside a random cluster with the same total degree. Mathematically,

$Q = \sum_{i=1}^k [\frac{W(C_i, \bar{C}_i)}{e} -(\frac{d(C_i)}{e})^2]$

where $C_i$ are clusters, $e$ is number of edges, and $d(C_i)$ represents the total degree of cluster C.
