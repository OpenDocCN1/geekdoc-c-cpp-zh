### 5.1 Introduction

Many of the well-known clustering algorithms, implicitly or explicitly, the assumption that data are generated from a probability distribution of a given type, e.g., from a mixture of $k$ Gaussian distributions. This is the case in particular for EM (Expectation Maximization) clustering and for k-means. Due to this assumption, these algorithms cluster spherical clusters and cannot deal well with datasets in which the actual clusters are nonspherical. Clusters occur naturally in spatial data, i- or three-dimensional concrete space corresponding to our real world. Spatial data includes points, lines, polygons and support a broad range of applications. Clusters in spatial data may have arbitrary shape, i.e., they are drawn-out, elongated, etc., because of the constraints imposed by geographic entities such as mountains and rivers. In geo-marketing, one may find to find clusters of a given characteristic, e.g., high-income homes, while in crime analysis one of the goals is to detect hot-spots, i.e., clusters of certain types of crimes. In higher-dimensional data the assumption of a certain number of clusters of a given shape is very strong and may be violated. In such cases, algorithms as k-means will break up or merge the actual clusters, leading to inaccurate results. The objective to minimize the average squared distances of points from their corresponding cluster center leads to a partitioning of the dataset that is equivalent to the Voronoi diagram of the clusters, irrespective of the shape of the actual clusters.
