To tackle the challenges with short time-series data, the authors of [36] proposed an algorithm specifically designed for clustering time-series expression data. The algorithm works by signing genes to a defined set of model profiles that capture the potential distinct patterns. After analysis and can be combined into clusters. Using immune response data, the authors have shown that their algorithm can correctly detect the most significant genes, retained for further analysis. Using Gene Ontology-based evaluation, the algorithm outperformed both gene clustering algorithms and algorithms designed for clustering time-series gene expression. STEM is a toolkit that is available based on this work for the analysis and clustering of short time series gene data

In certain scenarios, a gene might not be correlated with other genes at that time but might regulate another after a certain time. In order to identify time-lagged coregulated clusters, [53] proposes a novel clustering algorithm for effectively extracting the time-lagged clusters. This algorithm first generates time-information for gene clusters by processing several genes. Instead of considering the entire for it identifies only interesting parts (subsequences) of the genes that are coregulated where there is no direct relationship between the pair and/or gene clusters. It builds a new mechanism to extract clusters (which are referred to as q-clusters) of time-lagged coregulated genes over a subset of consecutive editions. Each such cluster contains information of genes that have similar expression patterns over a set of consecutive ones. More authors, in [113] have extended the content of time-lagged clustering to three-dimensional clustering.

# 16.2.7 Cluster Validation

All the clustering algorithms that are described in this section will yield groups of co-expressed genes or groups of samples with a common phenomenon [22]. Reliability of the clusters, which measures the probability that the clusters are not formed by chance, is a commonly used metric for validating the results of these clustering algorithms. To compute the p-values of a cluster, typically the genes from a given cluster are mapped to the functional categories annotated in databases such as Martinsried Index of Protein Sequences (MIPS) or Gene Ontology (GO) [87]. Typically, a hypergeometric distribution is used to calculate the probability of having at least $k$ genes from a cluster of size $n$ genes by chance in a biological process containing $f$ genes from a total size of $N$ genes:

```

```

This test measures if a gene cluster is enriched with genes from a particular functional category to a greater than what would be expected by chance. The range of p-values is from 0 to 1. Lower p-values indicate biological significance of the clusters.

Another popular metric for evaluating the goodness of the clusters is the Figure of Merit (FOM) which was originally proposed in [119] to estimate the predictive power of clustering algorithms. The FOM measures the mean deviation of the expression levels of genes in a particular condition relative to their corresponding cluster mean. Thus, a small value of FOM indicates high predictive ability of the resulting clusters.
