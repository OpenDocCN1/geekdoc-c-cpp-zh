
## 21.3.2.3 Eigenvectors of the Laplacian Matrix

Dasgupta and Ng [10] show that alternative clusterings can be found by looking at different eigenvectors of the Laplacian matrix. The input is a similarity matrix $S$ and no background knowledge is used. There is the strong requirement that each alternative clustering output is constrained to two clusters. The approach is a spectral, where the objects are represented as a graph with edges indicating pairwise similarity, and the partitioning is generated using a normalized cut. Let $D_{ii} = \sum_j S_{ij}$ and the Laplacian matrix $L = D^{-1/2}(D-S)D^{-1/2}$. The alternative clustering is produced by applying 2-means to the objects represented by $e_2$, the eigenvector corresponding to the second smallest eigenvalue of $L$. The $m^{th}$ alternative clustering is produced by applying 2-means to the objects represented by $(m+1)^{th}$ eigenvector. The objective is achieved by the orthogonality of the different eigenvectors. Quality is achieved by the 2-means algorithm, and the second and later alternatives will be "suboptimal," compared to the first, since the optimality decreases as $m$ increases. The approach has the advantage of being simple to implement, but the limitation of two clusters in clustering is restricted.

## 21.3.2.4 Decorrelated k-Means and Convolutional EM

The approach by Jain et al. [22] is a simultaneous one for generating two clusters $C_1$ and $C_2$, without using any background knowledge. Assuming each of the output clusters has $k_1$ and $k_2$ clusters respectively; then, they are generated in a decorrelated fashion using $k$-means style. The objective function is

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k_1} \sum_{x\in C_i} ||x-\mu'_i|^2 + \sum_{j=1}^{k_2} \sum_{x\in C_j} ||x-\mu'_j|^2 + \lambda(\beta^T\mu'_i)^2 + \lambda(\alpha^T\nu'_j)^2$$

where $\lambda$ is a parameter used for regularization, $\mu'$ and $\nu'$ are the representative vectors of clusters $C'_1$ and $C'_2$, respectively, and $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_j$ are the mean vectors of $C_1$ and $C_2$, respectively. The initial two terms correspond to $k$-means type error, while the second terms ensure similarity (decorrelation) between the two clusters. The objective function can be extended to generate more than 2 clusters, by including an extra $k$-means type error for each new clustering and including a pairwise dissimilarity for each possible pair of clusters. An iterative approach is used to minimize the objective function. The regularization parameter $\lambda$ is set empirically, and is also possible to extend the objective to a kernelized version to handle nonlinearities. A disadvantage is that the representative vectors in the decayed $k$-means algorithm do not have a natural interpretation for the user.

In a companionship proposal to decorrelate $k$-means, the work in [22] also outlines a convolutional expectation maximization (EM) algorithm, where it is assumed that the data can be modeled as the sum of mixtures of distributions, each of which is associated with a cluster. One clustering has $k_1$ clusters; the other has $k_2$ clusters. However since the distribution of two independent variables is the convolution of the distributions, the data is modeled as sampled from a convolution of mixtures. This leads to the problem of learning a convolution of mixtures using an EM method to determine the distributions. The technique is again simultaneous and can be kernelized.

## 21.3.2.5 CAMI

The CAMI [8] algorithm is designed to discover alternative clusterings at the same time using the original data. Formulating the clustering problem under mixture models, CAMI optimizes a dual-objective function where log-likelihood (accounting for clustering quality) is maximized, while mutual information between two clusterings (accounting for dissimilarity) is minimized. The objective function of CAMI is

$$L(\Theta, D) = L(\Theta^1; D) + L(\Theta^2; D) - \eta \sum_{i,j} p(C_i, C_j) \log \frac{p(C_i, C_j)}{p(C_i)p(C_j)}$$

The first two terms correspond to the likelihood of each of the two clusterings will be simultaneously discovered and $\Theta^1$ and $\Theta^2$ are their parameters. The third term corresponds to dissimilarity between $C_1$ and $C_2$ as measured by mutual information. $\eta$ is a regularization parameter used to trade off similarity and quality (which can be specified by the user).

## 21.3.3 Guided Generation with Constraints

The next class of techniques uses constraints to guide the generation of one or more alternative clusterings. The type of constraints used distinguishes:

### 21.3.3.1 COALA

COALA method takes into account a similarity matrix and a single clustering as background knowledge. A set of "cannot-link" constraints is generated for each pair of objects in the same cluster. Intuitively, it is less desirable for these pairs to be together in the same cluster of the alternative. A hierarchical clustering algorithm is used. At each iteration, COALA finds two pairs of clusters for possible merge, denoted as $(q_1, q_2)$, called qualitative pair, and $(o_1, o_2)$, called dissimilar pair. The qualitative pair is the one with the minimum distance over all pairs of clusters. The dissimilar pair has the maximum distance over all pairs of clusters that satisfy cannot-link constraints. Given a tradeoff parameter $\omega$, if $d(q_1, q_2) \geq \omega$, then the pair $(o_1, o_2)$ is merged. Otherwise, the pair $(q_1, q_2)$ is merged.

COALA is a simple technique and has been used as a baseline method for comparison in range of papers. A limitation of COALA is that it is specifically tied to hierarchical clustering algorithm. It was not formulated for the case of generating multiple clusterings. However, it is easy to make generalizations where multiple clusterings are used as background knowledge, yielding a larger set of cannot-link constraints for generating the alternative clustering.

### 21.3.3.2 Constrained Optimization Approach

The approach of Qi and Davidson [3] uses constraints in a different way. It takes the original dataset $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ and transforms it into a new dataset $Y = \{y_1, \dots, y_n\}$, where $Y = DX$ and $D$ is a transformation matrix representing a distance metric. Any clustering algorithm can then be used to generate the new dataset as an alternative to the original clustering as a constrained optimization task.

The objective function is

$$\min_{B \geq 0} D_{KL}(p(y_i) || p(x_i)) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1, x_i \in C_j} ||x_i - \mu_j||_B^2 \leq \beta$$

where $B = D^T D$, $||\cdot||_B$ is the Mahalanobis distance using matrix $B$, and $B \geq 0$ signifies that $B$ is required to be positive semidefinite.

### 21.3.3.3 MAXIMUS

Work by Baie and Dong [3] describes an algorithm known as MAXIMUS for discovering alternative clusters in a sequential manner. MAXIMUS is based on the use of ADCO, which can compare clusters according to their spatial characteristics. At a high level, the ADCO measure constructs a spatial histogram for each cluster and represents a clustering as a vector containing the spatial histogram counts. Intuitively, the output of ADCO is a containment judgment between a clustering $C_1$ and a clustering $C_2$, expressed as "How much of $C_2$ is contained in $C_1$" or "What percentage of $C_2$ is contained in $C_1$?”

Using the ADCO measure, one may generate a spatial template to ensure that a single alternative clustering has maximum (average) dissimilarity from the background clusterings. This template describes how many objects must be present in one-dimensional projections of the feature space for each bin. Next, consensus clustering is then used to combine the clusterings from all the bins into a single clustering. Using the template, a k-means algorithm is used to derive a clustering for the feature space. Thus, the quality of the alternative clustering is achieved by the use of k-means and consensus clustering. The dissimilarity objective is achieved by using the integer programming model and the ADCO measure to obtain a spatial template which can be expected to have very high dissimilarity from the background knowledge.

In order to use MAXIMUS, it is necessary to specify the binning strategy for representing the density profile (10 bins equidensity is recommended as a default). Unlike some other algorithms, MAXIMUS does not require the user to specify a regularization parameter to trade off between the quality and dissimilarity objectives.
