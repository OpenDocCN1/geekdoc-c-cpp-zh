As mentioned, approaches to clustering in arbitrarily oriented subspaces are also known as "correlation clustering" [32, 141]. It should be noted, though, that this term is ambiguously used for a completely different problem in machine learning where a partitioning of the data correlate as much as possible with a pairwise similarity function $f$ learned from past data [22].

# 9.5 Open Questions and Current Research Directions

Some interesting research directions have found attention in the workshop series on "Discovering, Summarizing and Using Multiple Clusterings" (MultiClust) at KDD 2010 [50], at ECML/PKDD 2011 [109], and at SDM 2012 [1110]. Intriguingly, problems in subspace clustering similar problems in other areas such as ensemble clustering, alternative clustering, or multiv clustering [93]. Such problems common to all of these areas are (i) how to treat diversity of clustering solutions (are diverse clustering solutions to be unified or be presented?), (ii) how to effectively summarize and present density to a user of clustering algorithms,(iii) how to treat redundancy of clusters, and how to assess similarity between multiple clustering solutions. Relations to frequent pattern matching, which was godfather at the origin of subspace clustering, have been discussed from a more recent point of view on research in fields [94].

As we pointed out, redundancy of subspace cluster results is a problem inherited from the bottom-up strategy of the very first approaches, borrowed from frequent pattern matching. For current research on subspace clustering, getting rid of too much redundancy is a major topic [70, 105]. Research on multustering [28, 36, 80, 72] seems to approach the problem from the opposite direction but equally at the same issue, seeking to allow some redundancy in order to exclude possibly interesting concepts although they might partially have a certain overlap with other clusters. Related thought to tackling the problem of distinctiveness of different clusters by directly assessing a certain notion of distance between different partitions (so-called alternative clustering approaches) [60, 61, 21, 38, 19, 37, 116].

A question related to the redundancy issue concerns the appropriate density level. This is also a general problem in density-based clustering [81], but for clustering in subspaces, the problem is aggravated. A fixed density threshold for an APPRI-style subspace search is not appropriate for all possible subspaces. For example consider CLIQUE-style grid approach: the volume of a hypercube increases exponentially with the dimensionality; the density decreases. As a consequence, any chosen threshold needs to identify clusters of (up to) certain dimensionality. This motivates research on adaptive density thresholds [18, 106]. When using Euclidean distance ($L_2$), the appropriate choice of e-range becomes extremely challenging as well, not only for subspace but for arbitrarily oriented clustering, due to the counter-intuitive behavior of the volume of the hypersphere with increasing dimensions. Choosing the size of the neighborhood in terms of objects or radius $k$ nearest neighbors of an e-range query has been advocated as a workout for this problem [7], to solve at least certain aspects such having a well-defined (nonempty) set of objects for the density estimation or for the estimation of spatial characteristics of the neighborhood.
