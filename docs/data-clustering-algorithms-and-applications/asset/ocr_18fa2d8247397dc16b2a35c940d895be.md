clustering on the measures is performed by using their correlation matrix. The resultant hierarchy can be found in Figure 23.5 (“s” means the similarity). As mentioned before, $R_n$, $\Gamma_n$, $J_n$, and $MS_n$ are equivalent, they have perfect correlation with one another and form the first. The second group contains $FM_n$ and $\Gamma_n$. These two measures behave similarly, and have just slightly weaker consistency with the measures in the first group. $VD_n$, $\epsilon_n$, and $VI_n$ have obviously weaker consistency with other measures in a descending order.

Furthermore, the data sets in Table 23.8 are divided into two repositories to explore the source of inconsistency among the measures, where $\mathfrak_1$ contains data sets with $CV_0 < 0.8$, and $\mathfrak_2$ contains the rest. After computing the correlation matrices of the measures on the two repositories (denoted by $M(\mathfrak_1)$ and $M(\mathfrak_2)$), their difference $|M(\mathfrak_1) - M(\mathfrak_2)|$ can be calculated as shown in Table 23.9. As can be seen, roughly, all the measures $VI_n$ show weaker consistency with one another on data sets in $\mathfrak_2$. In other words, while $VI_n$ acts in the opposite way, most measures tend to disagree with one another on data sets with highly imbalanced classes.
