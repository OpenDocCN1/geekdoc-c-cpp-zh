Clustering of time-series data, like clustering for all types of data, has the goal of producing clusters with high intracluster similarity and lowcluster similarity. Specifically, objects belonging to the same cluster must exhibit high similarity to each other, while objects belonging to different clusters must exhibit low similarity, high distance from each other. However, some specific properties that are part of the time-series data—such as the dimensionality, the presence of noise, and the high feature correlation [53, 25]—pose unique challenges for designing efficient and clustering algorithms. In time-series clustering it is crucial to decide what kind of similarity is important for the clustering application. An appropriate algorithm and appropriate distance should be chosen. For example, Euclidean distance reflects similarity in time, while dynamic time warping (DTW) reflects similarity in shape. Other approaches, like model-based clustering methods such as Hidden Markov Models (HMM) or ARMA processes [56] are followed similarity in change magnitude.

A difference between time-series and clustering of objects in Euclidean space is that the time to be clustered may not be equal in length. When this is not the case, all time series are of equal length, standard clustering techniques can be applied by representing each time series as a vector and using a traditional $L_p$-norm [18]. With such an approach, similarity in time can be exploited, while similarity in magnitude and change are disregarded. In this study we split the clustering process into two steps. The first one is the choice of the similarity that will be employed, and the second one the grouping algorithm that will be employed. In the following section we classify the approaches into two categories: for one-dimensional time series and for multidimensional time series. We present the traditional classification that defines three main classes of clustering algorithms: (a) $k$-means and its variants, (b) hierarchical approaches, and (c) density-based clustering. In addition to these three main classes, in Subsection 15.5.4 we discuss methods for trajectory clustering, as we consider this field to be of individual interest.

# 15.2 The Diverse Formulations for Time-Series Clustering

Time-series clustering lends itself to diverse formulations, which are highly application-specific in nature. For example, do we wish to determine sets of time series with similar online trends, or do we wish to determine time series with similar shapes? The objective function in these cases are also clearly different. However, the application-specific goals in these cases are also quite different, and the corresponding similarity functions used in the analytical process are therefore also quite different.

Broadly speaking, two major formulations exist for time-series data clustering:

+   • Correlation-based Online Clustering: In this case, a bunch of time series are clustered in real time on the basis of the correlations among the different time series. Such applications are common in scenarios such as financial markets, where it is desirable to determine groups of stocks which move over time. Such methods can also be used to find time series which have similar trends, or least correlated trends, over time. In these cases, a short window of history is used for the clustering process, and the similarity functions across different series are based on interattribute correlations.
