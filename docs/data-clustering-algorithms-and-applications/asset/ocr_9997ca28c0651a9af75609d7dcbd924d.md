# 12.1 Introduction

A growing number of clustering algorithms for categorical data have been proposed in recent years, with interesting applications, such as partitioning large software systems [8, 9] and protein interaction data [13, 21, 38, 77].

A categorical dataset with m attributes is viewed as an m-dimensional "cube", offering a spatial density for clustering. A cell of the cube is mapped to the number of objects having values to its coordinates. Clusters in such a cube are regarded as subspaces of high density and are represented by subarrays of low object density. Clustering the cube poses several challenges:

(i) Since there is no ordering of attribute values, the cube cells have no ordering. The search for dense subspaces might consider several orderings of each dimension of the cube to identify the best clustering (unless all the attributes are binary values).

(ii) The density of a subspace is often defined relative to a user-specified value, such as a radius. However, different radii are preferable for different subspaces of the cube [17]. In dense subspaces where no information should be missed, the search is more accurately "by cell" with a radius of 1. In sparse subspaces, a higher radius may be preferable to aggregate information. The cube search could start from a low radius and gradually move to higher radii. Although the term radius is used from geometrical analytics that assume circular constructs, with categorical data radius is not a Euclidean distance.

Figure 12.1 illustrates a 3-dimensional cube with subspaces that may be clusters. Figure 12.2 shows examples of creating and expanding clusters in a 3-D dataset. The radius is the maximum number of dimensions by which neighbors differ.

Categorical clustering algorithms have often been motivated by density-based clustering, as CLIQUE [4], CLICKS [75], CACTUS [32], COOLCAT [19], DBSCAN [29, 68], OPTICS [17].
