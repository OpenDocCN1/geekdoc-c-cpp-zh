# 20.6 Experimental Analysis

In this section, we provide an empirical evaluation of SSC with graph-based representations. Our experiments are divided into two parts: First, we compare seven methods on 14 benchmark data sets from their accuracy; we take a closer look at the performance of label propagation methods obtained from the perspective of semupervised manifold embedding. For the first set of experiments, we consider 6 standard methods: GF, LNP, CK, GWEM, TIRKE, and LG, as discussed in Section 20.4. In addition we use LTSALP, an approach based on semi-supervised embedding. For the second set of experiments, we consider the 6 embedding methods introduced in Section 20.5.

**Methodology:** We conducted experiments on the 8 UCI data sets: Hepatitis, Cancer, Pima, Wine, Glass, USS (1-4), and E and F. In fold-set we also ran experiments on 6 well-known benchmark data sets. These are subsets of the 20Newgroup data set: Different100, Similar100, Difference1000 and Same1000 [1]. Each dataset contains a subset of 3 groups with varying degrees of difficulty for clustering. Difference100 (1000) includes comp.altheism, res.baseball, and sci.space and are, hence, easy to cluster; Same100 (1000) includes comp.graph, comp.os-windows, comp.window.x and talk.politics, talk.politics.midelate, and talk.politics.misce are moderately difficult to cluster. Whereas Similar100 is more difficult to cluster. We also used the well-Classic-3 benchmark data set which contains 3893 entries, of which 1033 are from medical journals, 1460 are information retrieval papers. For each method and data set, we ran five-fold semupervised cross-validation. In particular, the training points were chosen from four folds with increasing number of labeled points, and the test error was measured on the fifth fold. All points were used to construct the neighborhood graph. Further, for each approach parameters, e.g., using SVMs with RBF kernel, parameter values were selected by cross-validation. Performance is evaluated on error rate of the true cluster label on the test set, unless noted otherwise.

# 20.6.1 Experimental Results

The performance of the 7 methods is shown in Figures 20.1, 20.2 and 20.3. All results are reported on the test set. To avoid clutter, we show the results for the five best methods on average test-set error in Figures 20.1 and 20.2. The performance comparison of 7 semupervised learning methods on 14 datasets are in Tables 20.1–20.3 with different numbers of labeled points (30, 40, and 50). We make the following observations over the results:

+   There is no dominating method across all datasets. However, there is a set of methods that seems to be fairly consistently across the top few, and the performance of the few methods is typically close. Across the top methods, the differences typically do not be significant when compared to the worst methods, For instance, if we examine the results in Figure 20.1(e) for the USPS data set, we can see that the error for the top methods is around 1.5–1.8%, while the performance for the worst methods is 3.0% and 7.6%. The improvements of the best methods are clearly significant in comparison to the worst methods judging by the standard deviation in error.

+   Our results also indicate that no given method is always the best methods for each data set. For any method, we could find at least one data set where its performance is among the worst. It appears that the assumptions made by various approaches do not work well all cluster structures encountered in various data sets.
