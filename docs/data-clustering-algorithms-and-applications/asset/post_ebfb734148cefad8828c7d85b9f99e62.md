## 4.2 Partitional Clustering Algorithms

The first partial clustering algorithm that will be discussed in this section is the K-Means algorithm. It is one of the simplest and most efficient clustering algorithms proposed in the literature of data clustering. After the algorithm is described in detail, some of the major factors that influence the final clustering solution will be highlighted. Finally, some of the widely used variations of K-Means will also be discussed in this section.

### 4.2.1 K-Means Clustering

K-means clustering [33, 32] is the most widely used clustering algorithm. It starts by choosing K representative points as the initial centroids. Each point is then assigned to the closest centroid on a particular proximity measure. Once the clusters are formed, the centroids for each cluster are updated. The algorithm then iteratively repeats until two steps do not change or any other alternative relaxed convergence is met. K-means clustering is a greedy algorithm which is guaranteed to converge to a local minimum but minimization of its score function is known to be NP-Hard [35]. Typically, the convergence is relaxed, a weaker condition may be used. In practice, it follows that the iterative procedure must be continued until 1% of the points change their cluster memberships. A detailed proof of the mathematical convergence of K-means can be found in [45].

A wide range of proximity measures can be used within the K-means algorithm while computing the cluster score. The choice can significantly affect the centroid assignment and the quality of the final solution. The different kinds of measures which can be used here are Manhattan distance (L1 norm), Euclidean distance (L2 norm), and Cosine similarity. For the general-means, Euclidean distance metric is the most popular choice. As mentioned above, we can obtain different clusters for different values of K and proximity measures. The objective function employed by K-means is called the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) or Residual Sum of Squares (RSS). The mathematical formulation for SSE/RSS is provided below.

Given a dataset $D=\{x_1, x_2, ..., x_n\}$ consists of $n$ points, let us denote the clustering obtained after applying K-means clustering by $C=\{c_1, c_2, ..., c_n\}$. The SSE for this clustering is provided in the Equation (4.1) where $c_k$ is the centroid of cluster $C_n$. The objective is to find a clustering that minimizes the SSE score. The iterative assignment and update steps for the K-means algorithm aim to minimize the SSE score for the given set of centroids.

$$SSE(C) = \sum_{x_i \in C_k} \|x_i - c_k\|^2 \quad (4.1)$$

### 4.2.2 Minimization of Sum of Squared Errors

$K$-means clustering is essentially an optimization problem with the goal of minimizing the SSE objective function. We will mathematically prove the reason behind choosing the mean of the data points in a cluster as the prototype for a cluster in the $K$-means algorithm. Let us denote $C_k$ as the $k$-th cluster, $x_i$ is a point in $C_k$, and $c_k$ is the mean of the $k$-th cluster. We can solve for the representative of $C_j$ which minimizes the SSE by differentiating the SSE with respect to $c_j$ and setting it to zero.

$$SSE(C) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{x_i \in C_k} (c_k - x_i)^2 \quad (4.3)$$

$$\frac{\partial SSE}{\partial c_j} = \frac{\partial}{\partial c_j} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{x_i \in C_k} (c_k - x_i)^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{x_i \in C_k} \frac{\partial}{\partial c_j}(c_k - x_i)^2 = \sum_{x_i \in C_j} 2(c_j - x_i)$$

$$\sum_{x_i \in C_j} 2 * (c_j - x_i) = 0 \Rightarrow |C_j| \cdot c_j = \sum_{x_i \in C_j} x_i \Rightarrow c_j = \frac{\sum_{x_i \in C_j} x_i}{|C_j|}$$

Hence, the mean of the data points in a cluster is the prototype for a cluster in the $K$-means algorithm. The SSE decreases with each iteration and will eventually converge to a local minimum.

### 4.2.3 Affecting K-Means

The factors that can impact the performance of K-means are:

- 1. Choosing initial centroids.
- 2. Estimating the number of clusters.

We will now discuss several methods to tackle these factors.

#### 4.2.3.1 Initialization Methods

In his classical paper, MacQueen [33] suggested a simple method. Other methods that have been successfully used in literature include:

- Hartigan and Wong [19]: Using the concept of SSE, this method uses random points.
- Milligan [37]: Using the sum of squared errors, this method uses random points.
- Bradley [5]: This method uses the sum of squared errors. 
- Ward []: This method uses the sum of squared errors to minimize the variance within clusters.

#### 4.2.3.2 Estimating the Number of Clusters

The problem of estimating the correct number of clusters is one of the challenges. Several researchers have proposed new methods:

- Calinski-Harabasz Index [6]: Based on the ratio of between-cluster variance to within-cluster variance.
- Gap Statistic [48]: Compares the total variation to null reference.
- Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) [52]: Developed by considering likelihood and adding constraints for model complexity.
- Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [39]: Serves as an approximation to the Bayesian posterior.
- Duda and Hart [11]: Involves hierarchical clustering by choosing the cut-off level in the dendrogram. The methods that are typically used to cut a dendrogram are:
  - Cutting the dendrogram at a specified level of similarity.
  - Cutting the dendrogram where the gap between successive merges is the largest.
  - Stopping the process when the density of the cluster that results from the merging is below a certain threshold.
