## 10.1 Introduction

In recent years, advances in hardware have allowed us to automatically reconstruct transactions and other pieces of information of everyday life at a rapid rate. Such processes generate huge amounts of online data which grow at an unlimited rate. These kinds of online data are referred to as data clustering: Algorithms and Applications. The issues on management and analysis of data streams have been researched extensively in recent years because of their emerging, imminent, and broad applications [1].

Many important problems such as clustering and classification have been widely studied in the data community. The problem has been investigated in the context of a wide variety of methods such as the k-means, k-medians, density-based methods, and probabilistic clustering methods. A detailed discussion of clustering methods may be found in [42, 44]. Most of the classical methods in the literature are not necessarily designed in the context of large data sets and data streams. The stream scenario brings a unique set of challenges with it, which cannot be addressed by most of the classical methods proposed in [42, 44].

The specific challenges in the context of stream scenario are as follows:

- Streams have massive volume, and it is not possible to store the data explicitly on disk. Therefore, the data needs to be processed in a single pass, in which all the information required for the clustering process needs to be stored and maintained. The time needed to process each record must be small and constant. Otherwise, the model construction process would never be able to catch up with the stream.
- The patterns in the data stream may continuously evolve over time [3]. From a stream mining perspective, this implies that the underlying cluster models need to be continuously updated. A useful model must be available at any time, because the end of stream computation may never be reached, and an analysis may require results at any point in time.
- Different domains of data may pose different challenges to data clustering. For example, in a massive domain of diverse attributes, it may not be possible to store many representations of clusters without increasing the computational complexity of the problem significantly. Therefore, space-efficient methods need to be designed for massive-domain clustering of data streams.

Clearly, the issue of scalability is a primary one from the perspective of stream processing, but by no means is it the only issue. In the context of stream processing, temporal locality is also quite important, because the underlying patterns in the data may evolve, and the clusters in the past history may not remain relevant to the future. The issue of scalability also arises in the context of data clustering of very large data sets [31, 37, 51, 63]. In such cases, the major constraint is that the algorithm should require no more than one (or at least a small number of) pass(es) over the data. Since I/O operations are traditionally quite expensive in databases, compared to memory operations, the optimum performance is often achieved, when the underlying algorithm is designed to minimize the number of passes over the data, rather than minimize the number of CPU operations. In the context of data streams, temporal locality issues are also very important and should not be ignored in the clustering process. Therefore, a variety of stream clustering algorithms attempt to take such streams into account the use of snapshot-based methods, decay-based techniques, windowing, etc. We will make an effort to point out such points where they are used.

This chapter is organized as follows. In the next section, we will study clustering methods that are based on the k-means or the k-medians method. In Section 10.3, we will study density-based methods for stream clustering. Section 10.4 discusses probabilistic algorithms for clustering data streams. High dimensional streaming algorithms are introduced in Section 10.5. Methods for discrete and categorical stream clustering introduced in Section 10.6. Methods for clustering text streams are discussed in Section 10.7. Other scenarios for data stream clustering are discussed in Section 10.8. The conclusions and summary are presented in Section 10.9.

## 10.2 Methods Based on Partitioning Representatives

A number of methods for clustering are based on partitioning representatives. These include methods such as k-means and k-medians. In the classical literature, these require multiple passes over the data. However, in the stream scenario, multiple passes are not required because a sufficient amount of data is available. The set of representatives is defined by such that each point is assigned to its closest representative. In this section, we will first discuss the k-means variant.

### 10.2.1 The STREAM Algorithm

The STREAM method [36, 53] is based on micro-clustering. The core idea is to break the stream into chunks, each of which fits in main memory. For the original $\mathcal{D}$, we divide it into chunks $\mathcal{D}_1, \dots, \mathcal{D}_n$, each containing at most $m$ data points. The value of $m$ is defined by a memory budget. The goal is to pick the representatives in such a way as to minimize the Sum of Squared Error (SSE) of the data points. For a set of data points $X_1, \dots, X_m$ in $S$, and a set of $k$ representatives $Y = Y_1, \dots, Y_k$, the objective function is defined as:

$$Objective(S, Y) = \sum_{X_i \in S, Y_j \in Y} dist(X_i, Y_j) \quad (10.1)$$

The assignment is denoted by $\in$. The sum of squared distances between a point and records is defined by $dist(\cdot)$. After the first chunk has been processed, we have a set of $k$ medians, which are stored. The number of points assigned to each representative is stored as level-1 representatives. The next chunk is independently processed to find its optimal median representatives. At the end of the second chunk, we will have $2-k$ representatives. The memory requirement for storing the representatives also increases with time, and after processing chunks when the number of representatives exceeds $m$, a second level of clustering is applied to this set of $r-k$ points, so that the stored points on the representatives are used in the clustering process. The resulting representatives are stored as level-2 representatives. In general, when the number of representatives of level-$p$ reaches $m$, they are converted to $k$ level$(p+1)$ representatives. Then, the process will result in increasing the number of representatives at all levels, though the number of representatives in higher levels will grow exponentially slower than the lower levels. At the end of processing the entire stream (or when a specific need for the clustering result arises), all remaining representatives of different levels are clustered together in one final application of the $k$-medians subroutine.
