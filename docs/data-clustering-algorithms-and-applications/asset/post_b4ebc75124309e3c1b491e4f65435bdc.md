# 8.1 Introduction

In this chapter, we introduce the family of spectral clustering algorithms which have seen increasing popularity over the past few years. Starting with the seminal works in [37] and [43], a large number of papers has been published along this line of work. As opposed to “traditional clustering algorithms” as k-means and generative mixture models which always result in clusters with convex geometric shape, spectral clustering can solve problems in much more complex scenarios, such as intertwined spirals, or other nonlinear shapes, because it does not make assumptions on the shapes of clusters. ## 8.2 Similarity Graph

Let the set of data points, which we wish to partition into $k$ subsets, be denoted by $X = \{x_1, ..., x_n\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$. In order to perform spectral clustering, we first need to represent this in the form of an undirected graph $G = (V, E)$. Each data point $x_i$ is represented by a vertex. Then we can use an $n \times n$ adjacency matrix (or affinity matrix) to describe $G$, where $W = \{W_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^n$. Since spectral clustering algorithms aim at partitioning the vertices leaves in the same cluster have high similarity and in different clusters have low similarity, it becomes critical to choose an effective method to construct such a matrix.

Recent studies in graph theory [8] and manifold learning [50] have demonstrated that the adjacency matrix models the local neighborhood of the data points. This issue will be discussed in more detail in Section 8.7. Based on this rule, we introduce three ways to construct $W$:

- **1. K-nearest neighbor graph:** The idea is that $v_i$ is connected with $v_j$ when $v_j$ is among the $K$-nearest neighbors of $v_i$ or $v_i$ is among the $K$-nearest neighbors of $v_j$. The distance is computed based on the original orientation of the data points $x_i$ and $x_j$. Some examples include $\ell_1$-norm, $\ell_2$-norm, and cosine distance. The graph structure is usually the $k$-nearest neighbor graph. The algorithm is to connect $v_i$ and $v_j$ when they are mutual in the neighborhood of each other. This is referred to as the mutual $K$-nearest neighbor graph or symmetric $K$-nearest neighbor graph. In both cases, after adding the edges according to the similarity of their endpoints, we can assign weights to the edges or adopt a 0–1 weight.

- **2. ε-neighborhood graph:** In this kind of graph, vertices are connected only when the pairwise distance $\|x_i - x_j\|$ is smaller than $\varepsilon$. However, this method often leads to graphs with disconnected components if $\varepsilon$ is not carefully chosen.

- **3. Fully connected graph:** In this case, we connect all vertices with positive similarity. Since the adjacency matrix models the local neighborhood, the selection of the similarity function itself becomes tricky. An example is the heat kernel function:

```
$W_{ij} = e^{-\frac{\|x_i-x_j\|^2}{\sigma^2}}$
```
