
As mentioned, approaches to clustering in arbitrarily oriented subspaces are also known as "correlation clustering" [32, 141]. It should be noted, though, that this term is ambiguously used for a completely different problem in machine learning where a partitioning of the data correlate as much as possible with a pairwise similarity function $f$ learned from past data [22].

## 9.5 Open Questions and Current Research Directions

Some interesting research directions have found attention in the workshop series on "Discovering, Summarizing and Using Multiple Clusterings" (MultiClust) at KDD 2010 [50], at ECML/PKDD 2011 [109], and at SDM 2012 [1110]. Intriguingly, problems in subspace clustering are similar to problems in other areas such as ensemble clustering, alternative clustering, or multiv clustering [93]. Such problems common to all of these areas are (i) how to treat diversity of clustering solutions (are diverse clustering solutions to be unified or be presented?), (ii) how to effectively summarize and present density to a user of clustering algorithms, (iii) how to treat redundancy of clusters, and how to assess similarity between multiple clustering solutions. Relations to frequent pattern matching, which was godfather at the origin of subspace clustering, have been discussed from a more recent point of view on research in fields [94].

As we pointed out, redundancy of subspace cluster results is a problem inherited from the bottom-up strategy of the very first approaches, borrowed from frequent pattern matching. For current research on subspace clustering, getting rid of too much redundancy is a major topic [70, 105]. Research on multustering [28, 36, 80, 72] seems to approach the problem from the opposite direction but equally at the same issue, seeking to allow some redundancy in order to exclude possibly interesting concepts although they might partially have a certain overlap with other clusters. Related thought to tackling the problem of distinctiveness of different clusters by directly assessing a certain notion of distance between different partitions (so-called alternative clustering approaches) [60, 61, 21, 38, 19, 37, 116].

A question related to the redundancy issue concerns the appropriate density level. This is also a general problem in density-based clustering [81], but for clustering in subspaces, the problem is aggravated. A fixed density threshold for an APRI-style subspace search is not appropriate for all possible subspaces. For example consider CLIQUE-style grid approach: the volume of a hypercube increases exponentially with the dimensionality; the density decreases. As a consequence, any chosen threshold needs to identify clusters of (up to) certain dimensionality. This motivates research on adaptive density thresholds [18, 106]. When using Euclidean distance ($L_2$), the appropriate choice of e-range becomes extremely challenging as well, not only for subspace but for arbitrarily oriented clustering, due to the counter-intuitive behavior of the volume of the hypersphere with increasing dimensions. Choosing the size of the neighborhood in terms of objects or radius $k$ nearest neighbors of an e-range query has been advocated as a workout for this problem [7], to solve at least certain aspects such as having a well-defined (nonempty) set of objects for the density estimation or for the estimation of spatial characteristics of the neighborhood.

(As discussed above, for axis-parallel subspace clustering at least some first steps have been taken [101]). Also for overlapping clusters, a common problem in subspace clustering but in other domains, proper evaluation is not known. Different approaches to evaluation have varied and all neglect certain requirements. This problem is sketched and discussed in more detail by Färber et al. [49]. Only some preliminary steps toward improved evaluation methodology can be found in the literature [115, 92, 66, 9].

As a consequence, a comprehensive study comparing the merits of a broad selection of the algorithms is still missing in the literature. There are only two preliminary attempts to compare a certain selection of subspace clustering algorithms [104, 108], where the applied evaluation procedures are not entirely convincing. Some of the algorithms discussed in this chapter are available in a unified implementation in the ELKI framework.

Despite the unresolved issues in the basic techniques for real-valued feature vectors, there are already attempts to generalize subspace clustering for more complex data. So-called 3D data add a third component (e.g., time) to objects and attributes. This means instead of a matrix, a tensor of 3rd order is needed [140, 123, 83, 81]. While 3D applications have complete access to the third component (as different points in time), applications to dynamic or stream data usually do not. When to use a clustering algorithm to grasp the subspace-clustering structure (and possibly some postprocessing on a structure). There are also attempts to address subspace clustering in dynamic or stream data [10, 138, 66, 112]. Other challenges include the fact that the data is not of quality or there are uncertain [71]. When some objects are labeled, lately, some subspace clustering approaches can be used for semi-supervised subspace clustering [134, 131, 56]. Some approaches use additionally available graph information simultaneously with the clustering process [68, 67]. A recent survey discusses such problems and approaches as “enhanced subspace clustering” [122].

## 9.6 Conclusion

Research in data mining and related disciplines such as statistics, pattern recognition, machine learning, and also applied sciences (e.g., bioinformatics) has led to a large variety of clustering techniques and also addressed the specialized problem of clustering high-dimensional data. New approaches to that problem are proposed in numerous conferences and journals every year. However, many researchers argue there is no such thing as a general clustering technique suitable to all problems and universally applicable to arbitrary data. The sets of concrete data influence the choice of the algorithm and this is true for clustering, but even when some tasks involve high-dimensional data.

In this chapter, we order to guide the readers to the literature most appropriate to the task they are facing, distinguished different problem settings, axis-parallel subspace clustering with the families of “subspace clustering” (in a narrower sense), (“soft”) projected clustering,” and an increasing number of “hybrid” approaches; as well as clustering in arbitrarily oriented subspaces (also called “correlation clustering”). Among these fields, one can find different problem settings addressed by “curse of dimensionality.” The curse of dimensionality, is nothing we could resolve. There are different aspects of this “curse” and, if one of these aspects is taken into consideration by some approach, other aspects will keep haunting the researcher.

Different approaches come with a different bias. The domain expert should decide which bias—if any—is most meaningful in a given application. For example, it could be meaningful in a given application to specifically search for axis-parallel subspace clusters.
