# Clustering High-Dimensional Data

Subspace clustering aims at finding all clusters in all subspaces. As a consequence, each data point can belong to many clusters simultaneously (i.e., the clusters can substantially overlap in different subspaces). This approach usually results in redundant clusters when the retrieved clustering.

Many approaches in the literature do not clearly fit in one of these traditional categories and the tendency of opinion in research is that more suitable problem descriptions should lie somewhere between these archetypal categories, allowing for overlap of clusters, but restricting the redundancy of clusters. Eventually, a suitable clustering should not be defined by the algorithm but by the domain-specific requirements.

Note that these categories are not (or coincidentally in the early approaches) related to the categories of subspace research (top-down vs. bottom-up).

SUBCLU [84] uses the DBSCAN cluster model of density-connected sets [46]. It is shown that density-connected sets enables DBSCAN to search for density-based clusters in subspaces in APRIORI-like style. The resulting clusters may include arbitrary shape and size in the corresponding subspaces. In fact, for each subspace SUBCLU puts clusters that have been found by DBSCAN applied to that subspace. Compared to the grid-based approaches, SUBCLU achieves a better clustering quality which requires a higher runtime.

It has been observed that a bias towards a certain density threshold, as used by SUBCLU and the grid-based approaches, leads to a certain similarity (recall Aspect 5 of dimensionality). On the one hand, a tighter threshold is needed to separate clusters from noise in low-dimensional subspaces and lose clusters in higher-dimensional subspaces. On the other hand, a more relaxed threshold which is to detect high-dimensional clusters will produce an excessive amount of low-dimensional clusters. Motivation by this, the problem uncertainty is addressed by a cluster model called DISC [18] (and Sim et al. [19, 70, 100]) has been proposed. DISC is based on a density measure that is adaptive to the dimensionality. A major drawback of this approach is lack of antimonotonic properties and, thus, the search space is not possible. A "weak density" is thus defined as a remedy, providing antitotonic properties. This remedy, however, introduces a global density threshold again, which renders the intended solution of the motivating problem ineffective. As this example shows, the curse of dimensionality should always be expected to keep haunting the searcher.

The initial problem formulation for projected clustering, finding all clusters in all subspaces [14], that adopted in many papers afterwards, is rather questionable since the information gained by retrieving huge set of clusters with high redundancy is not useful. The description of objective clustering is based on a naive use of the concept of item-sets clustering [129]. What constitutes a good clustering result is defined here in close relation to the design of the algorithm, i.e., the desired result should be defined according to the expected result (as opposed to what makes sense). The tool "frequent item-mining" was first, and the problem of "finding all clusters in all subspaces" has apparently defined in order to have some new problem where the tool can be applied straightforwardly. The resulting clusters are usually highly redundant and, hence, to a major extent useless.

Therefore, subsequent methods often concentrated on possibilities of constricting the resulting set of clusters by reducing the redundancy of clusters, for to keep clusters of highest dimensionality. Related approaches aim at reporting only the most representatives of a couple of redundant subspace clustering [19, 70, 105]. A broader overview over these issues has been presented by Sim et al.
