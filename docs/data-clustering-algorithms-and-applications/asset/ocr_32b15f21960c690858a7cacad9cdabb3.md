The next step is to define what it means for a grid to be dense. Since the total density over all grids is not larger than $\mu/(\mu-1)$, it follows that the average density of each grid is no larger than $\mu/(\eta-1)$. A grid is defined as dense if its density is constant times larger than this factor. This is essentially analogous to the concept of a $o$-microcluster, and a $p$-microcluster, which the work in [25] divides the grid-cells into sparse cells and transitional grid cells, with the use of a smaller threshold on the density. Grid cells can change between the different states of being sparse, transitional, or dense, both because of the addition of new data points and also because of decay.

One observation is that the density of grid-cells $\eta = \prod_{i=1}^{d} p_i$ is exponentially dependent upon the dimensionality $d$. However, most of these grid-cells are empty, and the information for empty grids is not sufficient in many applications, where many points appear sporadically in the data. The work in [25] designs for identifying and removing such sporadic grids from the data maintained dense grids can then be consolidated into denser regions in the data. As the case of [30], this is defined in the form of dense-connected grids, where adjacency of two grids is defined as a density-connection. The precise details of the dense region construction, we refer to [25]. Thus, it is evident that grid-based and microcluster-based clusters share a number of content similarities at various stages of the algorithm.

One weakness of the approach is that a significant number of grid cells needs to be discarded in order to keep the memory requirements. In many cases, such grid-cells occur at the borders of the clusters. The discarding of such cells may lead to degradation in cluster quality. Therefore, a method has been proposed in [43] to design a variation of the D-Stream method (known as $DD-Stream$), which includes data points at the borders in adjacent denser grids, which are retained by the algorithm. It has been shown that such an approach leads to some improvements in cluster quality.

# 10.3.2.2 Other Grid-Based Algorithms

The method in [35] updates a full-dimensional grid of the incoming data stream. The clusters are discovered from the updated density values in it. At any time, the density values of the grid can be used in order to determine the most effective clusters.

An important point to keep in mind is that grid-based algorithms require the discretization of the data along the different dimensions in order to create the grid cells. The granularity of the discretization is a critical design choice at the very beginning of the algorithm. Unfortunately, at the beginning of the stream we know little about how the underlying data points are distributed, and it is difficult to choose the level of discretization along each dimension in an optimal way. Furthermore, the appropriate level of discretization for each locality may be different, and a single global level of discretization may be suboptimal over different localities.

The work in [56] uses a dynamic approach called Statgrid for the stream process, where cells are recursively partitioned based on their local density. The algorithm starts with cells of equal size. As data points are added the number of points in a cell becomes sufficiently large, the algorithm partitions the cell into two along of the dimensions. This process of partitioning is executed recursively any time of the child cells become dense. Then, the maximum level of granularity is reached, and such a cell is called a unit cell, which cannot be further divided. We note that this approach naturally leads to a hierarchical clustering of the data, which can be very useful in many applications. The work does not use temporal decay and, therefore, does not adjust very well to an evolving stream.
