
## 15.4.1.3 EDIT Distance

EDIT distance (ED) comes from the field of string comparison and measures the number of insert, delete, and replace operations that are needed to make two strings of possibly lengths identical to each other. Specifically, the EDIT distance between two strings $S_1$ and $S_2$ of length $m$ and $n$, respectively, is computed as follows:

- (a) $D_{ED}(S_1, S_2) = m$, if $n = 0$
- (b) $D_{ED}(S_1, S_2) = n$, if $m = 0$
- (c) $D_{ED}(S_1, S_2) = D_{ED}(Rest(S_1), Rest(S_2))$, if $S_1 = S_2$
- (d) $D_{ED}(S_1, S_2) = \min \begin{cases} D_{ED}(Rest(S_1), S_2) + 1 \\ D_{ED}(S_1, Rest(S_2)) + 1 \end{cases}, \text{ otherwise}$

Although ED for strings is a metric distance, the two ED-related distance measures DTW and Longest Common Sequence (LCSS) which will be described in the next section are not to follow the triangle inequality. Lei Chen [11] proposed two extensions to EDIT distance, namely Edit Distance with Real Penalty (ERP) to support local time shifting and Edit Distance on Real (EDR) to handle both local time shifting and noise in time series and trajectories. Both extensions have a high computational cost, so Chen proposes lower bounds, and indexing techniques to retrieve similar series more effectively. Both ERP and DTW can handle local time shifting and measure the distance between out-of-phase time series effectively. An advantage of ERP over DTW is that the former is a metric distance function, whereas the latter is not. DTW does not obey triangle inequality, and therefore, traditional access methods cannot be used to improve efficiency in DTW-applications.

## 15.4.1.4 Longest Common Sequence

The Longest Common Sequence (LCSS) is a variation of EDIT distance described in 15.4.1 [52]. LCSS allows time to stretch the time axis and does not match all elements, being less sensitive to outliers than $L_p$-norm and DTW. Specifically, the LCSS distance defined between two real-valued sequences $S_1$ and $S_2$ of length $m$ and $n$, respectively, is computed as follows:

- (a) $D_{LCSS,\epsilon}(S_1, S_2) = 0$, if $n = 0$ or $m = 0$
- (c) $D_{LCSS,\epsilon}(S_1, S_2) = 1 + D_{LCSS,\epsilon}(HEAD(S_1), HEAD(S_2))$ if $|S_1 - S_2| < \epsilon$ and $|n - m| < \delta$
- (d) $\max \begin{cases} D_{LCSS,\epsilon}(HEAD(S_1), S_2) \\ D_{LCSS,\epsilon}(S_1, HEAD(S_2)) \end{cases}, \text{ otherwise}$

where $HEAD(S_1)$ is the subsequence $[S_{1,2}, \dots, S_{1,m-1}]$, $\delta$ is an integer that controls the maximum distance in the time axis between two matched elements, and $\epsilon$ is a real number $0 < \epsilon < 1$ that controls the maximum distance that two elements are allowed to be considered matched, as depicted in Figure 15.2.

## 15.4.2 Multivariate Distance Measures

A metric distance function, ERP, is proposed to support local time shifting in time series and trajectory data. A second distance function, EDR, is proposed to measure the similarity between time series or trajectories with local time shifting and noise [10].

### 15.4.2.1 Multidimensional L$_p$ Distance

The $L_p$-norm between $d$-dimensional time sequences $T_1$ and $T_2$ of length $n$ extends the $L_p$-norm for the one-dimensional case and is defined as

$L_p\text{-}norm(T_1, T_2) = D_{M,p}(T_1, T_2) = \left( \sum_{i=1}^n (T_{1i} - T_{2i})^p \right)^{1/p} = \left( \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^d (T_{1ij} - T_{2ij})^p \right)^{1/p} \quad (15.3)$

In the one-dimensional case, multidimensional $L_p$-norm has been proven to be very sensitive to noise and to time shifting [331]. A variety of methods have been described in this section, either use the Euclidean distance or expand it, in order to define new distance measures. Let us use the Euclidean distance to compare multidimensional time series [19]. They define the distance between two multivariate sequences of equal length as the Euclidean distance among all corresponding points in the sequence. For the case where the compared sequences are not of equal length, they stretch the shorter one over the longer one, and the overall distance is defined as the minimum of all mean distances, computed described above. Lin and Su use the Euclidean distance in order to define the distance from a point $p$ to a trajectory $T$ as follows: $D_{point}(p,T)=\min_{q}ED(p,q)$ where $ED(p,q)$ represents the Euclidean distance between points $p$ and $q$. $D_{point}$ is used to define the one-way distance (OWD) from one trajectory $T_1$ to another trajectory $T_2$ as the integral of the distance from $T_1$ to $T_2$, given by the integral. OWD is not symmetric, so the distance between trajectories $T_1$ and $T_2$ is the average of their one-way distances: $D(T_1, T_2) = \frac{1}{2}(OWD(T_1, T_2) + OWD(T_2, T_1))$. ## Time-Series Clustering

The goal of time-series clustering is to group a set of time series into several clusters such that time series within the same cluster are similar to each other and those in different clusters are dissimilar. There are many algorithms for time-series clustering, which can be categorized into two main types: distance-based and model-based. Distance-based algorithms use a distance measure to compute the similarity between time series and then use a clustering algorithm like k-means to group them. Model-based algorithms first fit a model to each time series and then group them based on the similarity of the model parameters.

The performance of time-series clustering depends heavily on the choice of distance measure and clustering algorithm. For distance-based algorithms, it is important to choose a distance measure that is robust to noise and time shifting. For model-based algorithms, it is important to choose a model that can capture the underlying dynamics of the time series.

## 15.4.2 Multidimensional DTW

DTW (Dynamic Time Warping) over $d$-dimensional time series $T_1$ and $T_2$ of length $m$ and $n$, respectively, is defined as the one-dimensional case as described in 15.4.1.2. Just like in the one-dimensional case, multidimensional DTW allows stretching in time, matches all elements, and is extensively used in the speech recognition domain. DTW, unlike Euclidean distance, does not require the two sequences under comparison to be the same length and is not sensitive to time shifting. DTW is not a metric, it doesn't follow triangle inequality and its complexity is $O(mn)$, which means it is computationally expensive for long time series and is useful only for short sequences, comprising a few thousand points. Salvadore and Chan propose FastDTW, an approximation of DTW in linear time. The standard DTW runs over the lower resolution and the produced warp path is passed over the next higher resolution. Finally the process is repeated until the error is reduced up to 19.2%, is because the number of cells it evaluates scales linearly with the length of the time series. Through experimental evaluation, the authors prove the accuracy and efficiency improvements over Sakoe-Chiba bands and data abstraction, which are two other DTW approximations [46].
