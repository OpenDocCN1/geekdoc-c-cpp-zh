# 9.4.2.2 Basic Techniques and Example Algorithms

Basic techniques to find oriented subspaces accommodating clusters are principle component analysis (PCA) and the Hough-transform. The first approach to this generalized clustering algorithm ORCLUS [13], using ideas similar to the axis-parallel approach PROCLUS [12]. ORCLUS is a $k_$-means-like approach, picking $k_c > k$ as it starts, assigning the data objects to these seeds according to a distance function that is based on an eigenystem of the corresponding cluster subspace (i.e., the distance in the projected subspace where the cluster objects exhibit high density). The eigenystem is iteratively adapted to the current state of the updated cluster. The number $k_c$ of clusters is reduced iteratively by merging pairs until the user-specified $k$ is reached. The closest pair of clusters is the pair with the least average distance in the projected space (span by the weak eigenvectors) of the merged clusters. Starting with a larger value for the parameter $k_c$ increases the effectiveness, but also the runtime.

In contrast to ORCLUS, the algorithm [32] is based on a density-based clustering paradigm [48]. Thus, the number of clusters is not decided before clusters grow from a seed as the density criterion is fulfilled. Otherwise, another seed is picked to start a new cluster. The density criterion is a required minimal number points within the neighborhood of a point, where the neighborhood is based on distance metrics computed from the eigenystems of the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of $p$ in Euclidean space. A parameter $\delta$ discards the range of small eigenvalues in the eigenvalue matrix $E_p$; then large eigenvalues are replaced by 1 and small values by a $\lambda \gg 1$. The adapted eigenvalue matrix $E'_p$, a correlation similarity matrix for $p$ is obtained by $V_p, E'_p, V'_p$. This matrix is then used to derive the distance of two points, $q$ and $p$, w.r.t. $p$, as the general quadratic form:

$$\sqrt{(p-q)^T \cdot E'_p \cdot (p-q)}$ \quad (9.7)$

By measuring this symmetrically to $q$ and choosing the maximum of distances helps to decide whether points are connected by a similar correlation of attributes and, thus, are similar and to each other. Regarding the choice of parameters, 4C suffers from similar problems as PreDeCon, from the hard to guess neighborhood size that it is used in the Euclidean full-dimensional neighborhood and the adapted subspace neighborhood (recall Aspect 5 of the curse of dimensionality).

As a hierarchical approach, HICO [8] defines the distance between points according to their local correlation dimensionality and subspace orientation and use hierarchical-density-based clustering [16] to derive a hierarchy of clusters.

COPAC [7] is based on ideas similar to 4c but suffers of some problems as meaningless similarity matrices due to $e$-neighborhoods. Instead of the problematic $\varepsilon$-range queries, COPAC is using a fixed number $k$ for the computation of local data characteristics—which raises the question of how to choose a good value for $k$. However, at choosing $k \gg \lambda$ ensures a meaningful definition of a $\lambda$-dimensional hyperplane. Essentially, choosing $k$ large enough but not larger than the minimum cluster size is a reasonable guideline.
