## 1.4.3 Multiew and Ensemble-Based Insights

As discussed above, one of the major issues in the clustering process is that different kinds of clusters are possible. When no supervision is available, the bewildering number of possibilities in the clustering process can sometimes be problematic for the analyst, especially from the perspective of interpretability. These are referred to as alternative clusters, technically represent the behavior from different perspectives. In many cases, the ability to provide different clustering solutions that are significantly different provides insights to the analyst about the key clustering properties of the underlying data. This area is also referred to as multiew clustering.

The most naive method for multiple clustering is to simply run the clustering algorithm multiple times, and then examine the different clustering solutions to determine which are different. Somewhat different approach is to use spectral methods in order to create approximately orthogonal cuts. Recall that the eigenvectors of the Laplacian matrix represent alternative cuts in the graph and that the smallest represent the best. Thus, by using a 2-means algorithm to embed each encoder, it is possible to create a clustering which is different from the clustering created by other (orthogonal) eigenvectors. The orthogonality of the eigenvectors is important, because it implies that the embedded representations are very different. Furthermore, the smallest eigenvectors represent the best clusters, whereas clusters differing from successively larger eigenvectors represent successively suboptimal solutions. Thus, this approach not only provides alternative clusterings that are quite different from one another, but also provides a ranking of the quality of the different solutions. A discussion of alternative methods is provided in Chapter 21.

In many cases, the alternative clustering methods can be combined to create more robust solutions using the use of ensemble-based techniques. The idea is that a combination of the output of the different clusters provides a more robust picture of how the properties are related to one another. Whether, the outputs of the different alternatives can be used as input to a meta-algorithm which combines the results from the different algorithms. Such an approach provides a more robust clustering solution. A discussion of ensemble-based methods for clustering is provided in Chapter 22.

## 1.4.4 Validation-Based Insights

Given a particular clustering, how do we know what the quality of the clustering is really? While one possible approach is to use synthetic data to determine the matching between the input and output clusters, it is not fully satisfying to rely only on synthetic data. The result is that synthetic data may be specific to a particular algorithm and may not be easily generalizable to arbitrary data.

Therefore, it is desirable to use validation criteria on the basis of real data sets. In the context of the clustering problem is that the criteria for quality is not as crisp as many other data problems such as classification, where external validation criteria are available in the form of labels. Therefore, the use of one or more criteria may inadvertently favor different algorithms. As the following discussion suggests, clustering is a process in which quantification is often not possible because of its unsupervised nature. Nevertheless, many techniques provide a partial understanding of the underlying layers. Some common techniques for cluster validation are as follows:

- A common method in the literature is to use case studies to illustrate the subjective quality of the clusters. While case studies provide intuitive insights, they are not particularly effective for providing a more rigorous quantification of the quality. It is often difficult to compare two clustering methods from a quantitative perspective with the use of such an approach.

- Specific measurements of the clusters such as the cluster radius or density may be used in order to provide a measure of quality. The problem here is that these measures may favor different algorithms in a different way. For, a k-means approach will typically be superior to a density-based clustering method in terms of average cluster radius, a density-based method may be superior to a k-means algorithm in terms of the estimated density of the clusters. This is because there is a circularity in a particular criterion to evaluate the algorithm, when the same criterion is used for clustering purposes. This results in a bias during the evaluation. On the other hand, it may be possible to reasonably compare two algorithms of a very similar type (e.g., two variations of k-means) on the basis of a particular cluster.

- In many data sets, labels are associated with the data points. In such cases, cluster quality can be measured in terms of the correlations of the clusters with the data labels. This provides an external validation criterion, if the labels have not been used in the clustering process. However, such an approach is not perfect, because class labels may not always align with the natural clusters in the data. Nevertheless, the approach is still considered more "impartial" than the other two methods discussed above and is commonly used for cluster evaluation.

A detailed discussion of the validation methods commonly used in clustering algorithms is provided in Chapter 23.

## 1.5 Discussion and Conclusions

Clustering is one of the most fundamental data mining problems because of its numerous applications to customer segmentation, target marketing, and data summarization. Numerous classes of methods have been proposed in the literature, such as probabilistic methods, distance-based methods, grid-based methods, and spectral methods. The problem of clustering has close relationships to dimensionality reduction, especially through projected clustering. High-dimensional data is often challenging, because of the increasing number of data. Streaming methods typically work with only one pass over the data. Many variations and enhancements of clustering can be used to improve the quality obtained from the clustering process. The diversity of data types adds to the richness of the clustering problems.
