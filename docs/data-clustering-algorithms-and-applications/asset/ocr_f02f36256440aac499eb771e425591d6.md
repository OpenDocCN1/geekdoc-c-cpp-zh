# Algorithm 25 BANG-Clustering Algorithm

+   1: Partition the feature space into rectangular blocks which contain up to a maximum of $p_{max}$ data points.

+   2: Build a binary tree to maintain the populated blocks, in which the partition level corresponds to the node depth in the tree.

+   3: Calculate the dendrogram in which the density of all blocks are calculated and sorted in decreasing order.

+   4: Starting with the highest density index, all neighbor blocks are determined and classified in decreasing order. BANG-clustering places the regions in the dendrogram to the right of the original blocks.

+   5: Repeat step 4 for the remaining blocks of the dendrogram.

A parent cell is the union of its children; the root cell at level 1 corresponds to the whole spatial area. The level cells are of uniform size, determined globally from the average density of objects. For each cell, hold attribute-dependent and attribute-independent parameters of the statistical information are maintained. These are defined in Table 6.3.

STING maintains summary statistics for each cell in its hierarchical tree. As a result, parameters of parent cells can easily be computed from the parameters of child cells. The distribution types may be normal, uniform, exponential, and none. Value of density may be either assigned by the user or obtained by hypothesis tests such as the $\chi^2$ test. Even though measures of these statistical parameters are top-down in a bottom-up approach from any leaf node, the STING adopts a top-down approach for clustering and querying from the root of its hierarchical tree structure. The algorithm is summarized in Algorithm 26 [10, 28].

The tree is constructed in $O(N)$, where $N$ is the total number of data points. Cells are identified and clustered by examining the density of these cells in a similar vein to the density-based DBSCAN algorithm [7]. If the cell has $K$ cells, the complexity of spatial querying and clustering for STING is $O(K)$. A common misconception is that $K$ is $O(2^d)$ where $d$ is the number of dimensions and that this would be problematic in high dimensional data. STING may have problems with higher dimensional data than all grid-based algorithms (e.g., handling noise and selecting most relevant attributes) [111], but scalability of the STING structure is not one of them.

There are several advantages of STING. First, it is a query-independent approach where the statistics are independent of queries. The computational complexity of STING is $O(K)$, and this is efficient in clustering large data sets when $K \ll N$. The algorithm is readily parallelizable and allows for multiple resolutions for examining the data in its hierarchical grid structure. In incremental data updating is supported, there is lower overhead for incorporating new data.
