Integrating the concepts of dense subgraphs and subspace clusters, Moser et al. [28] introduced the problem of finding cohesive patterns. A cohesive pattern is defined as a subgraph

+   1. connected,

+   2. has a density exceeding the threshold of α, and

+   3. has homogeneous values in a least d features, for example, the values of these features may have at most a specified variance within the vertices of the pattern.

Different from graph partitioning methods and similar to frequent pattern mining methods, cohesive patterns can overlap and not cover the entire dataset. Furthermore, the number of patterns does not need to be specified in advance. Integrating constraints on the feature reduces the number of patterns substantially and adds meaning to the identified patterns. In order to reduce the potentially large number of cohesive patterns, the search is restricted to finding only maximal cohesive patterns, i.e., those whose supergraphs do not form cohesive patterns.

The problem of discovering cohesive patterns is NP-hard, but it becomes tractable in many practical cases by exploiting the monotonicity properties. A constraint is called if the satisfaction of the constraint by a pattern of size k implies the satisfaction of the constraint by all subpatterns of size k-1, and a constraint is called anti-monotone if the density and connectivity constraint are anti-monotone. Finally, the simultaneous satisfaction of all three constraints is anti-monotone for α ≥ 0.5. The CoPAM algorithm enumerates subgraphs in a levelwise, bottom-up fashion, starting with subgraphs of size 2. At level k, subgraphs of size k that do not satisfy all of the three constraints are pruned, while the remaining entities are extended by one neighboring vertex to form candidates for the next level k+1. The algorithm terminates at a level where no cohesive patterns are discovered.
