# 1.3.4 Clustering Time-Series Data

Time-series data is common in all forms of sensor data, stock markets, or any other kinds of temporal tracking or forecasting applications. The major aspect of time series is that the data values are not independent of one another, but are temporally dependent on one another. Specifically, the data contain a contextual attribute (time) and a behavioral attribute (data). There is a significant diversity in problem definitions in the time-series scenario. The time-series data can be clustered in a variety of different ways, depending upon whether correlation-based analysis is required or shape-based offline analysis is required

+   - Correlation-based online analysis, the correlations among the different time-series data streams are tracked over time to create online clusters. These methods are often used for sensor selection and forecasting, especially when sensors exhibit lag correlations within the clustered patterns. These methods are often used in stock market applications, where it is desirable to maintain groups of clustered stock tickers, in one manner, based on their correlation patterns. Thus, the distance between different series need to be computed continuously, based on their mutual regression coefficients. Examples of these methods include the MUSCLES approach [86] and a large scale time-series correlation monitoring method [90]

+   - In-shape offline analysis, the time-series objects are analyzed in offline manner, and the specific details about when a particular series was created is not important. For example, for a set of ECG time collected from patients, the precise time when a series was collected is not important, but the overall shape of the series is important for the purposes of clustering. In such cases, the distance function between two series is important. This is important, because the different series may not be drawn on the same range of data values and may also show time-warping effects, which the shapes can be matched only by elongating or shrinking portions of the time-series in the temporal direction. As the previous case, the design of the distance function [42] holds the key to the effective use of the approach.

A interesting case is that multivariate time series, in which many series are simultaneously produced over time. A classical example of this is trajectory data, in which the different coordinates represent the different components of the multivariate series. Therefore, trajectory analysis can be viewed as a special kind of time-series clustering. In the case of univariate time series, it is possible to perform these using either online analysis (trajectories moving in real time) or offline analysis (similar shape). An example of the former is discussed in [59], whereas an example of the latter is discussed in [68]. A survey of time-series data is found in [60], though this survey is largely focussed on the case of offline analysis. Chapter 15 discusses both the online and offline analysis of time series clustering.

# 1.3.5 Clustering Discrete Sequences

Many forms of data create discrete sequences instead of categorical ones. For example, web logs, the command sequences in computer systems, and biological data are all discrete. The contextual attribute in this case often corresponds to placement (e.g., biological data), rather than time. Biological data is also one of the most common applications of sequence clustering.
