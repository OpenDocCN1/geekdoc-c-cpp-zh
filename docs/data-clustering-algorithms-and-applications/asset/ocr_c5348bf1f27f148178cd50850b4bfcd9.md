+   **Divisive:** In these methods, a top-down approach is used to successively partition the data points into a tree-like structure. Flat clustering can be used in order to perform the partitioning at each stage. Divisive partitioning allows greater flexibility in both of the hierarchical structure of the tree and the level of balance in the different clusters. It is not necessary to have a perfectly balanced tree in terms of the depths of the nodes or in the degree of which every branch is exactly. This allows the construction of a tree structure which allows tradeoffs in the balancing of the nodes and node weights (number of data points in the node). For example, in a top-down method, if the branches of the tree are unbalanced in terms of node weights, then the largest cluster can be chosen preferentially for density at each level. Such an approach [53] is used in METIS in order to create well balanced clusters in large social networks, in which the problem of cluster imbalance is particularly severe. *METIS* is not a distance-based algorithm, these general principles apply to distance-based algorithms as well.

Distance-based methods are very popular in the literature, because they can be used with almost any data type, as long as an appropriate distance function is created for that data type. Thus, the problem of clustering can be reduced to the problem of finding a distance function for that data. Therefore, distance function itself has become an important area of research for data mining in its own right [5, 82]. Dedicated methods have also been designed for specific data domains such as categorical or time series data [32, 42]. Of course, many domains, such as highimensional data, the quality of detection reduces because of irrelevant dimensions [43] and may show errors and concentration effects, which reduce the statistical significance of data results. In such cases, one may use the redundancy in larger portions of the pairwise distance matrix to account out the noise in the data with spectral methods [19] and projections in order to directly find the clusters in relevant subsets of attributes [9]. # 1.2.4 Density and Grid-Based Methods

Density and grid-based methods are two closely related classes, which there to explore the data space at high levels of granularity. At any particular point in the data space is defined either in terms of the number of data points in a probability density of its locality or in terms of a smoother kernel density estimate [74]. Typically, the data space is explored at a reasonably high level of granularity and a processing phase is used to “put together” the dense regions of the data space into an arbitrary shape. Grid-based methods are a specific class of density-based methods where individual regions of the data space are explored and formed into a grid-like structure. Grid-like structures are often convenient because of putting the different dense blocks in the post-processing phase. Such grid-like methods can also be used in the context of high-dimensional methods, since the lower dimensional grids define clusters on subsets of dimensions [6].

A major advantage of these methods is that they explore the data space at high level of granularity, they can be used to reconstruct the entire shape of the data distribution. Two classical methods for density-based methods and grid-based methods are DBSCAN [34] and STING [83], respectively, the major challenge of density-based methods is that they are naturally defined on data points in a non-Euclidean space, unless an embedding approach is used. Thus, many arbitrary data types as time-series data are not quite easy to use with density-based methods without specialized transformations.
