# 17.13.2 Dynamic Graphs

So far we have discussed algorithms that assume that the underlying network is stable and static. In many real networks this assumption is not made because the networks are changing continuously. For instance, social networks keep changing, and hence, the assumption of a static structure is not very practical. As a result, several questions arise such as: How do the communities evolve over time? How are communities formed? How should existing algorithms be modified to accommodate these dynamic networks? How persistent are the communities? What is the temporal pattern in the network? Some of the recent work has started looking at these problems. We give an overview of some of the representative works that have looked into these emerging questions.

An event-based approach that provides a way to reason about how communities and individuals in these networks evolve over time and what characterizes their behavior is proposed by As et al. [10] where events involving communities are dissolve, merge, split, continue, k-merge, k-split, and events involving individuals join, appear, disappear, etc. The authors demonstrate that their framework can effectively detect behavioral indices such as stability, influence, and a diffusion model and that to analyze real-life evolving networks “incrementally.” This model can predict future behaviors such as collaboration between groups as well identify influential nodes. One important capability of this model is that semantic content can be integrated into it naturally.

A simple approach to dealing with dynamic networks is to treat each snapshot of the network independently and apply conventional clustering algorithms on. This might result in unwanted fluctuations in community structures between snapshots. One extreme example is given in [29] where an “optimal” clustering would cause the clusters to change radically and a consistent feature would provide consistent clustering while prioritizing approximately to optimal answer. This problem was solved by constructing temporal slices of the network and discovering community in individual slices to detect temporal change in community structure between slices. Berger-Wolf and Sia [16] took partitions of nodes at each timestamp as input to produce a metsequence of sequences. They defined three extreme metagroups as most persistent, most stable, and largest and discussed algorithms for extracting them. Given the affiliation of each individual in each timescale, Tipiathannaraman et al. [164] tried to identify “true” community among them in a dynamic setting by formulating it as a combinatorial optimization problem and proved it to be NP-hard. They solve the problem using approximate heuristics and dynamic programming [131].

Another interesting approach to dynamic analysis is referred to evolutionary clustering as it takes a holistic view of the community discovery across-time-slices by connecting in a time-slice from diverging too many previous slices. The critical issue is, as opposed to first extracting communities on snapshots and history as a whole [131]. It allows the study of community structure and its evolution at the same time as well as the compromise between these two linear combination of snapshot quality and history cost. Different clustering techniques have also been adapted for the framework.

Use of the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle was proposed by Sun et al. [160] as an alternative approach to clustering dynamic graphs. Graphs of consecutive timestamps are grouped into graph streams by change-point that represent drastic change in network structure.
