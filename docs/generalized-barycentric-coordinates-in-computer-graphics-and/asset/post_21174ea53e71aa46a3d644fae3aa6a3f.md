### 1.2.10 Local coordinates

Zhang et al. [433] propose to minimize the sum of total variations of the coordinate functions \(\phi_i\),

\(\min_{\Phi} \sum_{i=1}^n \int_P \|\nabla \phi_i\|,\)

subject to the constraints given by (1.8), (1.9), (1.10a), (1.10b), and (1.10e), where the total variation of \(\phi_i\) is the \(L_1\)-norm of \(\|\nabla \phi_i\|\) in \(P\). The solution of this convex minimization problem gives the so-called _local coordinates_, which are at least \(C^0\). The name stems from the fact that the numerically computed function values are very close to zero in a large part of \(P\). The exact support of these coordinates, however, is not known.

### 1.2.11 Affine coordinates

Among all generalized barycentric coordinates with respect to a scattered set of points \(\Pi\), Waldron [408] suggests considering those with minimal \(L_2\)-norm, which are uniquely defined as the _affine_ functions

\(\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x}) = (\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{c}) \cdot ((VV^*)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{v}_i - \boldsymbol{c}))^T + \frac{1}{n}, \quad i = 1, \dots, n,\)

where \(\boldsymbol{c} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \boldsymbol{v}_i\) is the barycenter of \(\Pi\), \(V = (\boldsymbol{v}_1 - \boldsymbol{c}, \dots, \boldsymbol{v}_n - \boldsymbol{c})\) is a \(2 \times n\) matrix, and \(V^*\) is the adjoint of \(V\). These coordinates are non-negative inside a convex region that contains \(\boldsymbol{c}\), and they are equal to \(\frac{1}{n}\) at \(\boldsymbol{c}\). They are smooth and well-defined everywhere in the plane, but, in general, the Lagrange property (1.10b) holds only in the case \(n=3\) (see Section 1.1.1).

### 1.2.12 Sibson coordinates

Let \(V\) be the Voronoi diagram [404] of the set \(\Pi\) and \(C_i\) be the Voronoi cell in \(V\) that contains \(\boldsymbol{v}_i\). Further let \(V_{\boldsymbol{x}}\) be the Voronoi diagram of \(\Pi \cup \{\boldsymbol{x}\}\) and \(C_{\boldsymbol{x}}\) be the Voronoi cell in \(V_{\boldsymbol{x}}\) that contains \(\boldsymbol{x}\) (see Figure 1.4). Intersecting the cells \(C_i\) with \(C_{\boldsymbol{x}}\), we can define the weight functions

\(w_i = \text{Area}[C_i \cap C_{\boldsymbol{x}}], \quad i = 1, \dots, n.\)

These areas were originally proposed by Sibson [360, 361] with the emphasis on natural neighbor interpolation, where the natural neighbors of some \(\boldsymbol{x}\) are all points from \(\Pi\) for which \(w_i(\boldsymbol{x}) \neq 0\).

Normalizing the weights in (1.18) as in (1.12) defines the _Sibson coordinates_. These coordinates are well-defined over the convex hull of \(\Pi\), have local support, and satisfy the Lagrange property (1.10b). They are $C^1$, except at $v_i$, where they are only $C^0$. A simple computational algorithm for Sibson coordinates can be found in [371] and further details in [376].

### 1.2.13 Laplace coordinates

The concept of natural neighbors (see Section 1.2.12) is also used for the definition of so-called *Laplace coordinates* [34, 103, 370], which are given by the weight functions

$$
w_i = \begin{cases} \frac{s_i}{r_i}, & \text{if } i \in I_x, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \quad i = 1, \dots, n,
$$

where $s_i$ is the length of the edge of $C_x$ that is contained in $C_i$, $r_i = \|v_i - x\|$, and $I_x \subset \{1, \dots, n\}$ is the subset of indices of the natural neighbors of $x$ (see Figure 1.4).

These coordinates have the same properties as Sibson coordinates, but they are only $C^0$ along the boundary of their support. Interestingly, for polygons, whose vertices all lie on a circle, Sibson, Laplace, Wachspress, and discrete harmonic coordinates are all identical [375]. A simple computational algorithm for Laplace coordinates can be found in [371] and further details in [377].

### 1.2.14 Hermite coordinates

To interpolate not only boundary data, but also derivative information along the boundary, we complement the functions $\phi_i$ with a second set of $n$ functions $\psi_i$ and substitute the Lagrange property (1.10b) with the Hermite property

$$
\begin{aligned} 
\phi_i(v_j) &= \delta_{ij}, & \psi_i(x) &= 0 \quad \forall x \in \partial P, \\ 
\frac{\partial \phi_i}{\partial \nu}(x) &= 0 \quad \forall x \in \partial P, & \frac{\partial \psi_i}{\partial \nu}(x) &= \delta_{ij} \quad \forall x \in [v_j, v_{j+1}], 
\end{aligned} \tag{1.19}
$$

where \(\nu\) is the unit vector normal to the boundary \(\partial P\). In this setting, the key properties of generalized barycentric coordinates in Definition 1.1 take on the form

\(\sum_{i=1}^n \phi_i(\boldsymbol{x}) + \sum_{i=1}^n \psi_i(\boldsymbol{x}) = 1 \quad \forall \boldsymbol{x} \in \bar{P}, \quad (1.20)\)
\(\sum_{i=1}^n \phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{v}_i + \sum_{i=1}^n \psi_i(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\nu}_i = \boldsymbol{x} \quad \forall \boldsymbol{x} \in \bar{P}, \quad (1.21)\)

where \(\nu_i\) is the unit normal of the edge \([\boldsymbol{v}_i, \boldsymbol{v}_{i+1}]\). Note that (1.20) is essentially the same as (1.8), because \(\psi_i(\boldsymbol{x}) = 0\).

To the best of our knowledge, Lipman et al. [252] were the first to derive these coordinates \(\phi_i\) and \(\psi_i\) that satisfy (1.20) and (1.21) from Green's third identity. These *Green coordinates* have a closed form, but do not satisfy (1.19). By fitting a bivariate polynomial in a weighted least squares sense to values and derivatives given along the polygon's boundary, Manson and Schaefer [267] construct *moving least squares coordinates*, which are defined for arbitrary, even disconnected polygons and have a closed form. Another closed-form solution for Hermite coordinates with property (1.19) for arbitrary simple polygons is presented by Li et al. [248], who derive *cubic mean value coordinates* from the mean value property of biharmonic functions. Weber et al. [418] extend harmonic coordinates to *biharmonic coordinates*, which satisfy (1.19), but do not possess a closed form.

### 1.2.15 Complex coordinates

If we interpret \(P\) as a subset of \(\mathbb{C}\) and view its vertices \(\boldsymbol{v}_i = (x_i, y_i)\) as complex numbers \(z_i = x_i + iy_i\), then we can reformulate generalized barycentric coordinates as complex functions [415, 416]. These *complex coordinates* are very useful in the context of planar shape deformation (see Chapter 7 for more details). The complex setting also reveals that Green coordinates (see Section 1.2.14) can alternatively be derived from Cauchy's integral formula [415].
