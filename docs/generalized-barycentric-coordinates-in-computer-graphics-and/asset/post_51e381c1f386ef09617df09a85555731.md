## 4.3 BIJECTIVE COMPOSITE BARYCENTRIC MAPPING

Section 4.2.1 suggests that if source and target polygon are sufficiently close, the mapping is close to the identity and hence bijective. Therefore, by “splitting” the barycentric mapping from source to target polygon into a finite number of intermediate steps, where each step perturbs the vertices only slightly, it should be possible to obtain a bijective composite mapping.

To this end, suppose that $\psi_i: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^2, i = 1, \dots, n$ are a set of continuous vertex paths between each source vector $v_i^0 = \psi_i(0)$ and its corresponding target vector $v_i^1 = \psi_i(1)$, as shown in Figure 4.6. Let $\tau = (t_0, t_1, \dots, t_m)$ with $t_0 = 0, t_m = 1$, let $t_k < t_{k+1}$ for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$ be a partition of $[0, 1]$ and let $f_k$ be the barycentric mapping from $\Omega^{t_k}$ to $\Omega^{t_{k+1}}$, based on the barycentric coordinates $\phi_i^{t_k}$.

The mapping
$$
\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau} = \boldsymbol{f}_{m-1} \circ \boldsymbol{f}_{m-2} \circ \cdots \circ \boldsymbol{f}_{0}
$$
is called a *composite barycentric mapping* from \(\tilde{\Omega}^0\) to \(\tilde{\Omega}^1\) [350]. An example of a composite barycentric mapping between a square and a concave quadrilateral is shown in Figure 4.7.

Denoting the maximum displacement distance between \(\tilde{\Omega}^{t_k}\) and \(\tilde{\Omega}^{t_{k+1}}\) by
$$
d_k = \max_{1 \le i \le n} \left\| \boldsymbol{v}_i^{t_k} - \boldsymbol{v}_i^{t_{k+1}} \right\|,
$$
it follows from the previous results that \(\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}\) is bijective if
$$
d_{\tau} = \max_{0 \le k < m} d_k < \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2nM^*},
$$
where
$$
M^* = \max_{1 \le i \le n} \sup_{t \in [0,1]} \sup_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \Omega^t} \left\| \nabla \boldsymbol{\psi}^t_i(\boldsymbol{x}) \right\|.
$$

### 4.3.1 Limit of composite barycentric mappings

The idea of uniform composite barycentric mappings leads to the interesting question of the behavior in the limit. To this end, we consider the *infinite composite barycentric mapping* $f_\infty = \lim_{m \to \infty} f_{r_m}$ and its backward mapping $g_\infty : \Omega^1 \to \Omega^0$. Figure 4.10 shows the result of mapping a star with a uniform composite mapping $f_{r_m}$ composed with its backward mapping $g_{r_m} : \Omega^1 \to \Omega^0$ with the same number of steps. Computing the maximum distance $\|x - g_{r_m}(f_{r_m}(x))\|$ for one million random points $x \in \Omega^0$ indicates that this distance converges to zero and so $g_{r_m} = f_{r_m}^{-1}$ as $m \to \infty$. Consequently, the inverse of an infinite barycentric mapping is likely to be a infinite barycentric mapping itself, which is not the case for standard barycentric mappings.

This observation can be proven by considering the difference between two successive steps and taking its limit [145]. For any point $x^0 \in \Omega^0$ we evaluate the first step $f_0$ of the composite mapping $f$,
$$
x^{t_1} = f_0(x^0) = \sum_{i=1}^n \phi_i^{t_0}(x^0) \psi_i(t_1).
$$
Because of the linear reproduction property,
$$
x^0 = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i^0 \phi_i^{t_0}(x^0) \psi_i(t_0),
$$

![](img/4913dd83a2a14bc205a0a92cce102c82_90_0.png)

Figure 4.10 Composing the mapping $f_{r_m}$ and the backward mapping $g_{r_m}$ converges to the identity as the number of uniform steps $m$ increases.

hence
$$
x^{t_1} - x^0 = \sum_{i=1}^n \phi_i^{t_0}(x^0)(\psi_i(t_1) - \psi_i(t_0)).
$$
Dividing by $t_1 - t_0$ and taking the limit for $t_1 \to t_0$, yields
$$
x'(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n \phi_i(x(t), t)\psi'_i(t),
$$
which is a first-order differential equation in $x(t)$ of the form
$$
x'(t) = F(t, x(t)).
$$
with initial condition $x(0) = x^0$.

If the barycentric coordinates $\phi_i$ are Lipschitz-continuous, then $F$ is Lipschitz-continuous, too, which is a sufficient condition for the existence of a local unique solution, according to the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem [250, 308]. The solution of the differential equation is also a global solution, since $x(t)$ stays inside all intermediate polygons. This is the case because the mapping is bijective and edges are mapped to edges.

In order to show that $g^{-1} = f$, we consider, for any point $x^t = x(1) \in \bar{\Omega}^1$,
$$
y'(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n \phi_i(x(1-t), 1-t)\bar{\psi}'_i(t), \quad \bar{\psi}_i(t) = \psi_i(1-t),
$$
with $y(0) = x(1)$ as the mapping of $x^1$ from $\bar{\Omega}^1$ to $\bar{\Omega}^0$. Because $\bar{\psi}'_i(t) = -\psi'_i(1-t)$, we conclude that $y(t) = x(1-t)$. Therefore, $y(1) = x^0$ and $g$ has an inverse generated by the previous equation with $g^{-1} = f$.

## 4.4 EXTENSIONS

The sufficient condition for guaranteeing bijectivity of a composite barycentric mapping between polygons can be naturally extended to mappings between closed curves and polyhedra.

### 4.4.1 Closed planar curves

We define the *barycentric mapping* between a closed planar source curve $\gamma^0(s)$ and a closed planar target curve $\gamma^1(s)$ with $s \in [0, 1]$, as
$$
f(x) = \int_0^1 \phi(s, x)\gamma^1'(s)ds,
$$
where $\phi(s, x)$ are the transfinite barycentric coordinates with respect to $\gamma^0$ (see Chapter 3). Analogously to the polygonal case, we now consider a perturbed target curve \(\gamma^1(s) = \gamma^0(s) + \boldsymbol{u}(s)\) and assume that the maximum displacement distance satisfies
$$
d = \sup_{s \in [0,1]} \|\boldsymbol{u}(s)\| < \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2M},
$$
where
$$
M = \sup_{s \in [0,1]} \sup_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \gamma^0} \|\nabla \phi(s, \boldsymbol{x})\|.
$$
We exploit the linear reproduction property to rewrite
$$
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{x} + \int_0^1 \phi(s, \boldsymbol{x}) \boldsymbol{u}(s) ds
$$
and compute
$$
J\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \left| \begin{matrix} 1 + \int \partial_1 \phi(s, \boldsymbol{x}) u_1(s) ds & \int \partial_2 \phi(s, \boldsymbol{x}) u_1(s) ds \\ \int \partial_1 \phi(s, \boldsymbol{x}) u_2(s) ds & 1 + \int \partial_2 \phi(s, \boldsymbol{x}) u_2(s) ds \end{matrix} \right| = 1 + \int \nabla \phi(s, \boldsymbol{x}) \cdot \boldsymbol{u}(s) ds + \iint \partial_1 \phi(s, \boldsymbol{x}) \partial_2 \phi(r, \boldsymbol{x}) (\boldsymbol{u}(s) \times \boldsymbol{u}(r)) ds dr,
$$
where the integrals range from zero to one. Therefore,
$$
J\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}) \ge 1 - Md - M^2 d^2,
$$
which is strictly positive for \(Md < (\sqrt{5}-1)/2\). Hence the mapping is injective as long as \(d < \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2M}\). It is interesting to note that this bound is the same as in the polygonal case.

Now suppose that \(\boldsymbol{\psi}\) is a continuous _closed curve_ interpolation function between the source curve \(\gamma^0 = \boldsymbol{\psi}(0)\) and the target curve \(\gamma^1 = \boldsymbol{\psi}(1)\). Again we let \(\tau = (t_0, t_1, \dots, t_m)\) with \(t_0 = 0, t_m = 1\), and \(t_k < t_{k+1}\) for \(k = 0, \dots, m-1\) be a _partition_ of \([0,1]\), and let \(\boldsymbol{f}_k\) be the barycentric mapping from \(\gamma^{t_k}\) to \(\gamma^{t_{k+1}}\), based on the barycentric coordinates \(\phi^k\). The mapping
$$
\boldsymbol{f}_\tau = \boldsymbol{f}_{m-1} \circ \boldsymbol{f}_{m-2} \circ \cdots \circ \boldsymbol{f}_0,
$$
