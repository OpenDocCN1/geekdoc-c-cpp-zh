
\(\frac{\partial^{n-1}u}{\partial e_1^{n-1}}(x + \rho_1 e_\theta) = \frac{\partial^{n-1}u}{\partial e_1^{n-1}}(x) + \rho_1 u^{(n)}(x) + \cdots + \frac{\rho_1^{2n}}{(2n)!} \frac{\partial^{2n}u}{\partial e_\theta^{2n}}(x),\)
\(\frac{\partial^{n-1}u}{\partial e_1^{n-1}}(x - \rho_2 e_\theta) = \frac{\partial^{n-1}u}{\partial e_1^{n-1}}(x) - \rho_2 u^{(n)}(x) + \cdots + \frac{(-\rho_2)^{2n}}{(2n)!} \frac{\partial^{2n}u}{\partial e_\theta^{2n}}(x).\)

Eliminating terms in \(\partial u(x)/\partial e_\theta, \dots, \partial^{2n-1}u(x)/\partial e_\theta^{2n-1}\) we arrive at

\(\frac{\partial^{2n}u}{\partial e_\theta^{2n}}(x) = \frac{(2n)!}{\rho_1^n \rho_2^n} [u(x) - \alpha_1 u(y_1) - \alpha_2 u(y_2) - \cdots\)
\(- \alpha_{2n-1} \frac{\partial^{n-1}u}{\partial e_\theta^{n-1}}(y_1) - \alpha_{2n} \frac{\partial^{n-1}u}{\partial e_\theta^{n-1}}(y_2)],\)

where \(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{2n}\) are some weights. Combining with (3.29) and mimicking polyharmonic interpolation gives a high-order generalization of the modified Gordon-Wixom interpolation scheme.

## 3.4 GENERALIZED MEAN VALUE POTENTIALS AND DISTANCE FUNCTION APPROXIMATIONS

In this section we study properties of generalized mean value potentials, which are generalizations of the normalization function for the transfinite mean value coordinates; that is, the denominator in (3.4). We show that these generalized mean value potentials deliver accurate approximations of the distance function \(\text{dist}(x, \partial\Omega)\) near \(\partial\Omega\) and can be seen as smooth distance functions. Efficient computation of smooth distance functions is important for a number of applications, including pattern recognition [435], computational mechanics [157], medical imaging [338], and computational fluid dynamics [332, 399].

### 3.4.1 Generalized mean value potentials for smooth domains

Floater et al. [77, 133] observed some remarkable properties of the mean value normalization function

\(\Phi(x) = \int_{S_x} \frac{d\theta}{\|x - y\|},\)

which is the denominator in (3.4). In particular, for \(\Psi(x) = 1/\Phi(x)\), they show that

\(\Psi(y) = 0 \text{ and } \frac{\partial \Psi(y)}{\partial n_y} = \frac{1}{V_{d-1}} \quad \forall y \in \partial\Omega, \quad (3.30)\)

where \(n_y\) is the outer unit normal for the boundary of \(\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) at \(y \in \partial\Omega\) and \(V_{d-1}\) is the volume of the unit ball in \(\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\) (\(V_1 = 2\) and \(V_2 = \pi\)). In view of (3.30), the function \(V_{d-1}\Psi(x)\) delivers an accurate approximation of the distance function \(\text{dist}(x, \partial\Omega)\) near \(\partial\Omega\).

This property can be used for Hermite interpolation purposes as in [77, 133]. More generally, the distance function property also plays an important role in computer graphics, visualization, and modeling. For example, it is used as a parameter for material modeling in [51], for the animation of shapes in [106], and in level-set methods [297]. See also [214] and the references therein for further applications in computer graphics and visualization.

It seems natural to consider the $L^p$ generalizations of $\Phi(\boldsymbol{x})$ and $\Psi(\boldsymbol{x})$. Following [39, 40], let us consider

\(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x}) = \int_{S_n} \frac{d\theta}{\|\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{y}\|^p} \quad \text{and} \quad \Psi_p(\boldsymbol{x}) = 1/\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x}) \quad (3.31)\)

with \(p > 0\). Below, for the sake of simplicity, we deal with the two-dimensional version of (3.31). A similar study of the generalized mean value potential \(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x})\) in the 3D case can be found in [39].

In the 2D case, \(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x})\) can be written as

\(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x}) = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \frac{d\theta}{\rho(\theta)^p}\)

where, as before, \(\theta\) is the angle between the vector \(\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{x}\) and a fixed direction and \(\rho(\theta) = \|\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{y}\|\). The generalized mean value potential \(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x})\) satisfies some interesting properties, including [40, Proposition 3].

\(\Delta \Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x}) = p(p+2)\Phi_{p+2}(\boldsymbol{x}). \quad (3.32)\)

It is clear that \(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x}) > 0\) and \(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x}) \rightarrow \infty\) as \(\boldsymbol{x}\) approaches \(\partial \Omega\). Further, (3.32) implies that \(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x})\) has no local maxima inside \(\Omega\), because otherwise \(\Delta \Phi_p > 0\) at a local maximum and (3.32) cannot be satisfied. Hence, \(\Psi_p(\boldsymbol{x})\) is positive inside \(\Omega\), vanishes on \(\partial \Omega\), and has no local minima inside \(\Omega\). Below we study the asymptotic properties of \(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x})\) near \(\partial \Omega\) (or, equivalently, the asymptotic properties of \(\Psi_p(\boldsymbol{x})\) near \(\partial \Omega\)) and show that, after a proper constant rescaling, \(\Psi_p(\boldsymbol{x})\) delivers an accurate approximation of \(\text{dist}(\boldsymbol{x}, \partial \Omega)\) near \(\partial \Omega\).

Let \(\boldsymbol{x}\) be near \(\partial \Omega\) and denote by \(h = \text{dist}(\boldsymbol{x}, \partial \Omega)\) the distance from \(\boldsymbol{x}\) to \(\partial \Omega\). We keep following [39] and now show that in the planar case (\(d=2\)) for \(p > 1\) we have

\(\Phi_p(\boldsymbol{x}) = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \frac{d\theta}{\rho(\theta)^p} = \frac{c_p}{h^p} + k \frac{c_{p-2}}{2h^{p-2}} + O\left(\frac{1}{h^{p-2}}\right) \quad (3.33)\)

with

\(c_p = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos^p \theta \, d\theta,\)

where \(k\) is the curvature of \(\partial \Omega\) at \(\boldsymbol{y} \in \partial \Omega\). The case \(p=1\), which corresponds to the mean value normalization function, yields

\(\Phi_1(\boldsymbol{x}) \sim \frac{2}{h} + \frac{k}{2} \ln \frac{1}{h} + O(1) \quad \text{as} \quad h \rightarrow 0, \quad (3.34),\)

which is an extension of one of the main results in [133].

Figure 3.7. To analyze the asymptotic behavior of \(\Phi_p(x)\) when \(x\) approaches \(\partial\Omega\) (\(h \to 0\)) we locally approximate \(\partial\Omega\) by osculating parabolas.

Let us demonstrate (3.33) and (3.34). Given a point \(x \in \Omega\), situated at the distance \(h \ll 1\) from \(\partial\Omega\), let us introduce Euclidean and polar coordinates, as shown in Figure 3.7, where the origin of coordinates is located at the closest point to \(x\) on \(\partial\Omega\) and the \(y\)-axis coincides with the direction of the orientation normal.

Let us start with the case of positive curvature \(k\) of \(\partial\Omega\) at the origin of coordinates. Locally, \(\partial\Omega\) is approximated by the osculating parabola

\(y = x^2/(2R),\)

where \(R = 1/k\) is the curvature radius, as seen in Figure 3.7 (left). In polar coordinates

\(x = \rho \sin \theta, \quad y = h - \rho \cos \theta\)

the parabola becomes

\(\rho^2 \sin^2 \theta + 2R\rho \cos \theta - 2Rh = 0.\)

Solving this quadratic equation for \(\rho > 0\) yields

\(\rho = \frac{-R\cos \theta + \sqrt{D}}{\sin^2 \theta}, \quad D = R^2 \cos^2 \theta + 2Rh \sin^2 \theta\)

and we can write

\(\frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{1}{2Rh} \left( R\cos \theta \sqrt{1 + \frac{2h}{R} \tan^2 \theta + R\cos \theta} \right) = \frac{\cos \theta}{h} + \frac{1}{2R} \frac{\sin^2 \theta}{\cos \theta} + O(h).\)

Therefore,

\(\Phi_p(x) \sim \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \frac{d\theta}{\rho(\theta)^p} = \frac{c_p}{h^p} + \frac{1}{R} \frac{\tilde{c}_p}{h^{p-1}} + O\left( \frac{1}{h^{p-2}} \right),\)

where

\(c_p = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos^p \theta \, d\theta \quad \text{and} \quad \tilde{c}_p = \frac{p}{2} \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos^{p-2} \theta \sin^2 \theta \, d\theta. \quad (3.35)\)

Simple calculations show that \(\tilde{c}_p = c_{p-2}/2\).

Above we assumed that \(p > 1\). If \(p = 1\) (the case of mean value coordinates), then we have

\(\Phi_1(x) = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \frac{d\theta}{\rho(\theta)} = \frac{2}{h} + \frac{1}{2R} \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin^2 \theta}{\cos \theta} \, d\theta + O(h)\)
