## 4.1 INTRODUCTION

A barycentric mapping between a source polygon \(\Omega^0 \subset \mathbb{R}^2\) with \(n \ge 3\) source vertices \(v_i^0, i = 1, \dots, n\), ordered anticlockwise, and a target polygon \(\Omega^1 \subset \mathbb{R}^2\) with the same number of target vertices \(v_i^1\), is a mapping

\(f: \Omega^0 \to \Omega^1, \quad f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n \phi_i(x) v_i^1, \quad (4.1)\)

where the functions \(\phi_i: \Omega^0 \to \mathbb{R}, i = 1, \dots, n\) are barycentric coordinates with respect to \(\Omega^0\) (see Chapter 1). Since \(f\) depends on the particular choice of coordinates, we denote the mapping \(f\) with the name of the coordinate generating it. For instance, a Wachspress mapping is a barycentric mapping based on Wachspress coordinates.

Because of the Lagrange property (1.10b), it is clear that \(f(v_i^0) = v_i^1\). Moreover, if a point \(x = (1 - \mu) v_i^0 + \mu v_{i+1}^0\) lies on the edge \([v_i^0, v_{i+1}^0]\), the only non-zero coordinates are \(\phi_i(x) = 1 - \mu\) and \(\phi_{i+1}(x) = \mu\), hence the mapping is \(f(x) = (1 - \mu) v_i^1 + \mu v_{i+1}^1\), which means that it is linear along the edges, too. Finally, the mapping \(f\) is always surjective, because the coordinates \(\phi_i\) are continuous, which implies that \(f\) is continuous on \(\Omega^0\), and edges are mapped to edges.

Figure 4.1 shows an example of a barycentric mapping for different coordinates. We see that the mapping is linear along the edges, regardless of the choice of coordinates, and that it is not bijective; that is, the grid folds over in concave regions.

## 4.1.2 Arbitrary polygons

For any choice of barycentric coordinates, it is possible to construct a source and a target polygon such that the barycentric mapping is not bijective [208]. In order to construct such a counterexample, let us consider the barycentric mapping between a square and a deformed square, as shown in Figure 4.3.

Because we only move \(v_i^0\), we know that \(v_i^1 - v_i^0 = 0\) for \(i = 1, 2, 4\), and we can rewrite the mapping \(f\), evaluated at the origin, as,

\(f(\mathbf{0}) = \sum_{i=1}^4 \phi_i(\mathbf{0})(v_i^1 - v_i^0 + v_i^0) = \sum_{i=1}^4 \phi_i(\mathbf{0})(v_i^1 - v_i^0) = \phi_3(\mathbf{0})(v_3^1 - v_3^0) = \phi_3(\mathbf{0})(v_3^1 + v_1^0),\)

where we exploit the fact that \(v_3^0 = -v_1^0\) in the last step.

We first assume that \(\phi_3(\mathbf{0}) > 0.5\) and show that there exists a choice of \(v_3^1\) such that \(f(\mathbf{0}) = v_1^1\), which contradicts the bijectivity of the mapping. To this end,

## 4.2 BIJECTIVE BARYCENTRIC MAPPING

Let us denote the *partial derivatives* of the barycentric mapping \(f = (f_1, f_2)\) in (4.1) at \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2) \in \hat{\Omega}^0\) by \(\partial_k f(\mathbf{x}) = \partial f / \partial x_k, k = 1, 2\), and the *gradients* of its two components \(f_i\) by \(\nabla f_i = (\partial_1 f_i, \partial_2 f_i)\).

Since we consider only source and target polygons without self-intersections and assume that the barycentric coordinates \(\phi_i\) are at least continuously differentiable, a sufficient condition for the injectivity of \(f\) is that its Jacobian determinant

\(J_f = \begin{vmatrix} \partial_1 f_1 & \partial_2 f_1 \\ \partial_1 f_2 & \partial_2 f_2 \end{vmatrix}\)

is strictly positive in \(\hat{\Omega}^0\) [272]. As it follows from (4.1) that a barycentric mapping between identical source and target polygons is the identity with \(J_f(\mathbf{x}) = 1\) for all \(\mathbf{x} \in \hat{\Omega}^0\), it is reasonable to expect that a small perturbation of the target vertices keeps \(J_f\) positive and the mapping bijective, as shown in Figure 4.4.

## 4.2.1 Perturbed target polygons

Consider first a target polygon with a single perturbed vertex, as shown in Figure 4.5. Formally, the target vertices are \(v_i^1 = v_i^0 + \mathbf{u}\) for some \(i\) and \(v_j^1 = v_j^0\) for \( j \neq i \). Substituting these target vertices \( \boldsymbol{v}_i^1 \) in (4.1) and recalling the linear reproduction property (1.9), we obtain

$$
f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{x} + \phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{u}
$$

for any \( \boldsymbol{x} \in \bar{\Omega}^0 \), and further,

$$
J_f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \left| \begin{matrix} 1 + \partial_1\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})u_{i,1} & \partial_2\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})u_{i,1} \\ \partial_1\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})u_{i,2} & 1 + \partial_2\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})u_{i,2} \end{matrix} \right| = 1 + \nabla\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x}) \cdot \boldsymbol{u} .
$$

Therefore,

$$
J_f(\boldsymbol{x}) \ge 1 - \|\nabla\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x}) \cdot \boldsymbol{u}\| \ge 1 - \|\boldsymbol{u}\|\|\nabla\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})\| ,
$$

which is strictly positive for \( \|\boldsymbol{u}\| < 1/M_i \) with

$$
M_i = \sup_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \bar{\Omega}^0} \|\nabla\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})\| .
$$

This result nicely extends to a perturbation of all vertices, where we consider a target polygon with vertices \( \boldsymbol{v}_i^1 = \boldsymbol{v}_i^0 + \boldsymbol{u}_i \) for \( i = 1, \dots, n \) (Figure 4.5). Using the linear reproduction property again, we reformulate the mapping as

$$
f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{x} + \sum_{i=1}^n \phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{u}_i
$$

for any \( \boldsymbol{x} \in \bar{\Omega}^0 \), hence

$$
J_f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \left| \begin{matrix} 1 + \sum_i \partial_1\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})u_{i,1} & \sum_i \partial_2\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})u_{i,1} \\ \sum_i \partial_1\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})u_{i,2} & 1 + \sum_i \partial_2\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})u_{i,2} \end{matrix} \right| = 1 + \sum_i \nabla\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x}) \cdot \boldsymbol{u}_i + \sum_i \sum_j \partial_1\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x})\partial_2\phi_j(\boldsymbol{x})(\boldsymbol{u}_i \times \boldsymbol{u}_j) ,
$$

where \( \boldsymbol{u}_i = (u_{i,1}, u_{i,2}) \) and the sums range from 1 to \( n \). Therefore,

$$
J_f(\boldsymbol{x}) \ge 1 - Md - M^2d^2
$$
