Mimicking harmonic interpolation thus leads to the modification of the Gordon–Wixom scheme (3.18)

```
u(\boldsymbol{x}) = \int_0^{2\pi} \left[ \left( \frac{u(\boldsymbol{y}_1)}{\rho_1} + \frac{u(\boldsymbol{y}_2)}{\rho_2} \right) \bigg/ \left[ \frac{1}{\rho_1} + \frac{1}{\rho_2} \right] \right] \frac{d\theta}{\rho_1 \rho_2} \bigg/ \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{d\theta}{\rho_1 \rho_2}, \quad (3.22)
```

which was obtained in [38]. In some sense, (3.22) resembles the transfinite mean value interpolation scheme (3.4).

The modified Gordon-Wixom interpolation scheme (3.22) is also pseudo-harmonic. Indeed, if \(\Omega\) is a circle, then \(\rho_{1,2}\) in (3.22) does not depend on the integration variable \(\theta\) and (3.22) reduces to (3.18), which is pseudo-harmonic. The modified Gordon-Wixom scheme also has linear precision.

Both (3.18) and (3.22) can be considered as particular cases of weighted mean value transfinite interpolation (3.7). Indeed, both can be rewritten as

```
u(\boldsymbol{x}) = \int_0^{2\pi} \left[ \left( \frac{u(\boldsymbol{y}_1)}{\rho_1} + \frac{u(\boldsymbol{y}_2)}{\rho_2} \right) \bigg/ \left[ \frac{1}{\rho_1} + \frac{1}{\rho_2} \right] \right] \bar{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta) d\theta \bigg/ \int_0^{2\pi} \bar{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta) d\theta, \quad (3.23)
```

where the weighting functions \(\bar{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta)\) in (3.23) and \(\omega(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta)\) in (3.7) are related by

```
\bar{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta) = (1/\rho_1 + 1/\rho_2)\omega(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta).
```

Setting \(\bar{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta) = (1/\rho_1 + 1/\rho_2)\) in (3.23) corresponds to the transfinite mean value interpolation scheme (3.4), choosing \(\bar{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta) = 1\) yields the Gordon–Wixom scheme (3.18), and \(\bar{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta) = 1/(\rho_1 \rho_2)\) gives the modified Gordon-Wixom scheme (3.22).

In practice (3.22) demonstrates a higher quality of interpolation compared with (3.18). In our numerical experiments (3.18) and (3.22) are evaluated using the composite trapezoidal rule (typically 100 equispaced directions are used). For non-convex domains, the ray \((\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y})\) defined by the unit vector \(\boldsymbol{e}_\theta\) can intersect the boundary at more than one point. For the two opposite directions \(\boldsymbol{e}_\theta\) and \(-\boldsymbol{e}_\theta\) we consider the intersections \(\boldsymbol{y}_1\) and \(\boldsymbol{y}_2\) that are closest to \(\boldsymbol{x}\), respectively. The absolute errors between the given function and its interpolations is used to illustrate the advantages of (3.22) over (3.18).

Gordon and Wixom extended their scheme in [171] to handle Hermite-type interpolation. Replacing linear interpolation with cubic interpolation of \((u(\boldsymbol{y}_1), u'(\boldsymbol{y}_1))\) and \((u(\boldsymbol{y}_2), u'(\boldsymbol{y}_2))\) gives

```
u(\boldsymbol{x}, \theta) = \frac{(3\rho_1 + \rho_2)\rho_2^2}{(\rho_1 + \rho_2)^3} u(\boldsymbol{y}_1) + \frac{(\rho_1 + 3\rho_2)\rho_1^2}{(\rho_1 + \rho_2)^3} u(\boldsymbol{y}_2) - \frac{\rho_1 \rho_2^2}{(\rho_1 + \rho_2)^2} \frac{\partial u}{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_\theta}(\boldsymbol{y}_1) + \frac{\rho_1^2 \rho_2}{(\rho_1 + \rho_2)^2} \frac{\partial u}{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_\theta}(\boldsymbol{y}_2). \quad (3.24)
```
