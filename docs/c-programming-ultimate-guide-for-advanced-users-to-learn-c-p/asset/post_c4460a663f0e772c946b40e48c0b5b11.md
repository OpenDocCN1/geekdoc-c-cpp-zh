## Inheritance

When you describe an inheritance relationship there are two classes required at least. One class is the parent and the second is the child. (The association among parent and child is also called superclass and subclass, where superclass is synonymous with parent and subclass is synonymous with child.) Such a parent-child relationship is called an IsA relationship. For example, I am the descendant of my father (and mother), so I am a Kimmel (and Symons), inheriting the DNA goop that originates from Kimmel's (and Symons').

When you describe an inheritance relationship in C#, the child class gets all of the members described in the parent class. When you inherit, you get all of the fields, properties, and methods described in the parent. Rather than chromosomal inheritance, object-oriented inheritance implies you get everything.

I assume most of you get inheritance, but there is one more illustration that I'd like to show since inheritance is an important concept. If class B inherits from class A, then, logically, an instance of B would be a class A.

The documentation for inheritance is different from that for interface inheritance. To show that class B inherits from class A, you would write the class header as follows:

```
public class B : A
```

Unlike with interface inheritance, when you inherit from a class, you don't have to implement any methods from the parent class since they are presently implemented in the parent class.

### Inheritance Terminology

There are a lot of terms related with inheritance associations. Since you will encounter them in this book, I will overview a couple of them here rapidly.

- If class B inherits from A, then A is the parent and B is the child.
- If class B inherits from A, then A is also referred to as the superclass and B is the subclass.
- Inheritance relationships satisfy the IsA test. In our model we would say that B IsA A (even though it is etymologically horrifying).
- Inheriting is also referred to as generalizing.
- Siblings are classes that inherit from a common parent.
- Every now and then, though occasionally, you will hear people imply grandparents, ancestors, and descendants when talking about inheritance relationships.

It is possible that these strategies for referring to something fundamentally the same as inheritance can provoke confusion.

Clearly, no one has referred to second cousins or distant aunts when talking about object inheritance relationships, yet these terms could be used and should be used in order to depict distinct associations from when the terms are used to describe human family relationship. For example, a cousin would be the offspring of your parent's siblings.

### General Guidelines for Inheritance

You're the director with respect to inheritance. In actuality, I have included a couple of rules for using inheritance associations; at the end of the day you should choose on a case-by-case basis.

- If you describe a class and later realize that it ought to be refactored, then consider inheriting from the base class to incorporate the new code.
- Try to keep your inheritance relationships shallow and wide. There is no perfect ratio between the numbers of siblings to children, but there generally are more siblings than descendants in established structures.
- Investigate Refactoring. Refactoring is the best way written so far to guide software engineers in improving existing code. Fundamentally, Refactoring is a disciplined approach to improving existing code. (Unfortunately, we can't explain Refactoring here, but Refactorings will be used to make the code listings in this book.)
- Don't be hesitant to test and change your point of view.
- Research existing frameworks to get a sense of scope. (Great frameworks to examine include Delphi's VCL and the .Net Framework.)

## Encapsulation and Aggregation

Encapsulation and aggregation refer to the capacity of classes to contain members. More specifically, aggregation refers to the capacity of classes (and structures) to contain members whose types are also classes (or structures).

My preferred example of encapsulation is the connection between the human body and the bladder. Fortunately, the bladder is encapsulated within the human body. Could you imagine a reality where individuals could express their disappointment with you by pressing your bladder? "Ouch, that's my foot!" Squeeze. What a mess.

Obviously, the bladder is a class. It performs a function and has state. We understand what the function is, and we frequently feel the state at the most difficult times. Fortunately, the bladder is encapsulated behind skin and bone and an innate sense of personal space.

This is how you should see encapsulation and aggregation. Encapsulation enables you to put information safely within classes, constraining access and avoiding embarrassing situations. Aggregation enables you to put complex types safely within classes, again constraining access and avoiding embarrassing situations.

Figure out where a specific information component should go by its semantic importance and place that information within a suitable class. A good way to think about where to put information is by thinking about whose job it is to own and secure the information. (Don't let your neighbor have your bladder.) Think of aggregation as including complex types—read classes—to new types. The bladder is a complex type, and the human body has a part that is a bladder.

In a specialist medical framework, implementing a class that represents the human body and the bladder may be fitting. ## Polymorphism

Polymorphism has been described from various perspectives. Several times I have seen reference to its Greek roots, given as "many forms," and sometimes the descriptions are long and complex. I am going to try something a bit different. I am going to use an analogy that I hope makes sense to you.

Assume you have a household chore that is appropriate for a child to do. I have four children. We start them on chores at eighteen months. Truly, a little child can empty wastebaskets at eighteen months. Cold-blooded, maybe, yet practical. Here is the relationship.

You are watching the Superbowl. It is the fourth quarter with two minutes to go, and your team has the ball but is down by 8 points. Not only do they have to make the touchdown, but your team needs to get the two-point conversion. To improve the tension, you have $500 wagered with a bookie for your team to win. Now, you're out of beer. You need a beer, but you can't leave the game because when you get back, the game will be over. You need a child to make the beer run, and any child will do.

That is polymorphism.

When you have a general problem that can be solved by any supplier within a common set of suppliers, then you need polymorphism.
