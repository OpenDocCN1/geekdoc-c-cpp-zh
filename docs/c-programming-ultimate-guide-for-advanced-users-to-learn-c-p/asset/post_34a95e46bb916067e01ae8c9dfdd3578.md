Programmers talk about programming on weekends, vacations, and over dinners not because they lack imagination, but because their imagination reveals worlds that others can't see.

Programming is likewise a skill that forms the basis of one of only a handful of professions that is consistently in demand, pays reasonably well, allows for flexibility in location and working hours, and which prides itself on rewarding merit, not circumstances of birth. Not every skilled programmer is employed, women are underrepresented in management, and development teams are not idealistic utopias. But overall, software development is an excellent career choice.

Coding, the line-by-line creation of precise instructions for the machine to execute, is and will always be the core activity of software development. We can say this with confidence because no matter what happens in terms of specification languages, probabilistic inference, and machine intelligence, it will always be meticulous work to remove the ambiguity from a statement of customer value.

Ambiguity itself is hugely important to people ("That's lovely!" "You can't miss the killer," "With liberty and justice for all") and software development, like making legal documents, is where the details are fundamentally given a prominence that is the exact opposite of how people like to communicate.

This is not to say that coding will always consist of writing highly structured lines of text. The Unified Modeling Language (UML), which specifies the grammar and semantics of various diagrams appropriate to various software development tasks, is expressive enough that one could code in it. However, doing so is enormously inefficient when compared to writing lines of text. On the other hand, a single UML diagram can explain in a minute structural and dynamic relationships that would take minutes or hours to appreciate with a word processor or a debugger. It is a certainty that as software systems continue to grow in complexity, no single representation will prove universally efficient. But the task of removing ambiguity, task-by-task, bit by bit, will always be a tedious, error-prone process whose efficiency is dependent on the talents of one or more developers.

There is more to professional software development than writing code. Computer programs are among the most complex structures ever devised by humanity and the difficulties of communicating desires and constraints, coordinating effort, managing risk, and above all, maintaining a work environment that attracts the best people and produces their greatest efforts... well, software project management takes an unusual combination of skills, skills that are perhaps rarer than the skills associated with coding. Every great programmer discovers this eventually (usually in the early years) and the best programmers inevitably develop strong opinions about the software development process and how it is best done. They become team leads and architects, build managers and CTOs, and as these elite programmers challenge themselves with these tasks, they sometimes ignore or dismiss as trivial the difficulties that arise between the lines of a method.

This book stops at the screen edge. That is not a judgment that issues of modeling and process and collaboration aren't as important as the task of coding; we the authors know that these things are at least as important to the development of successful products as coding. One reason that the concerns of coding (rather than the simple details of coding) are seldom examined in a language-specific way is that it is almost impossible to assume anything about the background of an individual programming in Java.

Since it's impossible for us to assume anything about your background, we instead assume a few things about your skills and motivation. This book continually moves the level of discourse from details to theory and back to details, a process that is clearly wrong for some students. Quick moves of abstraction levels are an integral part of software development, however. Most programmers can relate to the experience of a high-flying meeting with discussion of "collaboration" and "new paradigms" and "money dripping from the ceiling" being suddenly interrupted by a programmer who suspiciously says "Now wait a second... " and says something unintelligible to non-programmers.

Then another programmer says something similarly unintelligible in response. This "talking in code" goes back and forth for a moment or so and the suspicious programmer suddenly shakes his head and announces to the managers: "Oh, you don't see how huge this is!"

This is not a book about shortcuts and getting by, it is a book about solving difficult problems in a professional way. To understand why Java prevails at a programming level, however, it's critical to see how it prevails at the business level, which means talking about the economics of software development.

As far back as Alan Turing introduced the concept of a general-purpose computer and then John von Neumann the idea of a stored program, software creators have struggled to balance the image-manipulating power of increasing levels of abstraction with the physical constraints of speed, storage, and transmission-channel capacity of the existing machine. There are individuals still alive who can talk about reading the output of the most modern computer in existence by holding a cardboard ruler up to an oscilloscope and judging the signal as either a one or a zero. As late as the mid-1980s, the coolest computers in widespread use (the DEC PDP series) could be programmed by flipping switches on a board, directly controlling chip-level signals. Furthermore, until the 1990s, it was impossible for a computer programmer to be ignorant of a wide range of interrupt requests and the memory locations of various devices.

Between the mid-1960s, when the IBM 360 was released and Gordon Moore reported his famous law that transistor density in a given area would keep doubling at regular intervals, the cost of a single processing instruction has decreased by roughly 99.99%. This has completely transformed the economics of software development. Where once it made sense for the programmer to work with a mental model of the computer's internal representation and to sacrifice development time for execution efficiency, now it makes sense for the computer to be given a model corresponding to the developer's internal representation of the task, even if that representation is not ideally efficient.

Today, the quality of a programming language can be judged by how effectively one can express the broadest range of problems and solutions. By that standard, object-oriented, imperative programming languages absolutely dominate the universe of software development. An imperative language is one in which a sequence of instructions is given to the computer: do this, then do that, then accomplish this other thing. The imperative may seem the "common" way to program computers in that it relates to our mental model of how computers work, but as discussed above, this is no longer an excellent reason to embrace a programming language. Consider how easy some problems are to solve with a spreadsheet, which can be seen as a form of non-imperative programming. However, imperative programming is deeply ingrained in the mainstream zeitgeist and is unlikely to be deposed at any time in the near future.

Since the mid-'90s, the world of software has been transformed. Before the explosive growth of the Internet, most programs were written for either internal use within an organization or were "shrink-wrapped" applications that provided some sort of generic service for a nondescript client. With the rise of the Web, however, an enormous amount of programming effort has moved to directly delivering value to the client.
