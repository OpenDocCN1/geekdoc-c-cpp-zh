Short-interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) are typically up to 500 bases long (Gogvadze and Buzdin, 2009), and are lineage specific. They do not have a protein-coding payload. The most famous of these are the primate-specific Alu SINE, which comprises over 10% of the human genome. This SINE is derived from the 7SL RNA, and most SINEs are a source of recombination events in genome evolution. Because SINE elements are lineage specific, homology search against databases of known SINEs is important. REPBASE (Jurka et al., 2005) is a database with the largest source of repetitive elements, submitted by researchers and derived from combing the literature. RepeatMasker (www.repeatmasker.org) allows one to use various alignment methods to match such repeat sequence databases. REPBASE's maintainers also provide a repeat identifying system, Censor (Kohany et al., 2006). In either case, sensitivity thresholds play an important role in determining how sensitive repeat masking will be, especially when the organism being masked is distantly related to the well-characterized species from which REPBASE entries are derived. A database of known repeats is particularly useful for masking sequences during transcript or genome assembly, where there is no sequence information available to do de novo identification of repeats.

*de novo* methods for repeat identification are also essential, because the databases of known elements are not complete. The task of finding approximate substrings of length n or longer, with less than x% mismatch is computationally expensive on a genome-wide scale. EulerAlign (Zhang and Waterman, 2005) uses a data structure known as a de Bruijn graph to make the task time approximately linear to the sequence length. REPeter (Kurt et al., 2001) is able to handle errors or degeneracy in a repeat sequence based on a specialized algorithm, with runtime increasing significantly on the number of errors to allow. The RepeatModel software, available from the RepeatMasker Web site (www.repeatmasker.org), uses two different methods of *de novo* prediction and subsequently combines the results. In general, each method has strengths and weaknesses based on the most important factor to the software author (e.g., sensitivity, accuracy). It is preferable therefore annotate repeat elements in a genome based on the concurrence of two or more methods.
