Although mapping of short reads or assembled mRNAs back to the genome can be very helpful in determining correct intron and exon boundaries, it is only one component of the overall gene prediction process. This is because (1) it is unlikely that full-length mRNA sequences can be obtained for all genes; (2) in some species transcripts may encode operons of more than one gene; and (3) start stop codons cannot be determined from the mRNA alone due to multiple translation frames and factors affecting translation. As such, transcript information is therefore supplemented in most gene prediction software by statistical models of gene feature sequences.

## 2.3 STATISTICAL MODELS

Most initio gene predict models are based on a data structure known as a generalized hidden Markov model (GHMM). Conceptually, it can be thought of as a graph where the nodes represent events in the gene's expression, and arrows connecting the nodes represent the scanning of the sequence, as shown in Figure 2.1. It should be noted that the actual GHMMs used in prediction programs have many more states (nodes) and transitions (arrows) than in this example.

Each gene model is calculated by progressively scanning each base of the genomic sequence, looking for signals to transition from one state to the next. In the case of a transcription start, the signal being searched for is not necessarily the exact string GCCCTA but perhaps a pattern such as (G/C) CCXTA. Likewise, the real codon is not necessarily the first ATG encountered, so a training set of known full-length coding sequences can greatly assist in building a model of what the DNA sequence around the real start codons looks like. Gene features are often referred to by their acronyms in gene prediction systems. It is worthwhile remembering these as they are introduced here. Statistical models may include the features described next, roughly in genomic order.

### 2.3.1 5’ Untranslated Region

The 5’ untranslated region (5’ UTR) is the region between the transcription start site and the translation start codon. Many eukaryotic gene predictors can take advantage of user-identified 5’ UTR sequences to train their statistical models. When sufficient aligned mRNAs or mapped short reads are available, and the start codon is known via protein homology, the identification of 5’ UTR can be based on empirical evidence for training purposes. The 5’ UTR region contains several translation signals, as detailed next. The identification of transcription-factor binding sites is a complex issue. In the general transcription-factor binding sites locations are not often used to assist in the prediction of gene structures. Their discovery and potential usage is discussed later in Chapter 3.

### 2.3.2 –35 Signal

The –35 signal is located approximately 35 base pairs upstream of the start codon in prokaryotes. It can be used to aid identification of the proper binding site for the initiation complex during transcription. This signal has a consensus sequence of TTGACA (Rosenberg and Court, 1979), but is relatively weak compared to the other prokaryotic UTR signals, therefore multiple candidates often exist for a single gene.

### 2.3.3 B Recognition Element

The B recognition element (BRE) is found in many archaeal and eukaryotic genes (Qureshi and Jackson, 1998), just upstream of the TATA box, and consists of seven nucleotides. The first two nucleotides of the BRE sequence are either G or C, followed by either G or A. The last four nucleotides are CGCC. Transcription factor IIB (TIIB) binds in the major groove of the DNA at the BRE.

### 2.3.4 TATA Box

In prokaryotes, the TATA box is known as the Pribnow box, or the -10 signal, because it is usually centered 10 bases upstream of the start codon. Although there is TATAAT consensus (Pribnow, 1975), it is not universal. More important is that the region is rich in adenine and threonine bonds. Together with the -35 signal, these patterns provide suitability for transcription initiation. It is common to observe multiple candidate start initiation sites, due to the short length and variability of the -10 and -35 signals, therefore the predicted patterns and their putative locations are mostly used to support rather than define the start of the coding sequence.

In eukaryotes, the TATA box is only one of possible initiation factors. Core promoters, such as the initiator element (Inr), the GC box, or the CAAT box can replace or augment the eukaryotic TATA box. Other initiation elements specific to particular genes and tissue types are not generally used for statistical prediction of genes due to their rarity.

### 2.3.5 Ribosomal Binding Site

The Shine–Dalgarno sequence (Shine and Dalgarno, 1975) represents the ribosomal binding site (RBS) in prokaryotes and hence the start of translation. It is usually situated within eight pairs of the start codon. It is specific, determined by the reverse complement of the 16S RNA's 3' end, and may contain mismatches (Schurr et al., 1993). Not every gene necessarily starts with an RBS, but each transcript should. This means that scores for genes in the middle of an operon (also known as polycistronic transcripts) should not be penalized for having one.

In eukaryotes, the ribosomal binding site is referred to as the Kozak sequence (Kozak, 1986). This site does not have as strong a consensus as in prokaryotes (is not derived from the 18S rRNA), and it overlaps rather than precedes the start codon. Because of this overlap, the start codon begins at +1, and the immediate 5' base position is -1. The consensus sequence varies from species to species; therefore proper detection requires a training data set.

### 2.3.6 Start Codon

In eukaryotes, this sequence is almost universally ATG. In prokaryotes, ATG is in most instances the dominant start codon (approximately 80%), but GTG and TTG can also be used in descending order of frequency. Although there are exceptions or substitutions to these start codons in some species, they are so infrequent that no gene prediction system considers them a priori. Evidence for alternative start codons usually comes from mass spectrometry data or de novo sequencing and has to be entered manually when the genome analysis pipeline is being configured.

### 2.3.7 Protein Coding Sequencing

The determination of the protein coding sequence (CDS) is of course at the heart of every gene-prediction system. Because the sequence is constrained by the codon-usage table and the protein to be produced, strong signals exist that can be predictive of CDS regions in a genome. Different feature sets of the CDS are used in the statistical models underlying various prediction programs, but they roughly fall into two categories: sequence base composition and codon usage bias. Sequence base composition in many prokaryotes can be used to predict the coding strand in the genome, where the strand containing greater than 50% A+G is usually the protein-coding instance (to our knowledge only with the exception of retroviruses). Unusual G+C composition can be used to filter genes, which might be horizontally transferred from another species, and transposable elements. None of these sequences will follow the general statistical distributions for the reading sequences in a given species.

For eukaryotes, it is also suggested to separate the coding sequence sets into bins, based on their G+C content. The reason is that sequences in these bins can show subtle differences in their other sequence statistics, leading to systematic underreporting of some CDSs, if not explicitly modeled. This percentage G+C bins (called isochores) can be defined manually or automatically by some programs, such as IsoFinder (Oliver et al., 2004).

Even without comparison to databases of known proteins, it is possible to some degree to predict the coding sequence based on the low occurrence of rare codons. This is particularly useful for the determination of the correct start codon for the CDS, which is species specific.
