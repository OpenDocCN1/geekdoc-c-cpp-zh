Once contiguous sequences representing mRNAs have been assembled, they can be aligned to an already existing reference genome. The intron-exon structure of the genes can be revealed through the use of a double-affine, also known as intron-tolerant, alignment algorithm (Garber et al., 2011). Double-affine searches have a high penalty for small gaps (insertions, deletions, or sequencing errors) and a smaller one for large gaps (introns). This promotes the correct alignment of cDNA sequences.

Aligning across introns can lead to slight misalignments. For example, given alignment of the form:

```
CATCTTGGTACACGCTGCTGGAGAATGCATCCACTAGAAGCAACATCAGGATC
|||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||
CATCTTGGTACACGGCGTGC---------------------------------------
ACATTCAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAGAGAAGAAGAATTAGCAAGAAAGGCGTATGC
|||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||
----------------GCAAGAGGCGTATGC
```

The read could have just as easily aligned with the G at the start of the second exon of the first exon without disturbing the protein translation. This would have maintained the splice acceptor AG that is almost universal in the species being studied. Good software should take preferred splice sites for a species into account in order to provide accurate alignment.

Alternatively, the short reads can be mapped back to the genome using a fast short-read alignment tool. While many alignment tools exist, of particular note are those that are consistently updated and support the popular SAM (Sequence Alignment/Map) file format for read mapping, especially BFAST (Homer et al., 2009), BowTie (Meaghed et al., 2009), and MosaicAligner (http://code.google.com/pmosaik-aligner).
