While most DNA transposons are so-called cut-and-paste elements based on their transposase-mediated relocation mechanism during cell division, two other types of DNA transposons also exist: helitrons and polintons (Jurka et al., 2007). Helitrons are structurally related to bacterial IS97 with a helicase gene and are therefore believed to transpose via the same mechanism, the so-called rolling circle. Helitrons are found in various eukaryotes and are particularly known to be active in plants such as maize, where helitron-derived sequences compose approximately 2% of the genome (Feschotte and Pritham, 2009). Helitrons have a preference for transposing into other helitron copies, near but not within genes. Homology search and structural annotation can help identify these transposable elements.

The third type of DNA transposon, polintons, is found throughout eukaryotes (Kapitonov and Jurka, 2006). Polintons contain all of the genes required for their transposition and are therefore also cis-synthesizing transposons. The set of genes includes a protein-primed DNA polymerase B, a retro intron, a cysteine protease, and an ATPase. This sequence is likely derived from an ancient plasmid, but the unique combination of cis-genes makes it relatively straightforward to detect by using protein-homology methods. Additionally, polinton flanking inverted repeats are typically several hundred nucleotides long, capped 5’ by AG and 3’ by TC.

## 3.4.3 Retransposons

Many transposable elements are mobile based on transcription back to DNA and insertion back into the genome; these are called reverse transcription or retransposons (Jurka et al., 2007). Because this action is copy and paste, rather than DNA cut and paste, the elements tend to proliferate and enlarge the genome. The main distinction between various retransposons is whether they are of retroviral origin.

Retransposons of retroviral origin are typified by the presence of non-protein-coding terminal repeats (LTRs) flanking protein-coding genes. In fully active retroviruses, the 5’ flank consists of four regions: R, U5, PBS, and L. The R region is a short repeat found at both ends of the virus, used as a checkpoint during polymerization. The U5 sequence that follows is unique. This is followed by a primer-binding site (PBS), which is complementary to the 3’ end of a tRNA primer in the viral host. Sometimes the corresponding tRNA name is used in the nomenclature naming the virus. Finally, the 5' leader region (L) contains a signal important for the packaging of the genomic RNA. The viral 3' flank includes a polyuridine tract (PPT; i.e., polyA/G), which primes DNA synthesis during reverse transcription. This is followed by the proviral transcriptional primer U3 and finally R.

The genes of any retrovirus are referred to as gag, pol, and env (Jurka et al., 2007), and are always found in this order. Gag proteins are cleaved, typically by a viral protease, into “mature” peptides forming essential virus particle structures: the matrix, capsid, and nucleocapsid. Pol proteins nucleic acid processing enzymes covering important roles in the life cycle of the virus, such as reverse transcription (polymerase), genome integration (integrase), and RNase H activity. The Env protein encodes the viral envelope protein.

Integrated retroviruses that can be referred to as endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), with the integrated DNA referred to as a provirus (Gifford and Tristem, 2003). Retransposons near genes can affect their transcription. ERVs expression has also been linked to chronic diseases, their annotation is of more than academic interest. Depending on how long the retrovirus has been integrated into the genome, many parts of the original retroviral structure may be missing or mutated too to be easily detectable using a BLAST-based method. LTR_STRUC (McCarthy and McDonald, 2003) can be used to detect retroviral-like elements, which are flanked by long terminal repeats (LTRs). Retrosceptor (Sperber et al., 2007) uses less stringent criteria, therefore false positives must be taken into account. A combination of all three methods may be the best choice to find a balance between sensitivity and specificity. The nonviral Penelope of retrotransposons also contains a distinct LTR that likely reflects its lack of integration and dependence on hostomerase.

Retrotransposons with LTRs are either LINEs or SINEs (Jurka et al., 2007). Long-spersed nuclear elements (LINEs) in eukaryotes over 5 kb or greater genomic regions (Gogvaze and Buzdin, 2009), with a RNA polymerase 2 binding site 5' of a high-specificity reverse transcription gene. Inactivated LINEs are often truncated at the 5' end. The LINE may also contain an endonuclease with site-specific integration activity. The 3' end contains a polyadenylation signal (AATAAA), usually followed by a G/T-rich region. There are three families of LINEs, based on their origin: in humans, only LINE1 elements are actively transposable, with LINE2 and CR1/LINE3 elements mostly degraded (Gogvaze and Buzdin, 2009).

Short-interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) are typically up to 500 bases long (Gogvadze and Buzdin, 2009), and are lineage specific. They do not have a protein-coding payload. The most famous of these are primate-specific Alu SINE, which comprises over 10% of human genome. This SINE is derived from 7SL RNA, and most SINEs are a source of recombination events in evolution. Because SINE elements are lineage specific, homology search against databases of known SINEs is important. REPBASE (Jurka et al., 2005) is a database with the largest source of repetitive elements, submitted by researchers and derived from the literature. RepeatMasker (www.repeatmasker.org) allows one to use various alignment methods to match such sequence. In either case, sensitivity thresholds play an important role in determining how sensitive masking will be, especially when the organism masked is distantly related to the well-characterized species from which REPBASE entries are derived.

## 3.5 OTHER REPEATS

The task of finding approximate substrings of length n or longer, with less than x% mismatch is computationally expensive on genome-wide scale. EulerAlign (Zhang and Waterman, 2005) uses data structure known as de Bruijn graph to make the task approximately linear to the sequence length. REPeter (Kurt et al., 2001) is able to handle degeneracy in repeat with runtime increasing significantly on the number of errors. RepeatModel, available from RepeatMasker site (www.repeatmasker.org), uses two different methods of prediction and subsequently combines the results. In general, each method has strengths and weaknesses based on the most important factor (e.g., sensitivity, accuracy). Another popular tool for de novo repeat detection is PILER (Edgar and Myers, 2005), which uses filtering method to annotate four types of repeats: dispersed, tandem array, pseudosellite, and LTRs. Dispersed families have three or more copies throughout the genome, flanked by unique sequences in each case. Although these are typically mobile elements, they may also be from paralogous genes. A tandem array is a contiguous series of three or more copies of a motif within a chosen distance. A pseudosellite has copies, but the copies do not meet the distance threshold for tandem repeats. Short repeats of 1 to 6 base motifs (also known as microsatellites) are most sensitively detected by dedicated software such as SciSeq (Kofler et al., 2007). Overall diversity of repetitive elements is reflected in the fact that there are 75 “repeat” related terms in the Sequence Ontology. TEclass can help annotate the de novo repeat at the top level of DNA transposon/SINE/LINE/LTR. REPBASE provides a rough classification system for known repeats as well. Beyond these high-level annotations, more refined classification of repetitive elements is not well automated.
