# First Steps With The GPIO

The most basic task when working with the micro:bit is controlling the I/O lines. This isn't difficult if you use the framework provided, but there some subtle points to watch out for.

The microbit has at least 32 General Purpose I/O (GPIO) lines, although not all of them are available for use. Finding out how to use them is an important first step and you have to master both the hardware and the software aspects.

Warning: Despite the ease of making connections to the micro:bit using hoc cables and clips, the GPIO lines are very low power and sensitive to voltages and currents outside their normal range. Do not connect anything to the micro:bit, especially for output, without considering the voltage and current applied to the GPIO line. The voltage should be 3.3V or less and the current should be 0.5mA or less.

# Pinouts

The big irritation of working with any electronic device that has GPIO lines is what you call them. The manufacturer will give them names like GPIO 01, GPIO 02 and so on, but when the processor is used in a finished product like the microbit these lines are brought out to physical pins, connections, on the printed circuit board. These are often called Pin01, Pin02 and so on and there is no need for Pin01 to correspond to GPIO 0. Indeed, in most cases pin numbers and GPIO numbers are fairly independent. Why does this matter? The main reason is the low-level documentation provided by the chip manufacturer generally uses GPIO numbers and the documentation provided by the device manufacturer generally uses pin numbers. You can see the potential for confusion and mistakes.

The microbit uses a strange system of pin names, connector numbers, PCB connector and what are printed on the edge connector and these correspond to different GPIO numbers. They are also extended to include the GPIO lines used to control on-board peripherals. For example, P0 is the first “pad” on all micro:bits, but it corresponds to GPIO line 3 in the V1 and line 2 in the V2. It is also pin 18 on the physical edge connector. On the schematic diagram, the same pin is labeled PAD1 just to increase the confusion.
