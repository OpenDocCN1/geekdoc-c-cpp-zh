## 4.9.9 Index at Multipoles and Other Singularities

Changing the sign of (4.505a) also changes the sign of the forcing vector (4.505b) and adds $\pi$ its angle (4.505c): the addition of a constant (4.505d) does not change the angle of (4.505e) nor the index (4.505f):

```
$t - t_0, \ \{u, v\} \to \{-u, -v\}, \quad \Theta \to \Theta + \pi; \quad t \to t + t_0; \quad [\Theta] \to [\Theta], \quad I \to I.$
```

Thus the index cannot distinguish between a source, a sink, from a saddle or equilibrium. Some typical values (problem 155) of the index: (i-iv) a $2^N$-multipole has index $I=N+1$, for example $I=3$ for a quadrupole $N=2$ (Figure 4.3a), $I=2$ for a dipole $N=1$ (Figure 4.3b) and $I=1$ for a monopole $N=0$, such as $I=1$ for a source/sink (Figure 4.3c) and vortex (Figure 4.3d); (w-v) the latter corresponds to a saddle and inflection (Figure 4.3f) the spiral (Figure 4.3g) has unity index $I=1$; (viii) the index zero $I=0$ is a simple point (Figure 4.3h); and (ix) a saddle point (Figure 4.3i) has negative index $I=-1$. The following statements can be established as follows:

- (i) an ordinary point (Figure 4.3h) involves no change of direction hence index $I=0$;
- (ii) going around the saddle point has minus unity $I=-1$;
- (iii) the index plus $I=1$ corresponds to some complete rotation for the spiral (Figure 4.3g), center vortex (Figure 4.3d), inflection (Figure 4.3f) or tangent (Figure 4.3e), and source (Figure 4.3c);
- (iv) the dipole (Figure 4.3b) involves two rotations hence index $I=2$;
- (v) the quadrupole (Figure 4.3a) has $I=3$.

## 4.9.10 Location and Identification of Singularities and Stability

The theorem of addition of indices with (4.492a-d) or with (4.504b) the point at infinity can be used (problem 156) to locate and identify singularities and points of equilibria by determining the index of several loops. As an example, suppose that there is a saddle point in the finite part of the plane (4.50a) and a focus at infinity (4.507b). If there is one more singularity in the finite part of the plane its index be (4.507c), then it could be a dipole (Figure 4.34b), but could not be a quadrupole, center, focus, tangent, or saddle:

```
$I_1=-1,\tilde{I}_1=1; \quad I_2=2-\tilde{I}_2=2; \quad I_3+I_2=2; \quad (4.50a-d)$
```

if there were two possibilities:

- (i) two centers, spiral, foci, tangents, or inflections $I_2=1=I_1$;
- (ii) a quadrupole $I_2=3$ and saddle $I_3=-1$.

Returning to the case of two known singularities and one unknown, if $I_1+\tilde{I}_1\neq 1$ then $I_1\neq 1$ and the remaining point must be unstable or stable.

The sum of all indices including the point at infinity is zero. This result can be used to obtain compatible sets of singularities or to find singularities in an incomplete set. The singularities include:

- (i–iii) a multipole of order $n$, or $2n$-pole, that has index $I=n+1$, for example $I=1$ for $n=0$ monopole or source/sink (Figure 4.1a), $I=2$ for $n=1$ dipole (Figure 4.3b), and $I=3$ for a quadrupole (Figure 4.3a);
- (iv–vi) the inflection (f) tangent (e) (Figure 4.1c) has index $I=1$, as the center (d) that corresponds to (Figure 4.1g) to a vortex and monopole (viii);
- the spiral point (f) has (Figure 4.1f) index $I=1$ as the center; the simple point (h) has (Figure 4.1d) index $I=0$; the negative index $I=-1$ corresponds to the saddle point (Figure 4.1e).

A rotation of $3\pi/2$ along each of four lobes, corresponding to a total rotation $4\times3\pi/2=6\pi$ with index $I=3$. Generally, for a $2^N$ multipole of order $N$:
