## 4. Bifurcations, Iterated Maps, and Chaos

The dynamical systems may be represented by: (i) the trajectory in physical space; (ii) the path in phase space; or (iii) the bifurcations in parameter space (subsection 4.8.1). The parameter space is used to prove the theorem on stability boundaries (subsection 4.8.2) used before connection with non-linear resonance (subsection 4.6). The bifurcations apply both to linear (nonlinear) dynamical systems (subsection(s) 4.8.4 (4.8–4.11)) that include gradient and conservative dynamical systems (subsection 4.8.3). The perturbation of a linear system can lead to non-linear systems, for example the Hopf supercritical or subcritical bifurcation of spiral (subsection 4.8.5). In addition to the previous bifurcations for linear and non-linear oscillators, the van der Pol oscillator (subsection 4.8.7) is a non-linear example of non-separable displacement and velocity terms (subsection 4.8.6); it has a Hopf bifurcation with a hysteresis loop (subsection 4.8.7). Two of the most common or important bifurcations are the fold (cusp) catastrophe [subsection 4.8.8 (4.8.9)] corresponding to a one (two)-parameter value with a cubic (quadratic) term. The nature of the orbits of dynamical systems can be investigated with the help of iterated maps (subsection 4.8.10); can indicate the emergence of chaos (subsection 4.8.11), for example the transition from laminar to turbulent flow (subsection 4.8.12). The transition from laminar to turbulent flow is associated with a loss of lift that can cause an aircraft to stall (subsection 4.8.13); stall is a departure from stable flight that can lead to a spin (subsection 4.8.14); that is, a hazardous falling motion that needs recovery within the altitude to avoid an accident.

### 4.8.1 Physical, Phase, and Parameter Spaces

A second-order dynamical system has several equivalent representations in different spaces, and may include one (more) parameter(s) q. The trajectory in the physical space (x, y) is specified by (4.1a, b) = (4.426a, b) the two components of the velocity of a system of coupled first-order differential equations:

```
$$\frac{dx}{dt}=\dot{x}(x,y;t);\quad \frac{dy}{dt}=\dot{y}(x,y;t);\quad (4.426a, b)$$
```

choosing an initial position (4.426c, d) at time $t_0$ leads to the trajectory (4.426e, f) for all time:

```
$x_0\equiv x(t_0),y_0\equiv y(t_0);\quad x\equiv x(t_0,y_0;q);\quad y\equiv y(t_0,y_0;q);\quad (4.426c–f)$
```

Solving (4.426a) for $y$ leads to (4.427a) and substitution in (4.426b) leads to a single second-order differential equation (4.427b) for the dependent variable x:

```
$y\equiv y(x,\dot{x};t)=\quad F(x,\dot{x};t)=0,\quad (4.427a, b)
```

and likewise for $y$; choosing an initial position (4.427c) and velocity (4.427d) at time $t_0$ leads from (4.427b) to the trajectory (4.427e) for all time:

```
$x_0\equiv x(t_0),\dot{x}_0\equiv\dot{x}(t_0);\quad x\equiv x(t_0,y_0;q);\quad (4.427c–e)$
```

From (4.427e) follows the trajectory (4.428a) and eliminating time with (4.427e) leads to the path in the phase plane:

```
$$\dot{x}\equiv\dot{x}(t_0,x_0;q);\quad G(x,x_0;q)=0,\quad (4.428a, b)$$
```

whose coordinates are the position and velocity.

In the case of a force depending only on its position: (i) specifies the acceleration (4.429a), and (ii) derives from a potential (4.429b) specified within an added constant (4.429c):


The equilibrium position(s) correspond to zero force (4.430a) [stationary position (4.430b)] and (i) the force has negative (positive) gradient (4.430c); and (ii) the potential is minimum (maximum) (4.430f):

```

```
