# # 4
 Unsteady, Non-Linear, and Chaotic Systems

The basic linear second-order system with coefficients independent of time is the damped or amplified harmonic oscillator and forced attractor or repeller (chapter 2) that can be generalized in three directions. The first generalization is to consider all singularities of a linear system of two first-order differential equations with constant coefficients as regards the trajectory in the physical plane (section 4.2) and the paths in the phase plane (section 4.1); the corresponding singularities are the points that are not (i) ordinary, are the (ii) contact, (iii) nodal, (iv) focal, (v) saddle, and (vi) asymptotic or spiral points, plus the (vii) center. A second generalization is to allow the coefficients of the linear second-order system to depend on time; for example, in the case of a harmonic oscillator with vibrating support (section 4.3) the energy input into the oscillator with support causes a parametric resonance (section 4.3) that is distinct from ordinary resonance (sections 2.7–28). Parametric resonance occurs only for frequencies close to the first harmonic of the natural frequency, and also more weakly for its sub-multiples, and it leads to exponential rather than linear growth of amplitude with time, in the absence of damping. The effect of damping on the boundary while the amplitude still grows exponentially with time; in ordinary resonance there is no threshold amplitude and the oscillations have constant amplitude.

The third generalization considers a non-linear second-order system with constant coefficients, corresponding to non-linear resonance and friction forces (sections 4.4–4.6). An example is a soft or hard spring connecting to a non-linear restoring force (section 4.4) leading to elliptic integrals; the nonlinear resonance generates free harmonics, which causes oscillations multiples of the fundamental frequency (section 4.5). The forcing of a non-linear oscillator can lead to non-linear resonance with two distinct amplitudes, amplitude jumps and hysteresis loops extracting or feeding energy (section 4.6). The non-linear damping or amplification lead respectively to decay or growth of fields; an example of the latter is the self-excited dynamo (section 4.7) that generates the magnetic field of rotating bodies like the earth and other planets and stars. The preceding examples include cases of bifurcations; that is, the existence of multiple solutions of a differential equation beyond a bifurcation value of a parameter (section 4.8); a large number of successive bifurcations can lead to a “chaotic” state, in the sense that it is strongly affected by small changes of initial conditions.
