## 4.3.1 Oscillator with Variable Mass and Resilience

Considering an undamped linear oscillator (Figure 4.4) the equation of motion balances: (i) the restoring force (4.84a) is a linear function of the displacement that may be a function of time; and (ii) the inertia force (4.84a) equal to the time derivative of the linear momentum (4.84b) that equals the product of the velocity by the mass that may be a function of time:

![](img/6a4cb3b1429197bb64251c133_0.png)
FIGURE 4.4

The parametric resonance occurs for a linear system whose coefficients are periodic functions of time, for a mass suspended from a spring whose support oscillates at an excitation frequency $\omega$, that may differ from the natural frequency $\omega_0$. In the case of the harmonic oscillator, the parametric resonance occurs for frequencies that are submultiple of the first harmonic and is modified by damping (Figure 4.5).

Introducing the modified time (4.85a) $\equiv$ (4.85b) $\equiv$ (4.85c):

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{1}{m(t)}, \quad T = \int \frac{dt}{m(t)}, \quad X(T) = x(t)$$

the equation of motion (4.84d; 4.85d) takes the form:

$$-k(t)X(T) = \frac{dT}{dt} \frac{dX}{dT} = \frac{1}{m(t)} \frac{d^2X}{dT^2}$$

Thus an undamped linear oscillation with variable mass and spring (4.84d) satisfies (problem 90) a pseudo-harmonic oscillator equation (4.85a) $\equiv$ (4.85b):

$$\omega(T) = \sqrt{k(t)m(t)}, \quad \left[\frac{d^2}{dT^2} + \omega(T)^2\right]X(T) = 0$$

involving the modified time (4.85a) $\equiv$ (4.85b); the pseudo-natural frequency (4.86a) does not reduce to the natural frequency (2.54a) because the mass also appears in the modified time.

In the case of constant mass and spring the natural frequency if the support of the spring (Figure 4.4) performs oscillations with amplitude $2h$ and excitation frequency $\omega_s$, this is equivalent to a resonance of the spring varying in time (4.87):

$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k_0}{m}}, \quad k(t) = k_0[1+2h\cos(\omega_s t)]$$

and leads (problem 91) to the equation of motion (4.84d) in the original time:

$$\ddot{x} + \omega_0^2[1+2h\cos(\omega t)]x = 0$$

The latter corresponds to Mathieu equation (1873) (4.89):

$$\frac{d^2w}{d\theta^2} + [a-2q\cos(2\theta)]w = 0$$

with: (i) independent variable (4.90a); (ii) dependent variable (4.90d); and (iii) parameters (4.90b, c):

$$\theta = \frac{\omega t}{2}, \quad a = \left(\frac{2\omega_0}{\omega}\right)^2, \quad q = -h\left(\frac{2\omega_0}{\omega}\right)^2, \quad w(\theta) \equiv x(t)$$

The general properties of linear differential equations of second order with periodic coefficients (subsections 4.3.3–4.3.6) are used to solve (subsections 4.3.7–4.3.12) the Mathieu equation (4.89) exactly; these are illustrated by approximate solutions of the harmonic oscillator with vibrating support (4.88) without (with) damping [subsections 4.3.13–4.3.15 (4.3.16–4.3.18)]. An example is a pendulum with support oscillating vertically (subsections 4.3.19–4.3.20).

## 4.3.3 Linear Differential Equation with Periodic Coefficients (Floquet 1883)

The Mathieu equation (4.89) is a particular case of the unforced linear differential equation (1.34b; 1.36c) $\equiv$ (4.91b) with periodic coefficients

$$A_n(t+\tau) = A_n(t): \quad \sum_{n=0}^N A_n(t)\frac{d^nx}{dt^n} = 0$$

considered next (in the section 9.3) for order two (order N). In the second-order case:

$$N=2: \quad A_2(t)\ddot{x}(t) + A_1(t)\dot{x}(t) + A_0(t)x(t) = 0$$

the general integral is a linear combination (1.36b) $\equiv$ (4.92d) of linearly independent particular integrals with the non-zero Wronskian (1.40c) $\equiv$ (4.92c):

$$W(x_1, x_2) = x_1\dot{x}_2 - x_2\dot{x}_1 \neq 0$$

where $x(t) = C_1x_1(t) + C_2x_2(t)$, and $C_1, C_2$ are arbitrary constants. The differential equation (4.92b) is unchanged by the substitution $t \to t+\tau$ and $x_1, x_2$ are also solutions; since a second-order differential equation can have only two linearly independent solutions, it must be that $x_1, x_2$ are linearly independent.
