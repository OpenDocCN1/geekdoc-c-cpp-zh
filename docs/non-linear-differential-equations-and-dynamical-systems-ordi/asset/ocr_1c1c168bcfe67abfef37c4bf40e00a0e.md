A non-linear restoring force (Figure 4.7) associated with a potential, (a) in the absence of damping and forcing, leads (b) either to closed limit cycles or open paths. For example, (Figure 4.8a) the hard spring (b) has limit cycles (c), where the soft spring (d) has limit cycles (open) paths (outside) (e) separatrices. The soft or hard spring (Figure 4.8) corresponds to a term in the non-linear restoring force, and to a cubic term in the potential, restoring the equilibrium at the origin (Figure 4.9b); a quadratic term in the non-linear resonance and the inflection at the origin shifts the average position towards the lower potential. For a damped (Figure 4.10) non-linear oscillator forced to the natural frequency or half of it, there is non-linear resonance at the natural frequency with amplitude increasing upwards as the amplitude of forcing increases (a-c); if the forcing amplitude exceeds a threshold there are amplitude jumps and a hysteresis loop (d). In the case of forcing at the first (second) harmonic [Figure 4.14] the non-linear resonance at the natural frequency may have amplitude jumps (thresholds) as well as ranges of suppression. The cycle (Figure 4.10d) described in opposite directions extracts (or provides) oscillation. An example of a cycle with growing oscillation is the “flutter” of a control surface of an aircraft (Figure 4.11a) subject to non-linear aerodynamic forces. The oscillation grows until the control surface hits the bumps stops with control, structural damage, or both. The flutter may be avoided by reducing aerodynamic forces by flying at lower speed, thereby reducing (dotted line) the flight envelope (solid line) of the aircraft in terms of acceptable combinations of velocity $v$ and altitude $z$. The “breaking wave” sequence (Figure 4.10a-d) of non-linear resonance is analogous to water waves (Figure 4.12); (a) in the deep ocean the waves are linear because their amplitude is small with the depth; (b) when approaching a slope the amplitude is no longer small compared with depth, and the waves steep and break. Another example of non-linear is (Figure 4.13) a sound wave of large amplitude in a compressible medium: the wave is larger (smaller) in the compression (rarefactions) phase; by thus the compression fronts relative to the lagging rarefaction tail until a shock wave forms.
