# FIGURE 4.14

In the non-linear resonance of an anharmonic oscillator with a potential that is a quartic function of position with forcing at the first harmonic; that is, applied frequency equal to twice the natural frequency, there: (i) is one zero amplitude in the lower frequency range ε < −ε₀; (ii) three amplitudes of which only the highest and lowest (that is zero) are stable in the higher frequency range ε > ε₀; and (iii) two amplitudes of which only the higher is stable in the intermediate frequency range −ε₀ < ε < ε₀. This leads to three situations: (i) starting in the lower frequency range ε < −ε₀, there is no oscillation AB until ε = −ε₀ and then the amplitude increases with frequency BEF; (ii) starting with non-zero amplitude at F in the high-frequency range, the reverse of (i) happens with the amplitude reducing FEB until the oscillations are suppressed AB at ε = −ε₀; and (iii) starting with zero amplitude at D in the high-frequency range ε > ε₀, the oscillation starts at ε = ε₀ with an amplitude jump CE, then the amplitude decreases EB until the oscillation stops again AB at ε = −ε₀.

Thus a linearly damped anharmonic oscillator (4.303) forced at an applied frequency (4.320a) close (4.302a) to the first harmonic of the natural frequency, has (problem 119) non-linear resonance at the natural frequency (Figure 4.14) with: (i) oscillation always in the range (−ε₀, +ε₀) in (4.327c); (ii) suppression of oscillations always at lower frequencies ε < −ε₀; and (iii) at higher frequencies the oscillations are suppressed if coming from above ε > ε₀, until ε = ε₀ when there is an amplitude jump (4.328c), joining the oscillations whose amplitude grows smoothly if coming from below. This corresponds to the Hopf bifurcation (subsection 4.8.5).
