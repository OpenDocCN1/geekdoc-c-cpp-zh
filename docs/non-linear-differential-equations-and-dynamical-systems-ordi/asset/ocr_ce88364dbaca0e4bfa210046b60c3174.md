# Differentials and First-Order Equations

NOTE 3.20: Exception for a Homogeneous First-Order Differential of
Degree -1

The first-order differential:

```
0=(3x^2+y^2)dx+2xydy=d(x^3+xy^2)=dΦ, (3.349)
```

is homogeneous of degree two and exact; it can be checked that it satisfies the
integrability condition 3.350a:

```
∂dy(3x^2+y^2)=2y=∂x(2xy), x(3x^2+y^2)+2xy^2=3Φ=C, (3.350a, b)
```

and that the general integral is: homogeneous first-order
differential (3.351a):

```
0=dx/x-dy/x=-d(y/x), y=Cx, (3.351a, b)
```

has a general integral (3.351b); it does not coincide with 3.352a, b:

```
{X,Y}={y/x,-1/x}, C=xX+yY=0, (3.352a, b)
```

because the reason of 3.351a is q=-1, and the theorem (3.346b, c) fails.

# NOTE 3.21 Higher-Orders, Sub-Spaces, and Tensors

The differentials may be generalized from the first order (sections 3.8–3.9
and notes 3.1–3.20) to higher orders (note 3.21) with associated invariance
properties (note 3.22). A method of solution of higher-order differentials is the
factorization into lower-orders (note 3.23), for example a second-order
differentials may be factorized into two first-order differentials using a
difference of squares (note 3.24). The simplest second-order differential in two
variables (standard XIX) is:

```
0=A(x,y)(dx)^2+B(x,y)(dy)^2+D(x,y)dxddy, (3.353)
```

that may be solved as a quadratic equation for the slope:

```
0=B(x,y)y'^2+D(x,y)y'+A(x,y), (3.354)
```
