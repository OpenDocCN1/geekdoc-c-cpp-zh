In the case of an attractor (4.487a), both eigenvalues have positive (4.487b) negative (4.487c) real parts for amplification (damping) leading to instability (stability):

$$a > 0: \quad Re(\lambda_{\pm}) = \begin{cases}>0 & \text{if } b > 0,\<0 & \text{if } b < 0.\end{cases} \quad (4.487a–c)$$

The result (4.487b) [4.487c] follows from (4.488a–c) [4.488d–f]:

$$b > 0: \quad Re(\lambda_{\pm}) = \begin{cases}Re[b \pm i\sqrt{4a-b^2}]>0 & \text{if } b^2 < 4a,\sqrt{b^2-4a} > 0 & \text{if } b^2 > 4a;\end{cases} \quad (4.488a–c)$$

$$b < 0: \quad Re(\lambda_{\pm}) = \begin{cases}Re[b \pm i\sqrt{4a-b^2}]<0 & \text{if } b^2 < 4a,\sqrt{b^2-4a} < 0 & \text{if } b^2 > 4a.\end{cases} \quad (4.488d–f)$$

Thus, a non-linear second-order differential equation (4.488b) specified (problem 151) by a continuously differentiable function is:

- (i) always unstable (4.486b, c) for a repeller;
- (ii) is stable (4.487b) [4.487c] for an attractor (4.487a) in the case of damping.

### 4.9.5 Index of a Singularity or Equilibrium Point

Consider an isolated equilibrium point (4.489a) and draw a circle (4.489b, c) around it (Figure 4.33) not containing any other singularity or equilibrium point:

$$z_0 = x_0 + iy_0, \quad z = x + iy; \quad z - z_0 = re^{i\theta}; \quad (4.489a–c)$$

on each point of the circle (4.489c) draw the tangent vector (4.490b) to the integrable curve (4.490):

$${\dot{x},\dot{y}}=u,v(x,y) \in C^1(\mathbb{R}^2); \quad u+iv=we^{i\theta}. \quad (4.490a, b)$$

### 4.9.6 Theorems of the Index and Winding Number

If there are several singularities or equilibrium points in a domain, their indices add to each other, as stated more precisely in the **theorem of addition of indices**: the system (4.492a) has a continuously differentiable vector that does not vanish (4.492c) on a closed curve $\partial D$, not in its interior, except (4.492b) at a finite set of points $z_n$;

then (problem 152) the sum of the indices of each point equals (4.492a) the index of the domain, is $1/2\pi$ the variation of the angle of the vector tangent to the integral curves around the boundary of the domain. The domain is divided by a curve joining the two boundaries leaving one singularity on each side. The index of each singularity is (4.493a, b):

$$2\pi I_1=\int_{ADB} d\theta; \quad 2\pi I_2=\int_{ABCA} d\theta; \quad 2\pi(I_1+I_2)=\int_{ADB} d\theta+\int_{BA} d\theta+\int_{AB} d\theta, \quad (4.493a-c)$$

and their sum is (4.493c). The integrals in AB in opposite directions cancel:

$$\int_{AB} d\theta=-\int_{BA} d\theta; \quad I_1+I_2=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{ADCA} d\theta=\int_{AD} d\theta=1, \quad (4.494a, b)$$

and the remaining two in (4.494) add to the integral (4.494b) around the boundary. In particular if the boundary is a closed curve, the index is at least one, because there is at least one singularity or equilibrium point inside. The preceding theorems can be extended to the index (subsections 4.9.8) at the singularity at infinity (subsections 4.9.7).

### 4.9.7 Singularity at Infinity and Inversion/Stereographic Projection

The singularity at infinity can be obtained via the stereographic projection (sections I.2–I.3) by inversion (4.495a) of a complex (4.489b) (4.495b) relative to the origin (4.495a), implying (4.495d–g):

$$\bar{z} = \frac{1}{z}, \quad z = x+iy=re^{i\phi}, \quad \bar{z} = x-iy=re^{-i\phi} \quad (4.495a–c)$$

$$\bar{x}+iy = \frac{1}{x+iy} \frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2} = \frac{x-iy}{r^2}, \quad x+iy = \frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{r^2} \quad (4.495d, e)$$

$$r=|z|=\|\bar{z}|^{-1}=\bar{r}^{-1}, \quad \bar{\phi} = \arg(\bar{z})=-\arg(z)=-\phi. \quad (4.495f, g)$$

This leads to the direct (4.496a) inverse (4.497b) coordinate transformations (4.496c, d):

$${\bar{x},\bar{y}\} = r^{-2}\{x,-y\}, \quad \{x,y\} = r^{-2}\{\bar{x},\bar{y}\}, \quad r\bar{r}=1, \quad \phi+\bar{\phi}=0, \quad (4.496a–d)$$

and the corresponding direct (4.497a) inverse (4.497b) transformation matrices:

$$\frac{\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y})}{\partial(x,y)} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial\bar{x}}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial\bar{x}}{\partial y} \\ \frac{\partial\bar{y}}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial\bar{y}}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix} = r^{-4} \begin{bmatrix} y^2-x^2 & -2xy \\ 2xy & x^2-y^2 \end{bmatrix} \quad (4.497a)$$

$$\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y})} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial x}{\partial\bar{x}} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial\bar{y}} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial\bar{x}} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial\bar{y}} \end{bmatrix} = r^{-4} \begin{bmatrix} y^2-x^2 & -2xy \\ 2xy & x^2-y^2 \end{bmatrix} \quad (4.497b)$$

for example:

$$\frac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2+y^2)^{1/2} = x|x^2+y^2|^{-1/2} = \frac{x}{r}, \quad (4.497c)$$

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{2x}{r} = \frac{r^2-2x^2}{r^4}, \quad (4.497d)$$

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial y} = -\frac{2x}{r^3}\frac{2y}{r} = -\frac{2xy}{r^4}. \quad (4.497e)$$
