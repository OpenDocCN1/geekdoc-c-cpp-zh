$$\omega_a = \frac{\Omega}{2} \dot{x}_1 + \omega_0^2 x_1 = f \cos(\omega_a t), x_1(t) = \frac{4f_a}{\omega_0} \cos\left(\frac{\Omega_0 + \varepsilon}{2}t\right). (4.316a–c)$$

### 4.6.5 Flutter, Wave Breaking, and Shock Formation

The examples of divergent or unstable hysteresis loops include the flutter (Figure 4.11a) of the control surfaces of an airplane with aerodynamic forcing. At large angles of attack or slip from equilibrium, the large aerodynamic forces require large deflections of the control surfaces. If the hysteresis loop is a limit cycle with energy input the amplitude of the oscillations increases; eventually the control reaches saturation; then, possible deflections hit the stops. The violent oscillations and repeated collisions with stops lead to breakup. Flutter can be avoided, ensuring that the force does not exceed the limit (4.312d), a smaller aerodynamic force implying the velocity below a faster speed. Thus, an airplane may have a flutter envelope that is smaller than its flight envelope (Figure 4.11b), that is, speeds and altitudes at which it could fly if there is no risk of flutter. The more desirable situation is the inverse, a flutter envelope larger than the flight envelope.

The flight envelope (Figure 4.11b) of an aircraft in terms of airspeed versus altitude has typically four boundaries:
(i) stall due to lack of lift at low speed (AB);
(ii) maximum altitude (BC) for zero climb rate;
(iii) maximum speed at high altitude (CD) to drag or shock waves; and (iv) maximum speed at low altitude (DE) due to high air density and dynamic pressure. Flutter may reduce the flight envelope by limiting the maximum speed ($C'D'E'$) below the aerodynamic and propulsive capabilities of the aircraft ($CDE$).
