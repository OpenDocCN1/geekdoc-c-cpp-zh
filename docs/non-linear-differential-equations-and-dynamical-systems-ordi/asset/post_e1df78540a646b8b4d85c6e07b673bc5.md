
# Non-Linear Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems

The self-excited dynamo (Figure 4.16) in co(counter)rotation is a diverging (decaying) electric current, whereas a (Figure 4.17) self-excited electromechanical dynamo has a velocity tending to a finite non-zero (zero) value asymptotically for long time. The finite asymptotic solution or non-zero steady state occurs in the presence of: (i) linear amplification that prevents decay for small amplitude; (ii) quadratic damping that prevents decay for large amplitude. The result is a steady state $v_{*}$ at an intermediate amplitude (Figure 4.17). In the case of linear and quadratic damping, decay to $v_{*}$ is inevitable. The asymptotic non-zero steady state $v_{*}$ (Figure 4.17) of an electromechanical dynamo (Figure 4.16) may be analogous to magnetic field generation in planets and stars due to the combination of ionized fluids containing positive and negative electric charges. An example is the magnetic fields of the earth and their interaction (Figure 4.18) in the solar-terrestrial system. A different example of non-linear damping (Figure 4.20) concerns the quadratic damping (a) of a harmonic oscillator (b). Case of quadratic damping and large amplitude motion (Figure 4.21) is the circulating pendulum (Figure 4.19d), passing several through the highest (Figure 4.19) and lowest (Figure 4.9b) positions, until it oscillates (Figure 4.9a) and finally comes to rest at the lowest position of equilibrium. In the absence of damping there are possible cases of motion (Figure 4.19) of a circular pendulum (Table 4.5): (i) oscillation around the lowest point (b) if it cannot reach highest point (c); circulation or revolution (d) if it passes through the highest point (c) with non-zero velocity; or (iii) the intermediate case between (i) and (ii), which stays at the highest point (c).
