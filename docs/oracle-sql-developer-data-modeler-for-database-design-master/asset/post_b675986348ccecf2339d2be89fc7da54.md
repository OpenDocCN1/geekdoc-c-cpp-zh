
## Physical database design (or just physical design)

The process, or the result of the process, of deciding, given some logical design, how that design should map to whatever physical constructs the target DBMS happens to support. Observe, therefore, that physical design should be derived from the logical design and not the other around; ideally, in fact, it should be derived automatically.

For the remainder of this brief discussion, I'll concentrate on logical design specifically. The first thing I want to say is that there exists some science that can help with the logical design process; I refer, of course, to matters such as the principles of further normalization and the principle of orthogonal design. Thus, if you're a designer, you owe it to yourself—as well as to your clients, which is to say the people who are going to live with the databases you design—to be thoroughly familiar with those principles and to know how and when to apply them. (As an aside, note that there's quite a bit of making that many people seem to realize. It's certainly not a matter of making sure the tables are all in third norm form. However, this is not the place to go into details.)

The second thing I want to say is that although the science is important, there are, sadly, numerous aspects of design that the science doesn't address at all. And where practical experience comes in. If you have a lot of personal experience in the design field, good for you—you've learned (possibly around!) what works and what doesnnt. If you don't have much experience of your own to fall back on (and even if you do), then you'll need sound advice you can follow, advice from someone who does have experience. A good book on design, by a suitably qualified professional, can help meet that need. A word of caution, though: Books on database technology (as opposed to books on design specifically) might not be what you need here. Such books often describe design concepts but fail to give much guidance on how to apply those concepts to the practical task of design. Caveat lector.

Let me enumerate all these table predicate, business rule, and uncontrolled redundancy. First, the predicate for a given table is simply a reasonably precise, but informal, statement in natural language of what the table is. For example, suppose we have a table called EMP (“employees”), with columns called ENO, ENAME, DNO, and SALARY. Then the predicate for that table might look something like this:

```
The person with employee number ENO is an employee of the company, named ENAME, works in the department with department number DNO, and is paid SALARY.
```

ENO, ENAME, DNO, and SALARY are the parameters to this predicate, and of they correspond to the columns of the table.

Second, a business rule is also a reasonably precise but informal language statement, one that captures some aspect of how the data in the database is supposed to be constrained. (Actually, some writers refer predicates as a special case of business rules); however, there's more to business rules in general than just the table predicates as such.) To start with, there'll be rules that specify what type of information is denoted by the parameters to those table predicates; in the case of employees, for example, there'll be a rule to the effect that the SALARY parameter (“salaries”) denotes money values, expressed in, let's say, euros or U.S. dollars. Then there'll be rules that constrain the values parameters can take for a given employee considered in isolation (for example, a rule that says salaries must be negative and not less than specified upper limit). There'll also be rules that constrain the set of employees taken as a whole, independent of other entities, that might be represented in the same database (for example, a rule to the effect that employee numbers must be unique). Finally, there'll be rules that constrain employees considered with other entities (for example, a rule to the effect that every employee must be assigned to some department, or a rule to the effect that no employee can earn more than the manager of the department in question is assigned to).

Their formal counterpart—i.e., the thing they do which needs to be stated in some formal language and enforced by the DBMSs. Finally, what about matter of uncontrolled redundancy? We can say, loosely, that the database displays redundancy if and only if it "says the same thing twice." And we often say, again loosely, that we don't want the database to display redundancy in this sense. But it would be more accurate to say we don't want to display any uncontrolled redundancy. Uncontrolled redundancy can be a problem, but controlled redundancy shouldn't be. Let me explain. First some more details:

- **Controlled redundancy**: Redundancy in the database is controlled if the user is aware of it, but it is guaranteed never to lead to any inconsistencies.
- **Uncontrolled**: Redundancy in the database is uncontrolled if it has the potential to lead to inconsistencies.
- **Inconsistency**: The database is inconsistent (at least from a formal point of view) if and only there's some integrity constraint it's supposed to satisfy but doesn't.

So if controlled redundancy means no inconsistencies, it must also mean no constraints are violated. Of course, not all constraints have to do with redundancy as such; for example, if the database were to show some employee as having a negative salary; Thus, for example, a constraint to the effect that salaries mustn't be negative doesn't. Thus, salary, it would certainly be inconsistent, but that particular inconsistency wouldn't be one that arises from redundancy. (It would, however, mean the database would be incorrect, in the sense that it didn't faithfully reflect the state of affairs in the real world. Inconsistent implies incorrect, though the converse is false—the database can be incorrect without being inconsistent. For example, if I showed some employee as earning a salary different from employee's true salary, it would be incorrect but not inconsistent.)

To say again, constraints don't always have to do with redundancy. But redundancy always has to do with constraints. For example, suppose—very unrealistic?—that a constraint to the effect that all employees in the same department must earn the same salary. Suppose further that the database shows Heli and Chris are in the same department. If it were also to show Heli and Chris as earning the same salary, it would be redundant; by contrast, if it were to show Heli and Chris as earning different salaries, it would be inconsistent (and incorrect).
