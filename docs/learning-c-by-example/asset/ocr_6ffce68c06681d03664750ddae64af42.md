A FIELD GUIDE TO CREATIVE RECOVERY

Write faster with perfect concentration by breaking up with your muse. Artists who depend on inspiration fail. Learn how to identify your muse, assess the attachment, develop a ritual, and create new habits for sustainable creative work.

| Type | Characteristics | Prescription |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type One: The Romantic Muse | Mood swings, requires inspiration, waxing and waning energy | Use a schedule, not a feeling; work in consistent blocks |
| Type Two: The Monumental Muse | Overwhelming, full of grand ideas, none of which are finished | Cut projects into smaller pieces; finish every day |
| Type Three: The Distracted Muse | Feeds on novelty, social media craving, disrupts workflow | Build a single daily workspace and one list of tasks |
| Type Four: The Competitive Muse | Compares you to others, fuels jealousy and procrastination | Work in isolation; compare only to yesterday's output |
| Type Five: The Critic Muse | Judges your first draft, simplifies nothing, chases perfection | Write ugly first drafts; edit only in separate sessions |

# MUSE TYPE ONE: THE ROMANTIC MUSE

This is the passionate lover of the art. It wants you to wait for the perfect moment, the warm glow, the beautiful sunrise. It gives you sensations and takes away your discipline. The cure: ignore the feeling. Set an alarm. Work begins when the alarm rings, not when the inspiration sings.

In practical terms, schedule your writing for the same time each day. This allows the subconscious to be ready. Lean into the boring ritual of showing up. Over time, routine becomes charismatic and the muse loses its power.
