
with

\(\tilde{v}_j^n(x_1, \cdots, x_j) = v_j(x_j), \forall j \le n\)
\(\tilde{v}_j^n(x_1, \cdots, x_j) = v_0, \forall j \ge n+1.\)

We obtain

\(u^n(x) = E^{V^n, x} \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha^{j-1} l(y_j, \tilde{v}_j^n) + \alpha^n E^{V^n, x} w_0(y_{n+1}).\)

Therefore

\(u^n(x) = E^{V^n, x} \sum_{j=1}^\infty \alpha^{j-1} l(y_j, \tilde{v}_j^n)\)

\(u^n(x) = J_x(\tilde{V}^n).\)

Since \(\tilde{V}^n \in \mathcal{V}^n\) the proof of the Lemma is completed.

To finally give the interpretation of the maximum solution we introduce the set

\(\mathcal{V} = \{V|V = (v_1, \cdots, v_i, \cdots) \text{ where }\)
\(v_i = v_i(x_1, \cdots, x_i)\)
\(\alpha^{j-1} E^{V, x} \bar{u}(y_j) \to 0 \text{ as } j \to +\infty \}.\)

We can then state the

### Theorem 4.5. Under the assumptions (4.3.1), (4.3.2), (4.3.6) the maximum solution \(\bar{u}(x)\) of the functional equation (4.3.7) between 0 and \(w_0\) is given by

\(\bar{u}(x) = \inf_{V \in \mathcal{V}} J_x(V)\)

### PROOF. Consider the control policy \(\tilde{V}^n\). We are going to check that it belongs to \(\mathcal{V}\), for any \(n\). We need to check that

\(\alpha^{k-1} E^{V^n, x} \bar{u}(y_k) \to 0 \text{ as } k \to +\infty.\)

We may assume \(k \ge n+1\). Since \(\bar{u}(x) \le w_0(x)\) it is sufficient to check that

\(\alpha^{k-1} E^{V^n, x} w_0(y_k) \to 0 \text{ as } k \to +\infty.\)

However

\(\alpha^{k-1} E^{V^n, x} w_0(y_k) = E^{V^n, x} \sum_{j=k}^\infty \alpha^{j-1} l(y_j, v_0),\)

for \(k \ge n+1\).

But the series

\(E^{V^n, x} \sum_{j=n+1}^\infty \alpha^{j-1} l(y_j, v_0) \le u^n(x) < \infty,\)

and thus is a convergent series. Therefore indeed

\(E^{V^n, x} \sum_{j=k}^\infty \alpha^{j-1} l(y_j, v_0) \to 0 \text{ as } k \to \infty.\)

and the result follows. Next we know that

\(u^n(x) = J_x(\tilde{V}^n).\)

Therefore

\( u^n(x) \ge \inf_{V \in \mathcal{V}} J_x(V) \),

and also

\( \bar{u}(x) \ge \inf_{V \in \mathcal{V}} J_x(V) \).

On the other hand for any control, not necessarily in \(\mathcal{V}\) we can write

\(\bar{u}(x) \le E^{V,x} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha^{j-1} l(y_j, v_j) + \alpha^n E^{V,x} \bar{u}(y_{n+1})\)

\(\le J_x(V) + \alpha^n E^{V,x} \bar{u}(y_{n+1})\).

Now if \( V \in \mathcal{V} \) the second term goes to 0 as \( n \to \infty \). Therefore we can assert

\( \bar{u}(x) \le J_x(V) \forall V \in \mathcal{V} \),

and this completes the proof of the Theorem.

### Remark. We do not claim that the infimum is attained in the right hand side of (4.4.7). Moreover the maximum solution is u.s.c. and not l.s.c.

### 4.5. UNIQUENESS

We consider the problem of uniqueness of solutions of the functional equation (4.3.7), in the interval \((0, w_0)\). A necessary and sufficient condition for uniqueness is that the minimum and the maximum solutions coincide. To prove that the minimum and maximum solutions coincide, it is sufficient to prove that the optimal control \(\bar{V} \in \mathcal{V}\). A different approach is to use contraction properties, when they are available.

### Theorem 4.6. Assume \( l(x, v) \) bounded. Then there exists one and only solution of (4.3.7)

### PROOF. We can use a contraction property. Let us recall that \( B \) denotes the space of bounded functions on \( X = R^d \). We notice that (4.3.7) can be written as a fixed point for the map

\( T : B \to B, z = Tw, \)

with

\( Tw(x) = \inf_{v \in U} [l(x, v) + \alpha \Phi^v(w)], \)

and since \( l \) is bounded we can define \( T \) as a map from \( B \) into \( B \). With \( \alpha < 1 \) it is obvious that \( T \) is a contraction. Hence there exists one and only one fixed point.

### Remark. Since \( w_0 \) is bounded, the uniqueness follows also immediately from the fact that condition (4.4.6) is always satisfied.

This result can be extended in several ways.

### Theorem 4.7. Assume that

and \( U \) is compact. If \( \alpha\theta < 1 \) the solution of (4.3.7) in the space \( B_1 \) of functions with linear growth exists and is unique.

### PROOF. In equation (4.5.2) we have denoted by \(|x|\) the function \(x \to |x|\). We provide \( B_1 \) with an equivalent norm

\( ||u||_1 = \sup_x \frac{|u(x)|}{1 + \sigma|x|}, \)

where \(\sigma\) can be chosen arbitrarily and will be chosen small. For \( v \in U \) we have

\( l(x, v) \le h|x| + l_1 \)

\( \Phi^v(|x|) \le \theta|x| + m \)

\( \alpha \Phi^v(w) \le \alpha[|w|](1 + \sigma\Phi^v(|x|)) \)
\( \le \alpha[|w|](1 + \sigma\theta|x| + \sigma m). \)

Then

\( ||Tw_1 - Tw_2|| \le \alpha|w||| \sup_x \frac{1 + \sigma\theta|x| + \sigma m}{1 + \sigma|x|}. \)

Thanks to the assumptions we have

\( \alpha \sup_x \frac{1 + \sigma\theta|x| + \sigma m}{1 + \sigma|x|} \le \alpha(1 + \sigma m), \)

### Remark. Again it is possible to see the preceding result as a consequence of the fact that the maximum and the minimum solutions coincide. Indeed, take to fix the ideas \(v_0 = 0\). We note first that from the growth assumptions we have

(4.5.3) \quad w_0(x) \le \frac{h|x|}{1 - \alpha\theta} + \frac{l_0}{1 - \alpha} + \frac{\alpha m}{1 - \alpha\theta}.

Take any control policy \(V\). From the definition of \(V\), see (4.4.6), using the fact that \(\bar{u} \le w_0\), it is sufficient to prove that

\( \alpha^{j-1} E^{V,x}[y_j] \to 0 \text{ as } j \to +\infty. \)

\( E^{V,x}[|y_{j+1}| | Y_j] = \Phi^{v_j}[x](y_j) \)
\( \le \theta|y_j| + m. \)

\( E^{V,x}[|y_j|] \le \theta^{j-1}|x| + m \sum_{k=0}^{j-2} \theta^k \)

## INVENTORY CONTROL WITHOUT SET UP COST

In this Chapter, we apply the general results of Chapter 4 to the case of Inventory Control. We will consider situations when shortage is not allowed (No backlog) and when shortage is allowed (Backlog). In the case when shortage is not allowed, the demand which cannot be met is lost, and a penalty is incurred. The situation of set up costs will be considered in Chapter 9.

### 5.1. NO SHORTAGE ALLOWED.

### 5.1.1. STATING OF THE PROBLEM.

We are in the situation of section 4.2.3 of Chapter 4. If \( y_n \) denotes the inventory at time n, we have the evolution

(5.1.1) \( y_{n+1} = (y_n + v_n - D_n)^+ \quad y_1 = x, \)

where \( v_n \) is the control at time n and \( D_n \) is the demand, which is a sequence of independent random variables, with probability density \( f(x) \). We denote by \( F(x) \) the CDF, cumulated distribution function associated to \( f(x) \). The control policy, called \( V \), is defined by a sequence of measurable functions \( v_n(D_1, \cdots D_{n-1}) \). For \( n=1 \), \( v_1 \) is simply a deterministic number. Introducing the \( \sigma \)-algebra \( \mathcal{F}^n = \sigma(D_1, \cdots D_{n-1}) \), \( v_n \) is \( \mathcal{F}^n \) measurable. The operator \( \Phi^v \) is given by

(5.1.2) \( \Phi^v \varphi(x) = E\varphi((x+v-D)^+). \)

The transition probability is given by

(5.1.3) \( \pi(x, v; d\eta) = \bar{F}(x+v)\delta(\eta) + f(x+v-\eta)\mathbb{1}_{\eta \le x+v} d\eta, \)

hence

(5.1.4) \( \Phi^v \phi(x) = \bar{F}(x+v)\phi(0) + \int_0^{x+v} f(x+v-\eta)\phi(\eta)d\eta. \)

The set \( U = [0, \infty) \) is not compact. The image of a test function \( \phi(x, v) \) by the operator \( \Phi^v \) is given by

(5.1.5) \( \Phi^v \phi_0(x) = \phi(0,v)\bar{F}(x+v) + \int_0^{x+v} \phi(x+v-\eta, v)f(\eta)d\eta, \)

and the assumption (4.3.1) is satisfied.

We define the function \( l(x, v) \) as follows

(5.1.6) \( l(x, v) = cv + hx + p \int_{x+v}^{\infty} (\xi - x - v)f(\xi)d\xi. \)

It incorporates an ordering cost (purely a variable cost), a holding cost and a penalty for lost sales. We note the inequalities

(5.1.7) \( cv + hx \le l(x, v) \le cv + hx + p\bar{D}, \)
