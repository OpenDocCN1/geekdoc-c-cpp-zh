
We define next successively \( T_{n,x} \) as follows:

\( T_{0,x} = \tau_x; \quad T_{n+1,x} = T_{n,x} + \tau_{n,x} \)

\( \tau_{n,x} = \inf \{t > 0 | D(T_{n,x} + t) - D(T_{n,x}) \ge Q \} \)

We define \( y_x(t) \) by the relations

\( y_x(t) = x - D(t), t < \tau_x, \)
\( y_x(t) = S - D(t) + \tau(T_{n,x}), T_{n,x} \le t < T_{n+1,x} \)
\( y_x(T_{n,x}) = S; y_x(T_{n,x} - 0) \le s \)

We consider

\( u_\alpha(x) = E \int_0^\infty \exp -\alpha t \phi(y_x(t)) dt. \)

In the above notation, we do not write explicitly \( s, S \). On the other hand we note explicitly \( \alpha \), since we will let \( \alpha \) tend to 0. Note also that

\( u_\alpha(S) = C_\alpha(s, Q) = C_\alpha. \)

In the same way we have obtained the formula for \( C_\alpha \), we can check the following formula

\( u_\alpha(x) = E \int_0^{\tau_x} \exp -\alpha t \phi(x - D(t)) dt + C_\alpha E \exp -\alpha \tau_x \)

From the preceding formula we notice that

\( x \le s \Rightarrow \tau_x = 0 \Rightarrow u_\alpha(x) = C_\alpha. \)

A key point now is to obtain the probability distribution of \( \tau_x \). To simplify a little we will assume \( x, s \) integers. We have

\( P(\tau_x \le t) = P(D(t) \ge x - s) = \exp -\lambda t \sum_{j=x-s}^\infty \frac{(\lambda t)^j}{j!}. \)

This probability distribution has a density given by

\( \frac{d}{dt} P(\tau_x \le t) = \lambda \exp -\lambda t \frac{(\lambda t)^{x-s-1}}{(x-s-1)!}. \)

This has sense whenever \( x - s \ge 1 \) If \( x = s \) then

\( \frac{d}{dt} P(\tau_x \le t) = \lambda \exp -\lambda t, \)

and the density is 0 for \( x < s \).

It is then possible to compute the function \( E \exp -\alpha \tau_x \). We leave as an exercise to show that

\( E \exp -\alpha \tau_x = \left( \frac{\lambda}{\alpha + \lambda} \right)^{x-s}. \)

Next we can write

\( E \int_0^{\tau_x} \exp -\alpha t \phi(x - D(t)) dt = E \int_0^\infty \exp -\alpha t \mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_x > t\}} \phi(x - D(t)) dt \)

\( E \int_0^{\tau_x} \exp -\alpha t \phi(x - D(t)) dt = E \int_0^\infty \exp -\alpha t \mathbb{I}_{\{D(t) < x - s\}} \phi(x - D(t)) dt. \)

Introducing the notation

\( \tilde{\phi}_s(x) = \phi(x) \mathbb{I}_{x > s}, \)

we have

$$ E \int_0^{\tau_x} \exp -\alpha t \phi(x - D(t))dt = E \int_0^\infty \exp -\alpha t \tilde{\phi}_s(x - D(t))dt $$

Collecting results, we obtain the formula

$$ u_\alpha(x) = E \int_0^\infty \exp -\alpha t \tilde{\phi}_s(x - D(t))dt + C_\alpha \left( \frac{\lambda}{\alpha + \lambda} \right)^{x-s} $$

In ergodic theory, one is interested in the behavior of \(\alpha u_\alpha(x)\) as \(\alpha \to 0\), for any fixed \(x\). From the above formula, it is clear that

$$ \lim_{\alpha \to 0} \alpha u_\alpha(x) = \lim_{\alpha \to 0} \alpha C_\alpha $$

It is easy to check that

$$ \lim_{\alpha \to 0} \alpha C_\alpha = \frac{\lambda H_s(S)}{Q} $$

where

$$ H_s(S) = E \int_0^\infty \tilde{\phi}_s(S - D(t))dt $$

It is easy to check the relation

$$ H_s(S) - H_s(S - 1) = \frac{\tilde{\phi}_s(S)}{\lambda} = \frac{\phi(S)}{\lambda} $$

By induction, one checks the formula

$$ H_s(S) = \sum_{j=0}^{Q-1} \frac{\phi(S - j)}{\lambda} $$

Finally we have obtained

$$ \lim_{\alpha \to 0} \alpha u_\alpha(x) = \lim_{\alpha \to 0} \alpha C_\alpha = \sum_{k=s+1}^S \frac{\phi(k)}{Q} $$

Recalling that, from standard ergodic theory arguments

$$ \lim_{T \to \infty} \alpha u_\alpha(x) = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T \phi(y_x(t))dt = \sum_{k=s+1}^S \frac{\phi(k)}{Q} $$

The right hand side can be interpreted as \(E\phi(X)\) where \(X\) is a random variable distributed uniformly on \(s+1, \cdots, S\). We can now proceed with the optimization of the parameters \(s, S\). We optimize the ergodic cost

$$ \lim_{\alpha \to 0} \alpha E \left[ (K + cQ) \sum_{n=0}^\infty \exp -\alpha T_n + \int_0^\infty \exp -\alpha t \phi(y_x(t))dt \right] $$

From previous results, this amounts to

$$ L(s, Q) = \frac{\lambda K}{Q} + \sum_{k=1}^Q \frac{g(s + k)}{Q} $$

recalling

$$ g(x) = E f(x - D(L)) $$

To write necessary conditions we consider the continuous approximation which writes

\( L(s, Q) = \frac{\Lambda K}{Q} + \frac{1}{Q} \int_0^Q g(s + x) dx. \)

It is easy to recover the conditions (14.11.5).

## INVENTORY CONTROL WITH DIFFUSION DEMAND

## 15.1. INTRODUCTION

We continue the study of Inventory Control in continuous time. We want to consider a quite general demand process, combining deterministic rate and Poisson jumps as in Chapter 14 plus a diffusion term. We follow the work of [9] and the complement [4]. We will use the full power of Q.V.I (Quasi-Variational Inequalities) to solve the Dynamic Programming problem associated to the impulse control problem. We then check that the optimal feedback is obtained by an _s, S_ policy.

## 15.2. PROBLEM FORMULATION

### 15.2.1. DEMAND PROCESS.

The demand process is composed of three parts: a deterministic part with constant rate \(\nu\). It was called \(\lambda\) in Chapter 14, but \(\lambda\) will be used for the compound Poisson part. We consider a probability space \(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, P\) on which are defined a Poisson process \(n(t)\) with intensity rate \(\Lambda\), a Wiener process \(w(t)\) and a sequence of independent identically distributed random variables \(\xi_1, \cdots, \xi_i, \cdots\) All these random variables and stochastic processes are mutually independent. The random variables \(\xi_i\) have a probability density \(\mu\) on \(R^+\). We define the compound Poisson process by

\(N(t) = \sum_{i \le n(t)} \xi_i.\)

The demand on an interval \((0, t)\) is then given by

\(D(t) = \nu t + N(t) + \sigma w(t),\)

where \(\sigma\) is a positive coefficient. Let \(\mathcal{F}^t = \sigma(D(s), s \le t)\)

An impulse control is a sequence

\(\theta_n, v_n,\)

where \(\theta_n\) is a stopping time with respect to the filtration \(\mathcal{F}^t\) and \(v_n\) is a random variable \(\mathcal{F}^{\theta_n}\) measurable. Denoting by _V_ an impulse control, the corresponding inventory is described by the formula

\(y_\sigma(t; V) = x - D(t) + M(t; V),\)

with

\(M(t) = M(t; V) = \sum_{\{n \mid \theta_n < t\}} v_n\)

### 15.2.2. CONTROL PROBLEM.

Let again

$f(x) = hx^+ + px^-$.

We define the cost functional

(15.2.1) \( J_\sigma(V) = E \left[ \sum_{n=0}^\infty (K + cv_n) \exp -\alpha \theta_n + \int_0^\infty f(y_\sigma(t; V)) \exp -\alpha t dt \right] \)

and the value function

\( u(x) = \inf J_\sigma(V) \)

To obtain the relations of Dynamic Programming we can assume sufficient smoothness and write the optimality principle. If we do not order for a small amount of time \(\delta\) then we can write

\( u(x) \le \delta f(x) + (1 - \alpha \delta) Eu(x - D(\delta)), \)

and

\( Eu(x - D(\delta)) = Eu(x - \delta v - N(\delta) - \sigma w(\delta)). \)

Formally

\( N(\delta) = \begin{cases} 0, & \text{w.p. } 1 - \lambda \delta \\ \in (\xi, \xi + d\xi), & \text{w.p. } \lambda \delta \mu(\xi) d\xi \end{cases} \)

and \(w(\delta)\) is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance \(\delta\). Therefore

\( Eu(x - D(\delta)) \simeq (1 - \lambda \delta) Eu(x - \delta v - \sigma w(\delta)) + \lambda \delta \int_0^\infty u(x - \xi) \mu(\xi) d\xi \)

Expanding up to the first order in \(\delta\) assuming second order smoothness of \(u\) we obtain the inequality

\( Au(x) + \alpha u(x) \le f(x), \)

in which \(A\) is the integro-differential operator

(15.2.2) \( Au(x) = -\frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 u''(x) + \nu u'(x) - \lambda \int_0^\infty (u(x - \xi) - u(x)) \mu(\xi) d\xi. \)

In case we make an order of size \(v\), then the inventory becomes instantaneously \(x + v\) and we write the inequality

\( u(x) \le K + cv + u(x + v), \forall v \ge 0 \)

so

\( u(x) \le M(u)(x), \)

with

(15.2.3) \( M(u)(x) = K + \inf_{v > 0} [cv + u(x + v)] \)

and since one of the two decisions must be taken, we have the complementarity slackness condition

\( (Au(x) + \alpha u(x) - f(x))(u(x) - M(u)(x)) = 0. \)

### 15.2.3. QUASI-VARIATIONAL INEQUALITY.

We shall assume $\exists \bar{\pi} > 0$, such that $\int_0^\infty \exp \pi \bar{\pi} \mu(d\xi) = +\infty$

(15.2.4) 
$$ \int_0^\infty \exp \pi \xi \mu(d\xi) < +\infty, \forall \pi < \bar{\pi} $$

We look for a function \(u(x)\) which is \(C^1\), a.e. twice differentiable with linear growth such that

(15.2.5) 
$$ \begin{aligned} Au(x) + \alpha u(x) &\le f(x) \\ u(x) &\le M(u(x)) \\ (Au(x) + \alpha u(x) - f(x))(u(x) - M(u(x))) &= 0 \end{aligned} $$

We call this system of relations a Q.V.I. (Quasi-Variational Inequality)

## 15.3. s, S POLICY

### 15.3.1. NOTATION.

Let us set

$$ \begin{aligned} G(x) &= u(x) + cx; \\ g(x) &= f(x) + \alpha cx, \end{aligned} $$

then the Q.V.I is slightly modified into

(15.3.1) 
$$ \begin{aligned} AG(x) + \alpha G(x) &\le g(x) + cv + c\lambda\tilde{\xi} \\ G(x) &\le K + \inf_{\eta > x} G(\eta) \end{aligned} $$

(15.3.2) 
$$ (AG(x) + \alpha G(x) - (g(x) + cv + c\lambda\tilde{\xi})) \left( G(x) - K - \inf_{\eta > x} G(\eta) \right) = 0, $$

in which \(\tilde{\xi}\) denotes the mean of a random variable with probability \(\mu\)

$$ \tilde{\xi} = \int_0^{+\infty} \xi \mu(d\xi). $$

If s is a real number we define \(G_s(x)\) by solving the following problem

(15.3.3) 
$$ \begin{aligned} AG_s(x) + \alpha G_s(x) &= g(x) + cv + c\lambda\tilde{\xi} & x > s \\ G_s(x) &= K + \inf_{\eta > s} G_s(\eta) & x \le s \end{aligned} $$

Since we want the solution to be \(C^1\) we must impose the condition

(15.3.4) 
$$ G'_s(s) = 0 $$

### 15.3.2. FUNCTION \(H_s(x)\).

It is natural to look for the derivative $$ H_s(x) = G'_s(x) $$

which is the solution of

(15.3.5) 
$$ \begin{aligned} AH_s(x) + \alpha H_s(x) &= g'(x) & x > s \\ H_s(x) &= 0 & x \le s \end{aligned} $$

Since \(g'(x)\) is bounded we look for bounded solutions

**Proposition 15.1.** We assume (15.2.4). Then, for any s there exists a unique continuous bounded function solution (15.3.5). The solution is \(C^2\) on \((s, +\infty)\).
