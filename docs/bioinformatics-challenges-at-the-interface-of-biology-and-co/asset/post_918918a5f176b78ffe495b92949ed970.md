**Consensus expressions** are often referred to as patterns, which nearly summarize patterns of residue conservation within alignments. Being simple to create, patterns like these were used to encapsulate conserved residues of protein active sites, binding sites, etc., and collection provided the foundations of the first protein family database, PROSITE. This was an important development because for the first time, consensus expression was available to the community to search with. Each pattern also allows us to characterise newly detected sequences using tools like this has been one of the major goals of bioinformatics since the first sequence databases were created. The appeal of this particular method was, and still is, its simplicity is its drawback and to the development of other, more sophisticated, approaches. These issues are explored in Chapter 4.

Diagnostically, sequence patterns suffer certain limitations. Specifically, matching a pattern is a binary 'match/no-match' event: hence, even the most trivial difference (a single amino acid) is a mis-match. As Swiss-Prot grew and accommodated more and more divergent sequences, the more evident and problematic this weakness became. Indeed, it generated a kind of circularity in which the more particular PROSITE patterns were to fail to diagnose or characterise them correctly, and the more work curators had to do update or revise the sequence patterns in order to better the changing contents of Swiss-Prot. This immense dependence on both the size and composition of Swiss-Prot to become one of the toughest challenges for PROSITE curators (and indeed for the curators of all protein family-based databases in future).

Many researchers were aware of the limitations of sequence patterns, and worked to develop more powerful 'descriptors' of protein families and functional units. Profiles built from comprehensive sequence alignments, and are much more tolerant of amino acid changes or substitutions than patterns; they’re much more flexible in how they handle the differences in the lengths of sequences that arise from insertions or deletions. In principle, therefore, profiles allow the relationships between families of sequences to be modelled 'realistically'. Philipp Bucher was one of the first to research and develop profile-based protein sequence-analysis methods, and he began again with Bairoch to augment PROSITE with a range of these new sequence profiles – the first release to include them were version 12.0, in June 1994 (Bairoch and Bucher, 1994).

A different approach, which evolved independently from PROSITE (at least in terms of the underlying analysis method), is of protein family 'fingerprinting'. By contrast with sequence patterns, focus focuses on unique sites or motifs that are found in multiple sequence alignments, fingerprints are created from groups of conserved motifs. It is the unique inter-relations between these motifs that provide signatures for protein families or structural/functional domains (see Box 3.4). The fingerprint method was originally devised to analyse and characterise a superfamily of proteins known as G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), distinguishing trait is the presence of seven well-conserved transmembrane (TM) domains – the first fingerprint therefore identified these sequences as sequence motifs; but the method had more general utility, and was well used to characterise other families. The results were used to populate a database that was initially known as the Features Database, a component of the SERPENT information storage and analysis resource for protein sequences (Akigg et al., 1992). The Features Database (later to be reborn as PRINTS²) saw its first release in October 1991, with 29 entries. Most of these equivalent entries in PROSITE, which they had a much more substantial 441 family descriptions.

Although disparate in size, the Features and PROSITE databases shared certain attributes. The most important of these was the principle of adding value to the databases by manually annotating. In 1992, the similarities between these and Artwood discussed the possibility of unifying the PROSITE and Features databases into the first integrated, annotated protein family resource. Together with colleagues at the University of Leeds, they submitted a bid for European funding; however, the proposal was not successful, and they had to wait until seven years later before their goal was realised.

In the meantime, despite the value of PROSITE, on the one hand, and by its diagnostic limitations, on the other, a range of other databases began to emerge. One of the earliest, Blocks², developed by Steve and Jorja Henikoff (Henikoff and Henikoff, 1991). The Blocks approach was very similar to fingerprinting in far as it used multiple motifs; however, the method used a different scoring system and didn't exploit the relationships between motifs, and its diagnostic behaviour was different. A few years later ProDom² (Sonhammer and Kahn, 1994), which adopted a new approach, essentially using a clustering algorithm to Swiss-Prot intents contains protein 'families'; later still Pfam² (Sonhammer et al., 1997), which used a probabilistic method very similar to profiles. Initially linked to the annotation of predicted proteins from genomic sequencing of Caenorhabditis elegans, Pfam was to become one of the most widely used used protein family databases in Europe and the USA.

The main feature that distinguishes a fingerprint from a consensus pattern (Box 3.3) is that it draws its diagnostic power from groups of conserved regions, rather than individual motifs. The potency of fingerprints derives from the fact that they not only use multiple motifs, but they inherently encapsulate information relating to the number of motifs they contain, to their order in the alignment, and to the distance or interval between them (as shown in Figure 3.7); the resulting discriminator is thus a powerful signature for the given protein family.

Of course, different families are characterised by different patterns of conservation, and their fingerprints differently. Figure 3.8 shows a schematic illustration of fingerprints for three different families: the first two fingerprints contain four motifs, but their sizes and the distances between them are different; the third fingerprint contains five motifs, again with different sizes and inter-motif distances.

An especially important feature of protein fingerprints is that a query sequence need not match all of its constituent motifs equally well, nor need all of the motifs to be able to diagnose it as a potential family member – in other words, fingerprints can tolerate mismatches within individual motifs and within the fingerprint as a whole.

In principle, any protein family that sufficiently well can be uniquely fingerprinted. The flexibility of the approach, with regard to how well practised motifs match the fingerprint, and how many motifs match, lends great analytical potential, and hence offers powerful diagnostic opportunities beyond those afforded by consensus patterns.

# 3.9 TrEMBL

By 1996, the first quakes that followed the explosion of whole-genome sequencing projects were felt. The impact was great for databases whose maintenance involved significant amounts of manual annotation; many were not able to adapt in time; many did not survive. Swiss-Prot was a survivor of the onslaught, but this required radically new philosophical approaches to be embraced and new processes to be adopted.
