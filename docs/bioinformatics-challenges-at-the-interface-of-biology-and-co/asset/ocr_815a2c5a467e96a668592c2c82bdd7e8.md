# Bioinformatics challenges at the interface of biology and computer science

At this time, Swiss-Prot had the highest standard of annotation of any publicly available protein sequence database: from the outset, one of its leading goals had to provide critical analysis and annotation for all of its constituents. To this end, each entry was accompanied by a significant amount of manually gleaned information: this was derived primarily from original publications and review articles by an expanding group of curators, with input from an international panel of experts. This high degree of meticulous manual annotation was becoming a bigger and bottleneck, inhibiting the release rate of the resource. Eventually, faced with the relentless data flow from growing number of genome projects, this labour-intensive process became untenable.

To keep pace with the flood of data, a new approach was needed. The imperative was to release the products of genomic sequences into the public domain as swiftly as possible; the problem was how this could be done without compromising the high quality of the Swiss-Prot data, or eroding the editorial standards of the database. The solution was to create a computer-generated sequence, with a Swiss-Prot format, in which entries were derived by translation of coding sequences in the EMBL database library—this TREMb, first released in October 1996 (Bairoch and Apweiler, 1996). TREM1 contained almost 105,000 entries, not far twice the size of Swiss-Prot 34.0, with which it was released in parallel.

Initially, TREM contained no annotations – it was essentially the result of an automatic pipeline that extracted coding sequences from the EMBL database and reformatted them. The idea behind its creation was twofold. In addition giving Swiss-Prot users access to the products of genomic data more quickly, the resource was intended to act as a coral in which to hold ‘raw’ sequences prior to their annotation by curators, in readiness for accession to Swiss-Prot. As such, it had two main sections: SP-TREMb, containing entries that would eventually go into Swiss-Prot, once annotated; and REM-TREMb, containing sequences that would never be used in Swiss-Prot (fragments of less than enough, synthetic sequences, etc.).

The raw state of TREMb entries meant that most of work was necessary to upgrade them to the necessary Swiss-Prot standard. To truly accelerate this process, various tools and protocols were developed over the years to provide some level of annotation automatically: e.g., including information about the potential functions of proteins, their subcellular locations, the metabolic pathways in which they’re involved, active sites they may contain, factors, binding sites, domains they may possess, and so on. Initially, binding-site, domain and family-based information was derived from searches of databases like PROSITE, PRINTS and Pfam; later, information from the analgamated protein family resource, InterPro, was also used. By February 2015, with >90 million entries, TREMb2 was 163 times larger than Swiss-Prot, illustrating the vast gap that has opened between manual and computer-assisted strategies.
