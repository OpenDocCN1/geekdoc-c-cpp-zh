# Bioinformatics challenges at the interface of biology and computer science

Why is this discrepancy in eukaryotes? As we saw earlier, genes in eukaryotes are interrupted by introns, which must be excised to form a continuous transcript for translation. Turns out that introns can be hundreds of bases long, and genes can be hundreds of bases apart. Obviously, the smaller intergenic regions, and the fewer introns, the easier it is to find them. For some eukaryotes, which are more prokaryote-like in terms of their genome organisation, gene identification is a more tractable problem; however, with intron-rich genes, there is no single, reliable method for predicting protein-coding genes – for most organisms, it is therefore difficult or impossible to know how many genes really contain (e.g., the three different genes in Table 2.5 for Arabidopsis thaliana, and see Box 2.8). As we saw in Section 2.2.5, a difficulty in gene identification is in correctly recognizing intron-exon boundaries. Boundary identification relies on identification of splice sites. Most splice sites have similar sequences, and many are generalised by particular dinucleotides at each end of intron; but there are variants of these sequences, making prediction of intron-exon boundaries tricky.

Figure 2.21 illustrates some of the problems that can arise when gene prediction software goes wrong. If intron-exon boundaries are not correctly acknowledged, incorrect gene structures are generated, with missing exons or introns, extended exons, and inclusion of exons beyond the correct gene structure. Note that the figure shows a highly simplified gene model: it doesn’t account for the complications that arise from gene nesting, overlapping introns, and fusion of genes. Overall, gene prediction in higher eukaryotes poses major challenges, and results are obtained from combined approaches that use several different programs in parallel, that integrate extrinsic and intrinsic data, and train the prediction algorithms on organism-specific training sets.

In 2000 (when the first draft of the human genome was being assembled), a comparison of gene-prediction software against Drosophila revealed that the best tools could correctly predict only ~40 percent of genes (Reese et al., 2000). At the time, estimates for the numbers of genes encoded in the human genome varied dramatically (from tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands) – Box 2.8; the inaccuracy of the gene-prediction tools available at the time may be one reason for such uncertainty. Even today, predictions for most organisms are unknown, and this is why the numbers quoted in Tables 2.4 and 2.5 are given as ‘predicted’ gene counts. The numbers should be considered as snapshots of our current thinking, tempered by the limits of the technology of the day. In time, many of these numbers will probably change (indeed, some may already have so), as improvements are made both in sequencing technology and in gene-prediction software.
