# Chapter 3 Biological databases

Up to the summer of 2014, Ensembl assembled data based on the December 2013 Homo sapiens high-coverage assembly GRCH38 from the Genome Reference Consortium (GRC), which places sequences into a chromosomal context. The GRC aimss to provide the best possible reference assembly for humans by generating multiple representations (allele locus) for chromosomes, and revealing fixed terms, to improve the representation of chromosomal regions. But Ensembl is particularly noteworthy because it is much more than just a repository for information relating to the human genome. Its vision was to provide a powerful bioinformatics framework for orchestrating biological investigations, able to facilitate the storage, analysis and visualisation of large genomes (Hub et al., 2002). More a deal, the system handles the genomes of numerous vertebrate organisms, including those of our cousins, the chimpanzee and gorilla, and those of most exotic species, such as the banana and Tasmanian devil. Release 73, in September 2013, included data from 77 species, with support given to human, mouse, rat, variety and zebrafish (Fish et al., 2014) - also from the Ensembl home-page.

Non-vertebrate species are also supported by Ensembl, including yeast, birds and worm. Annotation for their genomes is imported from their respective organic databases in a collaborative project known as Ensembl Genomes. Overall, Ensembl provides access to a battery of genomes for non-vertebrate species, from bacteria, protists and fungi, to plants and invertebrate metazoa (Kersey et al., 2014) - again, a full current list⁴ can be accessed from. Information is also available from PreEnsembl³, which offers early access to genes that are not yet annotated - here tools for BLAST searches and genome visualisation are accessible but not full.

The enormous wealth of available information, coupled with powerful visualisation tools that allow users to drill down through different views of the data, has rendered Ensembl an invaluable system for comparative genomics studies. It is an impressive complement to the many general-purpose nucleotide databases.
