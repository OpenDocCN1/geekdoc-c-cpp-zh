One way to address the financial issues is to raise funds commercially, for example by ‘dual licensing’: i.e., providing a resource for free users but academic
charging for it (or for associated consultancy services) in say, a commercial environment. This is difficult for a number of reasons. First, defining what is meant by ‘commercial use’ can be hard; it might be that a privately owned pharma-company is ‘commercial’, but the status of many universities and research
institutions is often more complex. Second, policing such agreements is challenging,
an often relies on commercial organisations ‘doing the right thing’ and offering a
in return for using a resource that they could easily obtain for free (this ‘trust-based'
model worked well for a number of years for Swiss-Prot, but later had to
change), when NIH funding obtained to support it situated that the database
is made freely available. Third, and perhaps most importantly, it relies on the
resource’s creators (often academics) being sufficiently entrepreneurial to make such
an approach. There’s no doubt that such models can change. Founded in 1993,
Red Hat Inc., made a successful business providing commercial port for its open-source Linux distribution; but the number of companies that achieved stability off the back of free software is small compared to those with traditional business models. In any case, it could be argued that scientists are perhaps best to focus on science, rather than attempting to innovate in the dogeat of big business.

A seductive alternative view is that any sufficiently important and open resource be maintained by the community. The reality seems to do that is also hard, and works only rarely and
then only for the most prominent projects. The foundations that maintain Wikipedia and Firefox, for example, two of the world’s most prominent resources, are in a
constant state of fund-raising from users to keep these important projects alive; very fewer projects have the resources necessary to be able to manage such major
fund-raising initiatives.

On balance, it’s hard to avoid the conclusion that the only reliable way to keep cyber-infrastructure in good shape is for it to be founded alongside core research that relies
so fundamentally upon it. The difficulty here is that most funding agencies have mission
statements that speak about improving the world through fundamental research:
mission of the NIH, for example, is

to seek fundamental knowledge about the nature and behavior of living systems and the
application of that knowledge to enhance health, lengthen life, and reduce illness and disability.

The problem, perhaps, is funding infrastructure to 'keep the lights on' is a much harder and infinitely less sexy idea to those who hold the purse strings than 'curing cancer', even if one of them is a prerequisite for the other. As a result of, the world's scientific cyberinfrastructure is built on the back of projects whose primary goal is fundamental research, and any infrastructures they create tend to be of incidental. This issue was neatly summarised by Geoffrey Bilder, when he said that

If we funded and ran physical infrastructure like we fund and run scholarly cyberinfrastructure, and water would go out every grant we would have architects bidding to build Quonets /sic/ with the promise that the client would get railways and electrical grids as side-effects (Bilder, 2014).

As part of his scathing indictment of all attitudes towards building and maintaining a viable scholarly cyberinfrastructure, Bilder asked bluntly, 'Why are we so at infrastructure? How can we get it right for a change?' The sobering truth is that we almost certainly know the answer to these questions; however, the harsh reality is that implementing the solutions requires drastic changes in the mind-sets of academics and funding-bodies alike, and the recognition that modern scientific breakthroughs are not just the result of standing on the shoulders of giants, but also of creating the kind of cyberfrastructural bedrock upon which our scientists need to reach their achievements. An place, in 2002, of the European Strategy Forum on Research Infrastructures (ESFRI). ESFRI's mission is

to support a current and strategy-led policy-making on research infrastructures in Europe, and to facilitate multilateral initiatives leading to the better use and development of research infrastructures, at EU and international level.

Deadly though this might say, when high-level strategising is essential if research infrastructures are to be fully recognised for, and supported, the vital role they play in the underpinning of knowledge and technology. # Conclusion

As Kennard so precisely said, more than 18 years ago, ‘Free access to validated and enhanced data worldwide is a beautiful dream. The reality, however, is complex (Kennard, 1997). As we see, that arises from a sticky political, even when the financial, technical and cultural glut that tends to limit our progress, even when solutions appear ‘obvious’. Politicians need to be educated, funders and scientists need to change the way they think, the cultural barriers that divide scientific need need be torn down, and together we must start to bridge the gaps that exist between them. The world of bioinformatics, as we see in this book, part of the process of bridging cultural gaps involves recognising, on one hand, that it is hard to teach number-crunching computers to do, and on the other, that it is hard to teach users (by they life scientists or bioinformaticians) to use and interact approximately with computers – on both sides of the divide, there’s still a lot to learn.

On the upside, we live in the most computer-savvy, digitally-aware, Internet-enabled society the planet has ever witnessed; and, fortunately, you are part of the next generation of bioinformaticians and computer scientists who, by understanding the challenges we talked about above, here will be better equipped to take the pioneering steps necessary to transform the ‘beautiful dream’ into an exciting new reality. On route, we counsel you to ‘mind the gap’, and wish luck in what will undoubtedly be a challenging and inspirational journey!
