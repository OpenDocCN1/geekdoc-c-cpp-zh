## Chapter 1
## Introduction

## 1.1 Overview

In this chapter, we introduce the main perspectives of the book: bioinformatics and computer science. In Section 1.2, we offer a working definition of the term 'bioinformatics', we discuss where the discipline came from, and consider the impact of the genome-sequencing revolution. In Section 1.3, we discuss the origins of computer science, and note the emerging challenges relating to how to manage and describe biological data in ways that are computationally tractable. Having set the scene, we reflect briefly on some of the gaps that now confront computer science and bioinformatics.

By the end of the chapter, you should have an appreciation of how the field of bioinformatics evolved; you should also gained insights into the extent to which its future progress is linked to the advances in data management and knowledge representation that are engaging computer science today.

## 1.2 Bioinformatics

### 1.2.1 What is bioinformatics?

Bioinformatics is a term that means different things to different people, with so many interpretations - many of them entirely - that it can sometimes be difficult to know what bioinformatics actually means, whether it is just computational by another name. One way of making sense of the bioinformatics landscape is to recognise that it has both service and research components. Its service side primarily involves the routine storage, maintenance and retrieval of biological data. These may seem like rather tasks for today's technologies, we explore why this is far from true. By contrast, the research side of bioinformatics involves analysis of biological data using a variety of tools and techniques, often in combination, to create complex workflows or pipelines, including components ranging from pattern recognition and statistics, to visualisation and modelling. As we will, a particularly important facet of data analysis also concerns the use and development of prediction tools (later, we will look at the ramifications of our heavy reliance on structure- and-prediction approaches, especially in the light of high-throughput biology). The union of all these capabilities into a broad-based, interdisciplinary science, involving both theoretical, practical and conceptual tools for the generation, dissemination, representation, analysis and understanding of biological information sets apart from computational biology (which, as the name suggests, is perhaps more concerned with the development of mathematical tools for modelling and simulating biological systems).

In this book, we broadly explore the computational manipulation and conceptual representation of biological sequences and macromolecular structures. We choose this vantage-point for two reasons: first, as outlined in the Preface, this is where we can discuss many of the challenges from first-hand experience; our ‘home territory’, and hence, second, this is where the discipline of bioinformatics has its roots, and it’s from these origins that may of its successes, failures and opportunities stem.

### 1.2.2 The provenance of bioinformatics

The origins of bioinformatics, both as a term and as a scientific discipline, are controversial. The term itself was coined by theoretical biologist Paulien Hogeweg. In the early 1970s, he established the first research group specialising in bioinformatics, at the University of Utrecht (Hogeweg, 1978; Hogeweg and Hesper). Back then, with his colleague Ben Hesper, he defined the term to mean ‘the study of information processes in biotic systems’ (Hogeweg, 2011). But the term didn’t gain popularity in the community for another two decades; and, by the time it had, it taken on a different meaning.

In Europe, a turning point seems to have been around 1990, with the organisation of the Bioinformatics in the 90s (held in Maastricht in 1991), probably the first to include this ‘new’ term – bioinformatics in its title. During the same period, the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) had been established in the United States of America (USA) (Benson et al., 1990). But this was a centre for biotechnology information, not a bioinformatics centre, and it was established, at least in part, to provide the nation's needs.

### 1.2.3 The seeds of bioinformatics

It's useful to think about where and when the seeds of bioinformatics were first sown. But where do we start? We could go back to Franklin and Crick (1953), or focus on Watson and Crick (1953). We could consider the pioneering work of Anfinsen (1959), or the 3D structures of Kendrew (1958) and Perutz (1963). Viewed in the light of today's high-throughput sequence and structure determinations, these time-scales seem unimaginaginably slow.
