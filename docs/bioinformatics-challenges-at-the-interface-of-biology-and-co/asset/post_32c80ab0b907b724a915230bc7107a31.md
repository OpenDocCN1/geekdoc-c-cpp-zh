Regardless, since 2000, gene-prediction approaches have improved, genomic data-sets have been re-analysed, and gene counts have been subject to iterative refinement. When the draft human genome was made available, the estimated number of genes found to be 30,000-35,000; five months later, the numbers counted up again, to 65,000-75,000 (Wright et al., 2001). Almost a decade later, the number of predicted human genes weighed mostly between 18,877 and 20,251 (Pertea and Salzberg, 2010).

Aside from the difficulty of accurately locating intron-exon boundaries, another issue is what constitutes a gene has differed: whether it should be considered a heritable unit relating to an observed phenotype, or a packet of genetic information that encodes RNA, a protein or several proteins – as we see in Chapter 1, genes may encode multiple splice forms. Alternative splicing was first noted in the calcitonin of the human gene: the six exons of the human gene isoforms that span exons 1-4, and a third that spans exons 1-3, 5 and 6, skipping exon 4 – marking its significant difference from the others, the third is termed calcitonin gene-related peptide. These differences are shown in Figure 5.1.

The extent of alternative splicing in some genes makes this calcitonin appear tribal: e.g., the complexity of the Drosophila Dcam gene led to a prediction it may have 38,016 splice variants (Schucker et al., 2000)! To further matters, genes can exist in multiple copies, and they don't have to be expressed (it's little wonder, then, that a definition and set of guidelines had to be provided for the gene-prediction sweep to indicate what would actually count a gene in the contest). Needless to say, getting computers to handle such convolutions poses enormous challenges. With our understanding of the simple term 'calcitonin gene' presents a virtually unavoidable field of uncertainty and adversity to a machine; deconvoluting the concept into a technical specification that would allow a computer to find and reliably construct a given calcitonin gene (or any alternatively-spliced gene) becomes a significant undertaking.

## 5.4 Problems with names

In the Preface, we mentioned that getting humans to agree on what words mean is hard; getting them to understand when they're using the same word to mean different things is harder; getting a machine to understand the difference is harder still. For machines, precision is vital; however, adherence to standard nomenclature has, at best, in the life sciences.

Consider the protein, ‘DNA replication factor MCM4’, shown in Table 5.1: this has five different protein and six different genes in three species. With bit of work, humans can unravel this kind of intricacy relatively easily; but once we involve computers in the loop of comprehension, the task becomes more complicated, and the level of precision required to specify a protein or gene becomes much more difficult to define. For example, if we use a text-mining algorithm to search the literature for protein ‘proliferation abnormal’, we would miss other names of the same protein (cell division control protein, CDC homolog, P1-CDC21); DCS4, etc. Only if the software had been programmed to use comprehensive synonym dictionaries or if we search for the gene *dpa*, would we be sure we haven't missed a relevant gene or protein like *Mcm4* or *dpa* in the fly? To be absolutely confident in our results, of course, we'd need access to the ultimate gene and protein repository for all species, the creation and maintenance of which would be a logistical nightmare, given the dearth of nomenclature standards or, more to the point, our failure to adhere consistently to those¹.

## 5.5 Problems with sequences

Related to the nomenclature issues mentioned above are more subtle problems. For example, consider this: when we refer to the sequence of calcitonin, which sequence do we really mean? This might sound like a pedantic question. After all, we probably just the current, most up-to-date version; but the *current version* of a sequence is a historical artefact – the current version of calcitonin in 1986 was different from the *current version* in 1991. In the mid-1980s, the former is likely to have been a version of the sequence based on the 1986 PIR entry (AC# A03155, OOSH) – see Figure 5.2; during the 1990s and 00s, however, the latter is likely to have been a version based on the 1991 PIR entry (AC# A30407, ID OOSH) – see Figure 5.3.
