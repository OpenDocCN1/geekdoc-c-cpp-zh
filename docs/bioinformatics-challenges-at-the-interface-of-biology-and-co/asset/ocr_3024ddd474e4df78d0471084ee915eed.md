# Box 7.9 IP Addresses

Microsoft's Bill Gates reportedly, '64k should be more than enough for anyone'
legend has that IBM's Thomas J. Watson said once, 'I think there is a market for maybe
five computers'. They may not have predicted how much information technology would
change the world, it's still imagining these pioneers of computing understanding thetim and demand for their products so spectacularly. Disappointingly, there's no-
either that of them ever saw anything of the sort.

Where early computer scientists did rather underestimate the demand, however, was in the
design of the addressing system itself: the Internet Protocol 4 Address,
more commonly known as IP (or more commonly, IPv4 Address). IPv4 addresses are 32-bit
identifiers, used in human-readable forms from 0 to 255, and separated
by a dot (e.g., 103.88.192.9). There means that 2^32 possible IPv4 addresses, which is
4,294,967,296 – or –4.3 billion – unique. Even though several large blocks of this range
are reserved for special uses, the Internet's original designers, for whom, in the early 1980s, a-
puter was a power-hungry beast, can be forgiven for thinking that this
would be plenty of space to share around.

What they didn't really predict was that the cost and size of hardware would reduce
so much that all nowadays tend to carry around several devices - smartphones, laptops,
tables - that are designed to be connected (sometimes) to the Internet, and have,
in homes and offices, a growing number of other appliances - televisions, central
security systems, and - that each part of the growing 'internet of things'
which also needs an address. When every friday, toaster and pencil demands are IP
address, the problem will become even more acute.

Today, there are far more needing IP addresses than there are IPv4,
a number of variable length (Classless Inter-Domain Routing*) and NAT
(Network Address Translation) have been put in place while the world transitions to the
ext generation of IP Addressing. IPv6 uses an 128-bit address space, which gives 2^128
or 3.4x10^38 possible addresses (or, if you like, than 7.9x10^28 times as many
is IPv4). At a rough estimate, that is enough to allow every human that has ever lived
in the history of mankind to have more than 3.4x10^38 Internet-connected devices. That's more
than for anyone?
