Depending on their specialisms, life scientists (genetists, cell biologists, biochemists, physicists, bioinformaticians, etc.) tend to think in different ways: some on the biochemical activities of proteins (e.g., enzyme activity); others focus on the cellular processes in which proteins are involved, including details of catalytic mechanisms or molecular recognition; still others consider function in the genetic sense, when we consider that proteins may have multiple cellular roles; ascribing a single functional role to a particular phenomenon may be inappropriate – nevertheless, this often happens in databases.

To appreciate the function of a protein, we need to understand its action (its ‘local function’) and the role it performs in the cell (its ‘integrated function’). In terms of its action, we need to know about the cellular components with which it interacts and the types of interaction involved (e.g., protein-protein, protein-DNA, etc.). In terms of its role, we need to appreciate how the cell behaves when the protein is absent. Of course, function doesn’t function in alone systems – in several systems. Hence, function can be understood in different contexts and at different levels of granularity, from molecular to cellular, and to organism. Here, we can synthesise this into a meaningful one-line description that adequately annotates the function of a protein, which is typically the approach used to annotate database entries;

Another difficulty is, in the scientific literature and in database annotations, ‘function’ has been used to refer arbitrarily to biological activities, biological goals and cellular structures. In an attempt to introduce rigor into the field, several ontologies have been developed to better reflect biological ‘reality’. These controlled vocabularies to more explicitly define the relationships between gene products and their cellular components (e.g., the Gene Ontology GO; EcoC;) define relationships between enzymes, their locations in metabolic pathways, reaction products and substrates, and the superfamilies to which they belong (e.g., KEGG). Bio-ontology like this are now routinely to provide accessible annotations to supplement the free-text description lines traditionally used to describe the functions of sequences in UniProtKB.

The kind of formalism used in GO may feel a little more uncomfortable to the human reader, if we're to use computers in function-annotation tasks, but essential if we're able to more readily relate different terms or in different databases. Colourful, ad hoc nomenclature systems of the sort that appear to delight geneticists, are the source of bioinformatics (Pearson, 2001). In particular, those that include familiar, everyday words to describe biological entities (gene names like hedgehog, hairy, keen, tshirt, cap 'n' lost in space, etc.) define systematic analyses and quickly lose their amenity when faced with the task of trying to train computers to discover them in scientific texts, to link them to related concepts in databases that use different nomenclature systems, and ultimately to teach computers what they mean.

## 5.13 Summary

This chapter introduced some of the issues that have arisen as bioinformatics tools and databases have been moulded and adapted in ad hoc ways to tackle new problems. Specifically, we saw that:

- 1 The concept of a 'gene' is fluid and have different meanings for different scientists;
- 2 Genes are difficult to find and build computationally; their counts in newly sequenced genomes are therefore predictions that are subject to iterative refinement over time;
- 3 Genes and proteins may have different names in different species; tracking their occurrence in the literature and in databases requires the assistance of synonym dictionaries;
- 4 Protein sequences change over time, and the changes are tracked by means of their database identifiers and accession numbers;
- 5 Occasionally, the same accession number can point to different sequences – such changes can be tracked by means of the sequence version numbers in UniProtKB, and in the protein archive, UniProt;
- 6 The contents of database entries change over time, including the names of the entities and the data; this instability can make it difficult to know what biological entity a particular database entry refers;
