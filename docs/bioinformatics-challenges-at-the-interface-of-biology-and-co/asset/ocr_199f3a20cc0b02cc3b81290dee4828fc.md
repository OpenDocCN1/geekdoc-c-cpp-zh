Structural studies have also revealed that the similarities shared by some proteins reflect convergent evolution from unrelated ancestors, as a result, say, of physical principles that favour particular arrangements and fold topologies. The investigations of these phenomena have driven structural biologists to study the miniae of proteins from many different perspectives. Once elucidated, a structure becomes a kind of starting point from which atomic details may be refined through repeated structure determinations, and new molecular insights gained by replicating the analysis with the protein to its cognate ligand, or with the introduction of a particular mutation, so on. Consequently, with many variations, have been deposited in the PDB. Therefore, as the resource evolved, its contents have been skewed by a handful of well-studied proteins, like hemoglobin, immunoglobins, lysoymes and so on.

Returning to the evolution of the PDB, in 1996, a new database of macromolecular structures was created, the E-MSD (Boutelikakis et al., 2003), at the newly established European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI) in the UK. Building upon PDB data, the E-MSD was originally conceived as a pilot study to explore the feasibility of using relational databases to manage structural data more effectively. As it turned out, the prototype database emerging from the pilot was successful in its right, and the E-MSD consequently established as a major EBI resource.

During this period, a concerted effort was made to hasten the pace of knowledge acquisition from structural studies. This was partly motivated by the desire to augment the still-limited number of structures available in the PDB, but also partly to merge the redundancy. The answer was to establish a program of high-throughput X-ray crystallography in a high-profile venture known as the Protein Structure Initiative, or PSI (Burley et al., 1999). Several feasibility studies had already been launched; coupled with the broad-spanning vision of the PSI (which was gearing up to produce 10,000 new structures over the next ten years), the first indications that coping with high-throughput structure-definition pipelines would require many ways of gathering, storing, distributing and 'servicing' the data to end users.

One of the PDB's responses to this, and to the many challenges that lay ahead, was to form a new management structure. This was embodied in a three-membered Research Collaboration for Bioinformatics (RCSB), a consortium including Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey; the San Diego Supercomputer Center at the University of California; and the Center for Advanced Research in Biotechnology of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (Berman et al., 2000; Berman et al., 2003). With establishment of this consortium, the Brookhaven PDB ceased operations, and the RCSB formally took the helm on 1 July 2009.

With the RCSB in the USA, the E-MSD established in Europe, and a resource (PDB) subsequently announced in Japan (Nakamura et al., 2002), structure-collection efforts had taken on an international dimension. As a result, three repositories were brought together beneath an umbrella organisation as the world-wide Protein Data Bank (wwPDB), to harmonise their activities and maintain a single, publicly available archive of macromolecular structural data (Berman et al., 2003).
