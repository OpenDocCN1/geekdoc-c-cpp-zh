# Bioinformatics challenges at the interface of biology and computer science

From the perspective, of biological sequences, we've looked at tools and databases, algorithms and data structures, and teased out the relationships, possibilities and pitfalls of using computers to bridge the gap between biological data and the Web, combined with tools and knowledge; we have created a new 'knowledge infrastructure', both with potential and with problems. We've only hinted so far is the relationship between this new knowledge infrastructure and a much older one that existed – for the most part, fullyformed – before the invention of the first computer: the scientific literature.

As we saw in Chapters 1 and 3, early databases, like PIR and Swiss-Prot, were created by enthusiasts who painstakingly scoured the literature for papers containing information about newly sequenced proteins, and manually transcribed the amino acid sequences therein into electronic formats. To augment and add value to the raw sequence data, we would, however, possible, to gather assorted facts about the protein (its subcellular location, its function, its post-translational modifications, and so on; by large, they also record the records they used as information sources. Today, biological database annotators (or biocurators, as they're all known), still spend amounts of time scouring the literature for facts to support, refute or somehow add meaning or explanation to particular database records – they do this database entries are regarded as more authoritative and meaningful when they can publish experimental evidence.

The task of excavating specific facts from the literature can be quite tricky.
