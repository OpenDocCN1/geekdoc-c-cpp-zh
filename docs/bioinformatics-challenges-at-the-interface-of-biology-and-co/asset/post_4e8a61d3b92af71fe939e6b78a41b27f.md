Structure-based alignments necessarily differ from sequence-based alignments because the comparison frameworks are not equivalent; one is not 'better' than the other – both are valid for the different dimensions of information they are intended for the different residues they are intended to answer. Many researchers continue to use structure-based alignments as benchmarks for the quality of sequence alignments, the fact that the underpinning models of comparison are designed for different purposes: sequence- and structure-based alignments are not replicable of the sample, but formulations of comparison frameworks in different dimensions – one uses artificial gap characters to maximise the

It is not unreasonable, therefore, to seek a sequence-based alignment method to reproduce the results of a structural alignment protocol (Edgar, 2012).

If we want to ascertain the quality of alignments, it only makes sense to do so if we compare with like. Even then, we're likely to open a bale of worms surrounding how we know what a good or 'correct' alignment is, which requires us to understand what alignment means; as we see, this is from straightforward. We can use scoring matrices to quantify the degree of identity/or similarity between sequences, or their overall alignment scores (Section 4.3.2); and perhaps this tells us useful for pairs or small numbers of closely related sequences. However, in multiple alignments, we can't always be certain to have aligned residues that are homologous, not least because alignments merging evolutionary histories, generate many mutations other than substitutions, insertions and deletions (Daniels et al., 2012; Edgar, 2010); and the alternative – requiring strictly equivalent residues to be aligned - is, as we've seen, although fraught with difficulty. If we compare and evaluate alignments, we understand in detail how they were made, the parameters and context of the comparison framework we've used, and the limitations of the alignment and comparison methods, then ascertaining the quality of an alignment becomes a little easier (although by no means trivial). Unfortunately, however, these conditions are seldom true if humans find it hard to appreciate the nuances, how much harder is it to teach computers to do so?

As a kind of postscript to this section, if the examples we have led us to think more about what alignment means, consider how much more problems will be when next-generation alignment tools become mainstream – take ClustalW for example, which has been used to generate alignments of >190,000 sequences (Sievers et al., 2011). According to its authors, benchmark tests, Clustal2 is much more than widely used, fast methods and comparable in accuracy of some of the intensive methods.

Of course, the benchmark we used was a set of structure-based alignments, so the reported accuracy relates to concerned regions of sequence alignments to structural features, ignoring gaps, as they are not structurally concerned. Stepping aside what this really means (as we rehearsed the arguments above), how humans are likely to cope with trying to assemble the information hidden in, and the meaning of, alignments of hundreds of sequences, and does it even make sense to try (Edgar, 2010)? And if it does, how will computers help us to achieve this?

When, what do we mean by “family?” The bioinformatics resource that us to consider this question most possibly is InterPro (refer Figure 3.4). So, let’s investigate a little further, with the notion of what we mean by the ‘family’ of GPCRs.

When we examine GPCR sequences, we see that they fall into a number of different superfamilies. Between the superfamilies, the degree of sequence similarity is negligible; what the proteins share, however, is a structural framework, formed by a bundle of TM domains, that allows the receptors to transduce extracellular signals into intracellular biochemical (see Boxes 5.2 and 5.3). CRCR superfamilies include the Glutamate receptors, Rhodopsin-like receptors, Adhesion receptors, Frizzled receptors and Secretin-like receptors (sometimes referred to as GRAFS) – these superfamilies constitute what is known as a clan, members of which are believed to have a common architecture and high-level function, but no sequence similarity. A taste of some of these relationships is given in Figure 5.13.

Using their distinct diagnostic approach, the protein family databases (PROSITE, PRINTS, Pfam, etc.) attempt to distinguish members of the different levels of the GPCR clan; ultimately, their results are pooled in the integrated protein family resource, Inter-Pro – with interesting connections.
