How much easier our lives be if all articles did this kind of backward work for us! At least for now, this remains a dream. The truth is that, while Shotton et's study showed that significant value could be added to articles post hoc, the enhancements were both platform- and browser-dependent, and were confined to just one article. Although asserting that the approaches weren't 'rocket science' (they exploited standard mark-up languages, ontologies, style-sheets, programmatic interfaces), the authors admitted that the work was immensely intensive, and would necessitate levels of attention to bring into mainstream publishing.

The obstacles to making knowledge hidden in published articles machine-accessible are legion. Most of the scientific literature is therefore unsemantic (and consequently, in Shotton's view, 'anthetical to the spirit of the Web); and it will doubtless be time before tools, policies and incentives to encourage authors to engage with this enormous challenge are finally in place. Notwithstanding the difficulties, however, inmates the gap between human- and machine-readable scientific content remains. The Internet and digital technologies have irrevocably changed the way in which scientific experts continue to use the literature, and publishers continue to seek innovations to satisfy the increasing demands of their authors and readers.

We've touched, on just a couple of the many approaches that have been used to enhance scientific documents, to make the data and knowledge they contain more accessible and reusable. Although no universal solution has yet materialised, some themes have emerged: most HTML or XML-based, most provide hyperlinks to external websites and term definitions from relevant ontologies, and so on. However, some researchers have imagined far-reaching possibilities, where data in online papers become 'more alive': Bourne (2005), for example, a dream of which readers could toggle a table of numerical values and their graphical representation, allowing immediate investigation of relationships latent in the data; or between a static image of a molecule and interactive 3D views, allowing real-time exploration of molecular interactions or active sites described in the paper, without the need for specialised knowledge of esoteric data-manipulation tools – perhaps the scenarios showcased in the 2009 article by Attwood et al.¹¹⁵, and described more in detail in the following sections.

It turns out, many of the article repositories, ontologies, machine-readable standards, and so on, already exist for integrating content with data in public databases. Why haven't publications benefited more from the opportunities offered by such resources? Part of the reason is probably simply that the community has grown up with static manuscripts: most researchers are just used to reading articles in PDF or HTML formats (Lynch, 2007) and don't see the need to change – breaking deep-rooted cultural habits is hard.
