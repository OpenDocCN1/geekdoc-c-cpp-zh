# 4.2 Annotating raw data

Annotation can mean many things to different people. For the purposes of discussion, we will use the term in the broadest sense to mean any kind of extra information that can add value to raw data in order to make it useful and reusable. In the previous section, we explored the process of reading sequences: adding start and stop codons, delineating introns and exons, and identifying and translating sequences, etc. We now want to consider how we might achieve this information it can be used again. Assuming our gene product is a real protein, we could show raw nucleotide sequence and its translation in a file, as shown in Figure 4.7. However, without labels to show where the nucleotide sequence comes from, and without any clues to indicate how to produce it, we would have to go through the entire process to determine which part, or parts, of the sequence.

(a) Nucleotide sequence and b) its protein product. In this naked form, the sequences alone aren't very useful. RNA, there may have regulatory regions, there could be splices, and so on. For a sequence with thousands of nucleotides, directly superposing on all the start and stop codons, the correct triplet reading frames, the intron-exon boundaries, regulatory regions, and so on, is impracticable. It probably makes more sense to leave the sequence in its raw form and add supplementary notes; but just how much information should we require? To start, it might be helpful to know what the sequence is and where it came from, in both terms of its location in the genomic sequence and its source origin. For the example above, we gain useful information from knowing that it is genomic DNA of human origin, and that is derived from the α-globin gene line, which is 1138 nucleotide bases in length:

> source: genomic DNA, alpha-globin gene line, bases 1–1138
> organism: Homo sapiens

This is useful, but it hasn't identified the primary transcript and the name of the translated protein. We could do this in the following way, so at a glance, that there are three exons in the primary transcript, laying bases between 98 and 929:

> primary transcript: 98–929 (exon 1, 98–230; exon 2, 348–551; exon 3, 692–929)
> product: alpha globin

By noting features like this, we add increasing value to our raw sequence, such that we can re- the information quickly and conveniently. The more notes, or annotations, the richer the content. But there's a catch. There are many different ways in which annotations could be arranged in a plan. If we want to use the information again in a particular way, how might we determine which to add; or, if we thought of other potential uses of the information (things that perhaps we don't want to do now, but might want to do in future), this might make us add the notes in a different way, or it might make us consider how to accommodate different kinds of annotation in future.
