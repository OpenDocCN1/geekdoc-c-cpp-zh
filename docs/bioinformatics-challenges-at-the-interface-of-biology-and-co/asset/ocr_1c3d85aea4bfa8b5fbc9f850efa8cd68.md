The database searched in 1991 (Swiss-Prot) contained 22,654 sequences; that in November 2015 (UniProtKB) contained 549,832 sequences. The consequences of ~24-fold increase in the size of the underlying sequence database are witnessed in the Numerical Results (NR). In 1991 (Figure 4.6(a)), the pattern matched 116 sequences: of these, 115 were correct, and one was false. In 2015 (Figure 4.6(b)), of 2,126 matches, 1,976 were matched correctly; 150 were incorrect; 449 family members were missed; and 32 partial matches (i.e., sequence fragments). These results illustrate that a pattern is not necessarily correct and a match is not necessarily incorrect. Moreover, taken together, the figures show that, during the last 20 years, the overall rate of true expression has degraded from <1 per cent to -30 per cent.

Nevertheless, the current expression represents the compromise between the ability to match true members (true-positive matches) and its tendency to match false members by mistake, while minimizing the number of true members that fail to fail to match (false negatives). This delicate balance has required successive revisions to the expression, to reflect the formerly witnessed in the growing sequence database. The tension between diagnostic tools to match sequences incorrectly and to fail to match members lies at the heart of efforts to improve the diagnostic potency of sequence analysis techniques (see Box 4.6). For consensus expressions in particular, false negatives are a fundamental limitation, but patterns can be optimised to recognise known sequence variants, which can't be identified if unknown, or previously unseen, variants — we can only know that a pattern has failed to match a sequence if we have prior knowledge about, or have gained additional information from other sources.
