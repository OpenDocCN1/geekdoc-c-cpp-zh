# Box 3.4 Fingerprints

The concept of a protein family pattern is illustrated below. The main feature that distinguishes a fingerprint from a consensus pattern (Box 3.3) is that it draws its diagnostic power from groups of conserved regions, rather than individual motifs. The potency of fingerprints derives from the fact that they not only use multiple motifs, but they inherently encapsulate information relating to the number of motifs they contain, to their order in the alignment, and to the distance or interval between them (as shown in Figure 3.7); the resulting discriminator is thus a powerful signature for the given protein family.

![](bbox=[0.176, 0.246, 0.823, 0.303])

Of course, different families are characterised by different patterns of conservation, and their fingerprints differently. Figure 3.8 shows a schematic illustration of fingerprints for three different families: the first two fingerprints contain four motifs, but their sizes and the distances between them are different; the third fingerprint contains five motifs, again with different sizes and inter-motif distances.

![](bbox=[0.176, 0.423, 0.823, 0.616])

An especially important feature of protein fingerprints is that a query sequence need not match all of its constituent motifs equally well, nor need all of the motifs to be able to diagnose it as a potential family member – in other words, fingerprints can tolerate mismatches within individual motifs and within the fingerprint as a whole.

In principle, any protein family that sufficiently well can be uniquely fingerprinted. The flexibility of the approach, with regard to how well practised motifs match the fingerprint, and how many motifs match, lends great analytical potential, and hence offers powerful diagnostic opportunities beyond those afforded by consensus patterns.

# 3.9 TrEMBL

By 1996, the first quakes that followed the explosion of whole-genome sequencing projects were felt. The impact was great for databases whose maintenance involved significant amounts of manual annotation; many were not able to adapt in time; many did not survive. Swiss-Prot was a survivor of the onslaught, but this required radically new philosophical approaches to be embraced and new processes to be adopted.
