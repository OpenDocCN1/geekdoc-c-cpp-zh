Instead, we leap forward in time, past all the technology-related creativity inspired by the Second World War, to the early 1960s, and the coining of the term 'computer science' by analyst George Forster. By this time, the computer had many of the properties associated with contemporary machines: a Central Processing Unit (CPU) also able to manipulate numbers represented in binary and to execute a program toograph manipulations; some form of memory to store and retrieve data; back-up devices to retrieve the contents of memory when the device was switched off; connective infrastructure to allow the machine to communicate with devices other than itself; and various forms of input and output (screens, keyboards, etc.) to allow users to interact with the machine.

The first computers focused on solving mathematical problems (like finding large numbers) for which no equation existed that could be 'solved' by a human with pen and paper, but for which iterative algorithms could be devised to played the computer's strength of being able to mindlessly 'crunch' numbers according to prescribed rules. Over time, programs were written to perform calculations that were relative to pragmatic ethics: calculating payrolls and other business tasks, simulating engineering problems, and recording and searching over data individuals for all manner of purposes. Today, computers numerous mobile devices, to autonomously guiding the trajectory of space probes visiting the outer reaches of our solar system.

Of course, this unsashamedely selfish, whirlwind tour of the history of computer science overlooks a multitude of incredible developments and technological innovations of the past few decades. On the face of it, many of these appear to be merely improvements to existing ideas, resulting in something that is just a bit faster, smaller, bigger, greener, lighter—insert the adjective of your choice here—than before. Many of these improvements, however, are not the result of 'trying a bit harder': development of the next generation of particular technology has required considerable research and creative effort to overcome or circumvent what were previously unreachable limits of engineering or physics. Other inventions have resulted in turning points in the way we work: the first machines 'cheap' enough to have on one's desk, or enough to run off a battery in one's pocket; the early Internet as connective infrastructure for commerce and research, and the Web as more friendly endroit, with much broader appeal. Recently, social networking, wireless and mobile technology, and the ability to create and distribute audio and video, have changed how society interacts in ways that were inconceivable only a few years ago. Many of these technologies are now so integrated into modern society that we fail to recognise they exist, much less the effort and thought that led to their creation.
