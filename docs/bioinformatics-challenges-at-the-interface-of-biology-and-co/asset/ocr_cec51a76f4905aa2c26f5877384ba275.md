General-purpose 'question-answering systems' that combine traditional keyword-based techniques with relevance-ranking, entity-recognition, data-mining, natural-language processing and ontologies are beginning to appear on the Web: Google's Knowledge Graph and Wolfram Alpha are perhaps the most prominent examples of such data. It is interesting to note that pasting our full sample question directly into Google Scholar returns, as in December 2015, a link to the paper by Goebel et al., 1996, which describes the 'Efficacy of a small dose of oral dexamethasone for outpatient coup'; Wolfram Alpha, on the other hand – admitted not optimised for biomedical queries – gets into a bit of a pickle, fails to understand the query, and instead decides that the best thing would be to provide a fascinating array of facts associated with the word efficient¹¹. Biomedical semantic search engines, like Quertle and GoPubMed (Dieze and Schroeder, 2009), attempt to apply these techniques which are likely to gain traction in the community. What is, however, that developing responsibility for finding relevant material to an algorithm – any algorithm carries some risk. The more complex the algorithm, the less we can be that it has done the right thing; more conservative approaches give us more manageable numbers of results, but how can we be that the most important thing hasn't been missed entirely?

In the move towards more 'intelligent' search engines, another – perhaps more dangerous – lies lies to trap the unruly scientist. Although systems that learn the kind of results might seem attractive (especially to online retailers wanting to sell products!), from a scientist's perspective, there is a fundamental difference between retrieving 'results that are relevant to my research' and 'results that reflect the way I think'. Whether algorithms can be created that these scenarios is an interesting question; however, as social networks and search engines attempt to record more detailed information about our browsing habits, personalised, subjective ranking of results is likely to become increasingly common, and for scientists, dangerously subtle: searches that systematically reinforce the ideas that a scientist holds are likely to miss papers with divergent or contradictory views, potentially biasing arising from the research, and hence systematically doping the scientific record with further biases.
