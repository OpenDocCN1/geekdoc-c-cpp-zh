Operator-circuiting is a method many languages use when computing Boolean (true or false) expressions — it amounts to the premature ending of evaluation when the result is known with certainty. Expressions can represent anything that might be true or false, and appear in one form or another in any program that has make decisions: they might be a given amino acid is hydrophobic or hydrophilic, the length of a protein sequence is larger than some threshold. For simplicity, in the following expressions, we replace these concepts with single-letter symbols, of which (A–H) reduce to either true or false:

+   A and B and C and D (1)
E or F or G or H (2)

Most programming languages would process expressions from left to right, taking each sub-expression (A-D, E-H) and evaluating its truth. Looking at example (1), we see that first A is evaluated, followed by B, by C, followed finally by D. However, if A resolves to false, the values B, C and D are irrelevant, the overall result is guaranteed to be false. In fact, as soon as we come across a sub-expression that evaluates to false, we become certain of the overall falsity of the expression. We can therefore skip the evaluation of those subsequent sub-expressions. Example (2) is similar: the first sub-expression to resolve allows us to short-circuit the subsequent evaluations, as it can be seen that the overall expression must then evaluate to true. Of course, if the sub-expressions A-H were Boolean variables, this would gain us much; however, it's the case that these sub-expressions are relatively heavyweight, such as a function call, or complex arithmetic. In these cases, skipping evaluation provides a powerful tool.

Again, we appeal to our knowledge of any application of logic; specifically, of how heavyweight operations are, and the likelihood of a given sub-expression being either true or false. Take example (3) below: we have four conditions that must be true in order for the expression to be true. In this case, we intend two functions, oft() and sel(), that depending on their input, return either true or false, and so can be used as conditions in logical statements:

oft(P,Q) and sel(R,S) and T and U (3)

For this example, say that oft() and sel() are both time-consuming functions of some arbitrary variables (P, Q, R and S); the other two T and U, are simple Boolean variables. How can we rewrite this expression to make it more efficient? The simplest improvement is to re-order the sub-expressions to ensure that expressions come first; this should lead to fewer evaluations of the subsequent, heavyweight expressions. This gives us the more efficient expression seen in example (4):

U and oft(P,Q) and sel(R,S) (4)
