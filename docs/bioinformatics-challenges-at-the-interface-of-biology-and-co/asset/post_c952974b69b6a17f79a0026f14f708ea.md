Clearly, the Swiss-Prot curators have made greater and greater use of the literature during the 27 years or since human rhodopsin first appeared in the database; and most of that research has been done manually. Given the enormous growth in the number of published life-science articles, this has had important consequences:

- 1 the growth of the ‘gold standard’ (manually annotated) content of UniProtKB (i.e., Swiss-Prot) has become incredibly slow relative to the growth in the number of of proteins requiring annotation in TremBL;
- 2 the proteins that can be manually annotated necessarily represent a small subset, biased towards for which experimental data have been published;
- 3 the facts that are gleaned by any one curator are necessarily just a fraction of the sum of the knowledge available in the entire body of life-science literature for any one protein;
- 4 once these ‘facts’ have been committed to a database, they’re difficult or impossible to keep routinely up-to-date;
- 5 if established ‘facts’ are later refuted in the literature, the database entries are difficult or impossible to correct; and perhaps most of all,
- 6 the bulk of the knowledge gained from this subset of manually researched literature has probably been lost due to the death of computational tools available to capture this kind of information explicitly.

## 8.4 A historical perspective on scientific literature

Against this background, and allied particularly to this last point, recent years have witnessed an increasing interest in the development of tools to extract pertinent information more efficiently from the literature, to relieve the manual burdens of database biocurrent in particular and of scientists. The scientific literature itself is full of references to the catastrophes that we face if we fail to harness the power of machines to do exactly this: e.g., we read about drowning in data floods (Andrade and Sander, 1997), deluges (Hess et al., 2001), surging oceans (Dubitzky, 2009), or tsunamis (Wurman, 1997), or being threatened by icebergs (Hodgson, 2001; How et al., 2008), avalanches (Antezana et al., 2009), earthquakes (Willbanks, 2007) or explosions (Diehn et al., 2003) of data.

Faced with such predictions of doom, it's tempting to see the problem of harnessing the vast bodies of amassed scientific knowledge as a new phenomenon; but throughout history, scientists have been demonstrably better at creating knowledge than putting in place effective mechanisms for disseminating, accessing and exploiting it. For example, even getting to the literature has never been straightforward. Foreshadowing current debates, access to libraries was harder than it should be:

> ...the Palatine library was viewed as an extension of the house; consequently, access to [libraries] was, or could be, practically restricted” (Dix, 1994).

Perhaps surprisingly, even the phrase ‘information overload’ precedes the Internet revolution by a decade (Toffler, 1970); and, for example, as early as 1967, Dayhoff, meaning the number of known sequences is growing everfaster (letter to C. Berkeley, 27 February, 1967). Although the scale and nature of the problems may have changed over time, it appears that there has always, for one reason or other, more knowledge recorded by mankind can collectively make sense.

Until the revolution was caused by physical restrictions – the length of time to manually types articles and ship them to libraries, or the need to physically travel to a library to read contents. Today’s technology effectively removes these bottlenecks, but introduces problems of its own. Undeniably, there is a growing issue of scale, which has been reported and analysed repeatedly in the literature (e.g., Atwood et al., 2009); Fraser and Dunstan, 2010). Although the details of various commenters, their conclusions are consistent: we are promising (data and literature) even before. In some senses, the problem of dealing with the overwhelming amount of available ‘stuff’ doesnt matter, as long as it is useful to us; however, the goal of creating extract to analyse needs tools that are sufficiently sophisticated to filter out stuff that’s both relevant and of high quality remains elusive – this inability to separate scientific wheat from chaff has been filter failure (Shirky, 2008).

Aside from scale, perhaps a more fundamental change has occurred in the way which scientific discoveries were communicated to scholarly societies as ‘letters’, and journals were really collections of what we now consider informal exchanges between scientists, contemporary scientific articles are increasingly formalised, and reliant on data to make their case.
