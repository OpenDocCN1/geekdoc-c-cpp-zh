## 3.11 UniProt

The year 2004 marked a turning point for the way in which protein-sequence data were to be collected and disseminated globally. As we saw in Section 3.6, the PIR-PSD had been available since 1986, and Swiss-Prot became available in the same year. In the years that followed, problems arose how to maintain such, disparate and independent resources when, world-wide funds for database maintenance were dwindling. The situation was exacerbated by the appearance of TrEMBL in 1996. For PIR, some of the difficulties were mitigated, in the early years, by charging for copies of their databases and online access to their software; while the funding changed, as the international cooperation with MIPS and JIPID, supported by National Science Foundation (NSF) and European grants, helped to sustain the resource.

Meanwhile, despite its huge popularity, Swiss-Prot was having a rocky road and had to be rescued from the brink of collapse. The database had fallen into a ‘Catch-22’ scenario: the Swiss government had declined to provide further financial support unless the database also gained additional or matching funds from a European Union (EU) grant, because they now viewed Swiss-Prot as an international resource; however, a joint proposal with the EBI for an EU infrastructure grant had been declined because Swiss-Prot was not supported by the Swiss government! In May 1996, with only two months of salary remaining when he announced that Swiss-Prot and its associated databases and software tools were to be terminated owing to lack of funds. His appeal stimulated a storm of protest of such force (not just on the Internet, but also in high-profile academic journals and the media) that forced the Swiss government to reconsider their compromise to offer interim funding until the end of the year. In the negotiations that followed, it was clear that a more stable vehicle for long-term funding of Swiss-Prot was needed; in the ensuing debate, draft plans emerged to establish what was to become the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics (Bairoch, 2000).

Eventually, the continued difficulties of supporting such protein sequence databases in Europe and the USA in a joint bid for multinational funding; from the NIH, the NSF, the Swiss federal government and the EU. Thus, in 2002, Swiss-Prot, TrEMBL and the PIR-PSD joined forces as the UniProt consortium. Aside from ameliorating their combined funding problems, the idea of forming a Universal Protein Resource (UniProt) was to build on the partners' many years of foundational work, to streamline their efforts by providing a stable, high-quality, unified database. This would serve as the most comprehensive protein sequence database, replete with accurate annotations and extensive cross-references, and accompanied by freely-available, easy-to-use query interfaces.

In fact, UniProt27 provides an umbrella for several different resources. Initially, it hosted three separate databases, tailored for specific uses (Apweiler et al., 2004):

- 1 The UniProt Archive (UniParC)28, which aims to provide a complete, non-redundant repository of all publicly protein sequence data, reflecting the history of sequences in the resource;
- 2 The UniProt Knowledge (UniProtKB)29, which consists of Swiss-Prot and TrEMBL, acting as the central access point for protein sequences, with accurate, consistent and rich annotation (relating to function, family classifications, disease associations, sequence features, etc.); and
- 3 The UniProt Reference Clusters, or NREF databases (UniRef)30, which provide nonredundant subsets of UniProtKB, for efficient search.

From a user perspective, despite including three different sequence sources, UniProtKB reflects the contents of only Swiss-Prot and TrEMBL. There are probably several reasons for this. As we have seen, Swiss-Prot became the facto gold protein sequence database, because of the quality of its manually-derived annotations; but it also had its origins in PIR-PSD data, and was largely redundant with it accessed via UniParc. Some of the issues involved in amalgamating databases from different sources to produce non-redundant composites are outlined in Box 3.5.

By 2011, UniProt also included a Metagenomic and Environmental Sequence component, named UniMES (The UniProt Consortium, 2011), and offered UniMES clusters to provide comprehensive coverage of sequence space at different resolutions. By February 2015, the combined UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot and UniProtKB/TrEMBL contained >91 million entries (although this number dropped to ~55 million entries that year). By November 2015, the implementation of stricter redundancy criteria earlier that year allowed several of the world's major databases to be merged, marked a significant step in standardising protein sequence data, and access to it, and use of, much more straightforward for users across the globe. Nevertheless the quality of protein sequence data, and the management, analysis and annotation headaches was creating, and with nothing compared to the revolution waiting in. An unprecedented torrent of nucleotide sequence data was beginning to be unleashed by a new breed of sequencing technology that heralded the world-wide initiation of what are now known as Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) projects.

## Box 3.5 Composite protein sequence databases

Although choice is usually a good thing, the abundance of databases can also complicate matters. Faced with a range of resources that contain similar information and do similar things, a user will want to know which is the most comprehensive or the most up-to-date - in short, which is the best to use. One to the proliferation of databases is to aggregate resources into a composite, removing much of the redundancy in order to make data storage and searching more efficient. Over the years, researchers saw the advantages of aggregating databases, which in turn gave to several composites, with different format and redundancy criteria.
