# 3.5 GenBank

Six months after the first release of the EMBL data library became the first public release of GenBank¹http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank

Politically, the situation in the USA was complicated. It took the best part of three years for an appropriate database-funding model to emerge from the US Institutes of Health (NIH), by which time the EMBL data library was already up and running under the direction of Greg Hamm. By then, three proposals remained on the table for NIH support; two of these were from Los Alamos (with Bolt, Berenek and Newman (BBN)), the other with IntelliGenics), and the third from NBRF. Although there was a general sense of relief that the decision had finally been made, the outcome surprised some members of the community (including, no doubt, Dayhoff himself). After all, the NBRF seemed like a more appropriate home for GenBank, given Dayhoff's successful track record as a curator of protein sequence data (Smith, 1990). More to the point, although Los Alamos undoubtedly offered excellent computer facilities, it was probably best known for its role in the creation of atomic weapons — this was an obvious environment to establish the nation's first public nucleotide sequence database. The crux of the matter lies in the different philosophical approaches embodied in the NBRF and Los Alamos proposals; in particular, each opposed different policies with regard to scientific priority, data sharing/privacy and intellectual property.

Dayhoff had to continue gathering sequences from literature sources and from bench scientists, and wasn't particularly interested in matters of provenance (Eck and Dayhoff, 1966); the Los Alamos team, by contrast, advocated collaboration with journal editors to deposit their sequences into the database as part of their routine publication process. This latter approach was especially compelling, as it would allow scientists to assert priority, and to keep their research results until formally published and their provenance established; perhaps more importantly, it was unencumbered by proprietary interest in the data. Unfortunately, Dayhoff would see in the next section, stock revenues from its sales (albeit only to cover costs); this we held against her, because allowing the data to become de-facto private to the ‘private hunting grounds’ of one group of researchers was considered contrary to the spirit of open access (Strasser, 2008). At the time, it was considered paramount that the data and associated software tools be free and open – that the highly secured area of what may have appeared to some as The Atomic City had been chosen as the home for the new international database.

As an aside, it's interesting that the vision of free data and software was advocated so strongly at this time, not least because there was no funding model to support it. Thirty years later, the same arguments are vehemently opposed with regard to free databases, free software and free literature (e.g., Lathrop et al., 2011); but even now, database funding remains an unresolved issue. As Olgaard put around 20 years ago, Free access to validated and enhanced data worldwide is a beautiful dream, but more complex (Kennard, 1997).

Returning to our theme, the final nail in the coffin of Dayhoff’s proposal was that NBRF had only limited means of data distribution (via modems). Los Alamos, on the other hand, had the enormous benefit of being able to distribute their data via ARPANET. Together, these advantages were sufficient to swing the pendulum in favour of the Los Alamos team, who became the custodians of GenBank. From its inception, from 1986 onwards, also with the DNA Nucleotide Sequence Database (DBBJ), the databases were not identical (each had its own format, naming convention, and on), the teams adopted common data-exchange protocols in order to provide quality.
