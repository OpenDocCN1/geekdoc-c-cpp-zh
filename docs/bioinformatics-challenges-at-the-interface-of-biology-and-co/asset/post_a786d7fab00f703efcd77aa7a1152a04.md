In practice, producing a non-redundant database in this way is challenging, because it can be hard to decide where to draw the line: should we eliminate sequences that are 99 percent similar, on the assumption that they are probably the same (only the difference, being a methionine residue)? Or is this too conservative, and should we choose 95 percent cut-off? No matter which approach we use, the likelihood is that a composite database will still contain some level of redundancy and some amount of error – this is because source databases contain non-atic (usually human) errors, which are very hard to trace and cannot be corrected automatically. Consider NR, the default database of the NCBI BLAST service (Chapter 4). This resource includes updates of Swiss-Prot and GenBank, however, its construction is fairly simple. This means that multiple copies of the same protein may be retained as a result of polymorphisms or sequencing errors; those that have been corrected in Swiss-Prot are re-translated back from nucleotide sequences to GenBank, and many duplicates are. The contents of the database are not redundant (strictly speaking, it is more appropriate to consider NR a non-identical database).

Redundancy issues are tackled more systematically in UniProt (which amalgamates Swiss-Prot, TREBL and PIR). In UniProt, similar sequences are clustered to yield representative sequences using three different identity cut-offs: the resulting resources are UniRef100, UniRef90 and UniRef50, no pair of sequences in the representative set has over 90 or 50 cent mutual identity; in UniRef, representative sequences and their sub-fragments are presented as a single entity. ## 3.12 The European Nucleotide Archive (ENA)

The method of sequencing pioneered by Sanger (Section 3.4) was the predominant approach for around 30 years. Although automation of the process made whole-genome sequencing possible, the timescales were long and the costs were high—the Human Genome Project¹, for example, took more than ten years to complete and was estimated to cost $3 billion. While the timescales and prices did come down, and scientists had access to more affordable data, their hunger for more data in 2005 fueled the development of a new technology, which was capable of an approximate 100-fold increase in raw sequence throughput over the Sanger method—this technology was able to sequence 25 million bases, with high fidelity, in a single 4-hour run (Marquier et al., 2005). Perhaps because the accuracy of the new technology had not yet proven in the field, and because of the considerable financial investment already made in Sanger technology, uptake by the community was initially slow, but nevertheless, inspired by some of the early NGS studies, including landmarks such as the analysis of the mammoth (Poincar et al., 2006) and Neanderthal (Green et al., 2006), recent years witnessed the blossoming of NGS sequencing projects. Today, the cost is such that NGS projects can be conducted outside national centres, in the laboratories of small academic groups—effectively, 'desktop' sequencing has become a reality.

The real motivation driving the sequencing revolution was to bring down the cost of sequencing a human genome enough to enable sequencing to become a feasible biomedical and clinical tool (e.g., in early diagnosis of diseases like cancer). Significant progress has been made, and the cost of human sequencing is now much lower than it was years ago. At the time of writing, sequencing could be completed with a single machine, processing more than 100 million samples per run in a week for ~$20,000. But this isn't enough, and the goal is to develop third-generation (3Gen) sequencing technologies that are able to provide high-quality human genome sequencing for $1,000. One thing is clear: when this target is reached, the data-generation landscape will be radically different from the one we see today.

Meanwhile, a mounting tsunami of nucleotide sequence data has gained force as NGS technologies have swept the globe. In 2013, there were estimated to be 2,000 sequencing instruments operating in laboratories worldwide, collectively to yield 15 quadrillion nucleotides per annum (i.e., 15 petabytes of data). It has been argued that quantities of data are unprecedented, but familiar in fields such as astronomy and high-energy physics—e.g., detectors at the Large Hadron Collider² produced ~13 petabytes of data. But there's a difference—in the latter fields, the data are generated by just a few centred instruments; genomic data, on the other hand, are appearing out of thousands—and soon, possibly, thousands of instruments distributed around the globe³ (Schatz and Langmead, 2013).

Several important developments emerged in the wake of this data onslaught. For example, it became clear that providing access not only to the most recent versions of sequences, but also the rush to patent genetic information made issues of the priority, and the need to sequence entries exactly as they appeared in the past, increasingly important. As a result, the EBI established a Sequence Version Archive (Leinonen et al., 2003), to store both current and earlier versions of entries in the EMBL data library (which changed its name to EMBL-Bank). By September 2004, EMBL-Bank grew proudly to >42 million entries (Kanz et al., 2005); by 2007, it was accompanied by the Ensembl Trace Archive (ETA) – the ETA was set up as a permanent archive for single-cell sequencing (Noonan et al., 2006), recent years witnessed the blossoming of NGS projects. Today, the cost is such that NGS projects can be conducted outside national sequencing, in the laboratories of small academic groups—effectively, 'desktop' sequencing has become a reality.

## 3.6 Organism-specific databases and Ensembl

The development of high-throughput technologies made that, producing the first bacterial genome in the mid-1990s. The sequencing frequency followed in 1995 and the first draft of the human genome in 2001, has continued unabated and has created in its numerous organism-specific databases. Some of the databases include (as explained in Chapter 1):

- FlyBase⁵ (the repository of Drosophila genes)
- WormBase⁶ (the database of mapping, sequencing and phenotypic information to C. elegans and nematodes)
- TAIR⁷ (a database of the biological and genetics data of yeast cerevisiae)
- Molecular biology data for the higher plant Arabidopsis thaliana)
- Ensembl⁸ (the system housing data from the Human Genome Project (HGP))

The Ensembl project (a joint operation of the EBI and the Well Trust Sanger Institute in the UK) began in 1999, before the human genome became available in draft form. Early on, it was obvious that manual annotation of the information contained in the sequence's three billion bps wouldn't be practical. The Ensembl system was thereafter conceived as a means of storing and automatically annot the genome, augmented with data from other sources. It was first launched on the Web July 2000.
