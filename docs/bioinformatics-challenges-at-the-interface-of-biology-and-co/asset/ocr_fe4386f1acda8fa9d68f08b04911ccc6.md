# Box 5.2 Membrane proteins

Cells and their organelles are shielded from their environments by membranes that are impervious to most biological structures. The protection afforded by such separation, compartmentalisation, is crucial, their internal reactions would be disrupted in unpredictable ways. Yet, biological processes are dynamic and depend on the regulated transfer of molecules between cells/organelles and their surroundings. Integral membrane proteins play critical roles as heroes, mediators, between the inside and outside of cells and their organelles, allowing them to react to external stimuli and translate them into specific biological and physiological responses. Among membrane proteins are three important classes – transporters, receptors and enzymes whose structural and functional differences are determined by how many times they span the membrane.

Transporters use complex, multi-TM architectures and electrochemical gradients, or energy from chemical reactions, to usher a variety of subunits in and out of cells, as illustrated schematically in Figure 5.6 (a) Enzymes (the molecules responsible for speeding up biological reactions) are typically not membrane bound; those that tend to have simpler, usually single-TM architectures, as shown in Figure 5.6 (b).

Receptors transport 'signals' rather than substates across membranes. Many have 7TM architectures, which normally mediate cell responses by binding a ligand of some kind G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), which belong to the general class of 7TM receptors, have various modes of activation, illustrated in Figure 5.7. Some binding ligands within their 7TM scaffolds (a); other binding ligands within N-terminal regions (b), which the ligand interacts with through extracellular loops (c); the others are unusual in having their ligands covalently within the TM framework. In each case, the change in the membrane or the change in conformation triggered by ligand binding, causes further conformational changes to percolate through the scaffold and disrupt the bound trimeric G protein. The dislodged G proteins disperse, in turn, terminating their message to other cellular components (d), stimulating intracellular signaling cascades.

Because the primary structures of membrane proteins usually snake back and forth the membrane (hence, some receptors are referred to as 'serpentine'), tell-tale signs are left in their sequences – i.e., hydrophobic regions around 20 residues long. The likelihood of a sequence being a membrane protein is therefore often predicted (inevitably, not always reliable e.g., see Wong et al., 2010) by the appearance of multiple TM domains – seven in the case of GPCRs.
