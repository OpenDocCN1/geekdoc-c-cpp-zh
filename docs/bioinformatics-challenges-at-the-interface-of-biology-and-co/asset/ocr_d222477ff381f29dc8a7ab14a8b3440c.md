The InChI defines a standard way of representing small molecules, and is frequently used to identify interactions between drugs and proteins. Although InChIs are (quite reasonably) identified, the term is in a different sense from which we've explored so far: rather a 'name' for a concept, InChIs are actually a way of explicitly encoding the atoms and bonds for a small molecule. Each InChI starts with the string 'InChI=' followed by the version number of the InChI specification to encode the molecule. The rest of the InChI is made up of a series of layers and sub-layers (some of which are optional) separated by '/' characters; these describe the structure and other properties of the molecule, such as its charge or stereochemistry. The standard for ethanol (CH3CH2OH) is:

```
InChI=1S/C2H6O/c1-2/hH2,2H
```

and a sufficiently practised human would be able to draw the chemical structure of the molecule by interpreting the characters that form the InChI. Because InChIs contain optional components, also the algorithm that generates InChIs is subject to revision, they are neither opaque, unique nor moreover, it's very dangerous to treat them as identifiers in the same as the other case studies we've looked at. Treat them instead as a compact representation of a molecule, akin to a FASTA file than a URI.

# 7.4 Representation data

The latter half of the 20th century saw most biological data being stored in human-readable 'flat' files, each with its own syntax and format. In the absence of software and hardware required for the graphical visualisation techniques we take for granted today, in a flat-file often simply had to be interpreted by the act of reading the file. In consequence, many databases made use of a single data-file for each of the three stages discussed above: the file transferred to the client was an exact copy of what was stored, and it was this file, free of modification, that the user then read directly. These first databases, by comparison with what's available now, trivially small quantities of relatively simple data, and so, and by length, this size-fits-all approach to storage, processing and interpreting data worked.

The fragmented evolution of bioinformatics during this time led to the introduction of many disparate-file formats: as each database came into being, a new format was designed to store and search its data; each such format compounded the problems to be faced by the next generation of bioinformaticians (there are more than 30 formats for protein sequence-related data; see Box 7.3). Most of these legacy formats fell well short of what we now consider good practice, in terms of stability, usability and ambiguity, and not, this is because they were designed to simultaneously fulfil the three roles of storage, transfer and presentation that, in bioinformatics, are best considered independently.

One example of the problems caused by conflating these comes from the fact that most computer monitors at that time were limited to an 80-character width: as the formats were to be interpreted by reading them directly off the screen, this led most files to use similar line-width restrictions.
