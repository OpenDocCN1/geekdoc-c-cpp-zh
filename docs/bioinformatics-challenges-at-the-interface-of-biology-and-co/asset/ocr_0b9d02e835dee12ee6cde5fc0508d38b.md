# 1.2.3 The seeds of bioinformatics

It's useful to think about where and when the seeds of bioinformatics were first sown, as this helps to provide a context for the situation we're in today. But where do we start? We could go all the way back to Franklin and Gosling's foundational work towards the elucidation of DNA (Franklin and Gosling, 1953a, b, c), or to Watson and Crick's opportunistic interpretation of their data (Watson and Crick, 1953). We could focus on the painstaking work of Sanger, who in 1955, determined the amino acid sequence of the peptide hormone. We could consider the ground-breaking work of Kendrew (1958) and offold and Perutz (1963) in determining the 3-dimensional (3D) structures of proteins. We could go fast-forward to the progenitors of the first databases of molecular structures and sequences in the mid-1960s and early 1970s. This was a clearly fertile era, heralding some of the most significant advances in molecular biology, and leading to the award of several Nobel Prizes (e.g., 1958, Sanger's prize in chemistry; 1962, Watson, Crick and Wilkins' shared prize in chemistry). These advances, any one which could provide a suitable stepping-point, each played an important part in the unfolding story.

Because this book focuses on biological sequences, especially protein sequences, we have chosen Fred Sanger's pioneering work on the peptide hormone to elucidate the order of amino acids in the primary structure of a protein. This was an immensely difficult puzzle, requiring the use of a range of chemical and enzymatic techniques in a variety of experiments over many years. Each step of this incremental process was successfully new and exciting to warrant a separate publication. Eventually, around ten papers detailed the intricate work that led to the elucidation, first of bovine insulin (e.g., Sanger, 1945; Sanger and Tby, 1953a, b; Sanger and Thompson, 1953b; Sanger et al., 1955; Ryu et al., 1955), and that of porcine and rabbit insulins (Brown et al., 1955). This monumental achievement had taken decades to complete. It seems incredible how that such a small protein sequence (containing only ~50 amino acids – see Figure 1.1) could have resisted so many experimental assays and withheld its secrets for so long; and more, that its 3D structure would not be known for a further 14 years (Adams et al., 1969).

Manual sequencing before the sequence of the next protein was deduced: this a small enzyme called ribonuclease. Work began on ribonuclease in 1955. Following a series of preliminary studies, the first 'draft sequence' was published in 1960 (Hirs et al., 1960), and a carefully refined final version was published three years later (Smyth et al., 1963). Importantly, this eight-year project offered a stepping-stone towards elucidation of the protein's 3D structure: in fact, knowledge of a 3D jigsaw puzzle that was to take a further four years to solve (Wycock et al., 1967). Viewed in the light of today's high-throughput sequence and structure determinations, these time-scales seem unimaginaginably slow.
