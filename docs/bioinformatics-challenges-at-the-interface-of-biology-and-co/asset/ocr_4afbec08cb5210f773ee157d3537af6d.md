Aligning proteins in 3D is essentially different from this 2D-grid approach. Based on the geometric locations of Ca atoms in the polypeptide backbone, structural alignment programs tend to produce rigid-body transformations that align the structures in space, from which they generate sequence alignments with apparently homologous residue equivalences (Daniels et al., 2012). Clearly, the more divergent structures are, the more difficult it is to assess structural equivalence, and

there are no unique criteria for structural similarity of individual residues; rather, structural equivalence must be defined in the context of a particular alignment protocol and requires arbitrary parameters as distance contours (Ectard, 2012).

To get a better feel for what this might mean in practice, return again to the example of two sequences which have different lengths. Recall that in the 2D grid, we simply inserted gaps to bring 'equivalent' residues into register. In 3D, however, sequence-length discrepancies manifest themselves as slightly longer or shorter helices, slightly longer or shorter strands or shorter loops – there are no gaps in protein structures. Invertructure-based and sequence-based alignments are not the same thing; the former employs a 3D-model of comparison, the latter a 2D model in which gaps have a very specific role. When comparing proteins of different length, the gaps that sequence alignment tend to accumulate in regions corresponding to loops, Sometimes, insertions appear within alignment regions that correspond to secondary structures, the sequences of which are produced.

Consider an insertion of a single residue within an α-helix. In sequence alignment, this would demand a gap to be added to the sequence of the shorter helix, in order to bring equivalent downstream into register with those of the longer sequence. In the structure-based alignment, however, such insertion results in a 100° shift (in terms of angular separation – recall, α-helices contain 3.6 residues per 360° turn) of downstream residues; thus, ‘equivalent’ residues in the rest of the helix become mismatched.
