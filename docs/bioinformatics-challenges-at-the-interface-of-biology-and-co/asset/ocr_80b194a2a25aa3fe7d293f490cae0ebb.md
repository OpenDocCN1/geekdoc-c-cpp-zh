# 5.4 Issues with homology

Inference of homology is fundamental to the process of sequence annotation. Unfortunately, the meaning of this term is widely misunderstood, and has consequently been misused, polluting the literature with statements that equate homology and similarity – they are not the same. Sequences are said to be homologous if they are related by divergence from a common ancestor. A related term, analogy, denotes the acquisition of certain functions (folds or functions) via convergent evolution from unannotated ancestors: e.g., β-barrels in a variety of proteins (trypsin, the Tubby protein, etc.), despite their similar architectures, share no sequence or functional similarity; the enzymes chymotrypsin and subtilis share catalytic residues, with near-identical spatial geometries, but no other sequence or structural similarities (e.g., chymotrypsin and subtilisin in a 3-layer architecture).

The important point is that homology is not a similarity; it is an statement that sequences have a divergent rather than a convergent evolutionary relationship – unlike similarity, it is not quantitative. If we use the term “homology” when we really mean similarity (e.g., these sequences share 5 per cent homology), then we are pre-supposing a homologous relationship that is only 5 per cent amino acid identity, but they may not be homologous which could easily arise by chance.

Database search tools identify quantify similarity between sequences; it is for users of the tools to ascertain, whether the sequences are likely to have derived from a common ancestor. The same arguments apply when comparing structures: structures may be similar, but their common evolutionary origin is a hypothesis that then needs to be verified with additional evidence. When we compare homology and similarity, things are bound to go wrong. In the past, investigations of globular proteins led to the “common sequence/function/ancestry” paradigm by considering packing of hydrophobic cores. A given protein fold will have a hydrophobic interior; thus, two proteins sharing the same fold are frankly likely to share similar hydrophobic interactions. For hydrophobic segments of non-globular proteins, however, the development of membrane protein structures has meant that the implications of similarity haven’t been studied to the same extent; nevertheless, homology considerations have been ‘extended’ to Wong et al. (2010). This interest has particular ramifications for membrane-spanning signal peptide TM helices, where sequence similarity necessarily results from physical requirements rather than ancestry. Signaling of peptide TM regions thus the creation of matching hydrophobic cores (Wong et al., 2010), which may in turn lead to incorrect annotation – in fact, these authors discovered >1,000 problems of this in Pfam 23.0.

Another aspect concerning sequence analysis is that we can recognise different types of homologous. At a simplistic level, we define orthologs and paralogs: orthologues are proteins that have arisen following speciation events – hence, they usually perform the same function in different species; whereas on the other hand, arising via gene-duplication events – hence, they do perform related functions within one genome. The study of orthologues allows us to explain why paralogs allow to to changes in a given protein across species, as each duplicated gene follows a separate evolutionary pathway, allowing new species to evolve through variation and adaptation. Complexity presents present significant challenges for bioinformatics. For example, following a database search, how functional annotation can be inherited by a query?

In the past, many automatic analysis systems operated on the assumption that it was sufficient to transfer the annotation from the best-matching database to a query sequence. But how can we be sure that the best match is the true ortholog and not a paralog, whose function might be slightly different? Unfortunately, many have don’t consider the functionality engendered by divergent evolution, have spawned numerous annotation errors in databases.
