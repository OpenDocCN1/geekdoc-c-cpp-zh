# Chapter 8 Linking data and scientific literature

Faced with such predictions of doom, it's tempting to see the problem of harnessing the vast bodies of amassed scientific knowledge as a new phenomenon; but throughout history, scientists have been demonstrably better at creating knowledge than putting in place effective mechanisms for disseminating, accessing and exploiting it. For example, even getting to the literature has never been straightforward. Foreshadowing current debates amounts ‘Open Access’, as far back as the ‘public’ libraries of Ancient Rome, scholars and others were complaining that getting at the content of libraries was harder than it should be:

> ...the Palatine library was viewed as practically an extension of the house of Augustus; consequently, it must have been possible to control physical access to the library building when the Emperor was expected to be. Paradoxically, then,... access to [libraries] was, or could be, restricted” (Dix, 1994).

Perhaps surprisingly, even the phrase ‘information overload’ precedes the Internet revolution by a decade (Toffler, 1970); and, for example, as early as 1967, Dayhoff, meaning the huge challenge of keeping Atlas up-to-date, observed ‘the number of known sequences is growing everfaster’ (letter to C. Berkeley, 27 February, 1967). Although the scale and nature of the problems may have changed over time (e.g., the deluge threatening overwhelming Dayhoff amounted to a veritable double of 65 sequences!), it appears that there has always, for one reason or another, more knowledge recorded by mankind can collectively make sense.

Until the revolution was caused by physical restrictions – the length of time to manually types articles and ship them to libraries, or the need to physically travel to a library to read their contents. Today’s technology effectively removes these bottlenecks, but introduces problems of its own. Undeniably, there is a growing issue of scale, which has been reported and analysed repeatedly in the literature (e.g., Atwood et al., 2009); Fraser and Dunstan, 2010). Although the details of various commenters, their conclusions are consistent: we are promising (data and literature) even before; the rate of publishing is increasing; and the tools available to make sense of the growing body of knowledge continue to lag behind our needs. In some senses, the problem of dealing with the overwhelming amount of available ‘stuff’ doesnt matter, as long as it is useful to us; however, the goal of creating extract to analyse needs tools that are sufficiently sophisticated to filter out stuff that’s both relevant and of high quality remains elusive – this inability to separate scientific wheat from chaff has been filter failure (Shirky, 2008). Later in this chapter, we will examine some potential ways forward, but time will tell whether these are likely to mature real ‘solutions’

Aside from scale, perhaps a more fundamental change has occurred in the way which scientific discoveries were communicated to scholarly societies as ‘letters’, and journals were really collections of what we now consider informal exchanges between scientists, contemporary scientific articles are increasingly formalised, and reliant on data to make their case.
