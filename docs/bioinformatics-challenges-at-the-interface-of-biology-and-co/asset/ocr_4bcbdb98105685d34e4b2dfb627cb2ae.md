+   1. Repeatable - run the experiment again. Enough information for the original researcher or others to be able to repeat the experiment, years later, in order to verify the results or validate the experimental design. This also helps the repetition of experiments demanded by data intensive research.

+   2. Reproducible - enough information for an independent to start with the description of the experiment and if a result can be reproduced (or replicate) for someone else - one of the tenets of the scientific method as we know. One experiment may call upon another, and assembling these methods as new experiments we can conduct research, and ask questions, at a higher level.

+   3. Usable - aside as part of new experiments.

+   4. Repurable - uses the parts in a new experiment. An experiment which is a black box is only reusable as a black box. By opening the lid we find combinations of parts, available for reuse, and the way they are assembled can be reassembled.

+   5. Reliable - to trust the Research Object when it is used to verify and validate it, and derive a measure of trust of the results and methods it contains. It must also be resilient 'in the world and subject to regulatory review.

+   6. Referenceable - it is essential for a Research Object to have an identifier for it to be referred programmatically, so we can cite it and use properly. Implicit in this is versioning.

+   7. Reinterpretable - useful in and and across different research communities. Papers are aimed at one target audience but we do not have constraint with Research Objects. This relates to the nature of Boundary Objects.

+   8. Respectful and Respectable - with attention to credit and attribution for the component parts and methods, and to the flow of intellectual property in generation of data and privacy, with incentives for reuse.

+   10. Repeatable - a comprehensive record enables us to go back and see what has happened. The ability to repeat (rather than replace) the experiment, and to focus on crucial details, is essential for human understanding of what happened in experiments that might occur in nanoseconds or years.

+   11. Refreshable - updating a Research Object with when something changes, like when next year's figures come through

# # 8.7 Data publishing

Historically, scientists have tended to keep their research data secure under wraps until the time they can publish their results in a scholarly journal, cocooned in a suitable erudite narrative, so they can receive appropriate approbation from the community. The fear of being 'scooped', of seeing others making insightful leaps to exciting discoveries from one's own hard-won experimental results – and getting not completely unheard. We only have to remember Watson and Crick's controversial exploitation of Rosalind Franklin's X-ray data to determine the structure of DNA (made possible without her, Wilkins gave Crick a copy of her MRC research report), and recall that Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize in 1962 for their work on nucleic acids (after Franklin's), to understand that scooping can have significant ramifications.
