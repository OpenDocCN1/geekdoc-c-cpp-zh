In practice, 30D for the majority of newly determined sequences has been inferred by comparison with proteins whose structures have already been determined. For the millions of sequences now pouring from genome-sequencing projects, high-throughput structural initiatives will remain a vital part of the continued efforts to augment the PDB and provide structural insights on a large scale.
The PDB was created in the era that interest in collecting protein sequences was growing. With the sequence repositories, the database evolved long before its format is therefore a reflection of the technology current at that time. As a result, the 40 years have born a patchwork of developments to try to make the data more self-contained, and to try to make the format sufficiently robust to sustain its usefulness in the 21st century.

# 3.4 The EMBL nucleotide sequence data library

Despite the advances in protein sequence- and structure-determination technologies between the mid-1940s and 1970s, sequencing nucleic acids had remained problematic. The key issues related to size and ease of molecular purification. It was possible to sequence tRNAs, mostly because they are short (typically less than 100 bases long), and hence individual molecules could be purified; but chromosomal DNA molecules are in a completely different bulk-part, containing millions of bases. Even if such molecules could be broken into smaller chunks, the longest fragment that could then have been sequenced in a single experiment was ~500bp; and yields of potentially reaching a half million chromosomes were simply beyond the technology of the day.

Progress on these problems came in the mid 1970s, when Sanger developed the Sanger method that made it possible to work with much longer nucleotide fragments. The new technique allowed completion of the sequencing of the 5,385 bases of the single-bacteriophage φX14 (Sanger et al., 1977), and subsequently permitted rapid and accurate sequencing of even longer fragments (another achievement of significant merit, as we saw in Chapter 1, to earn him his Nobel Prize in Chemistry). With this approach, Sanger went to sequence human mitochondrial DNA (Anderson et al., 1981) and bacteriophage λ (Sanger et al., 1982). Landmarks in achievements (see Table 3,4), these projects also provided the first evidence of the phenomenon of overlapping gene sequences and of the non-universality of the genetic code, mentioned in Chapter 2 (Sanger, 1988; Dobson, 2005). But was automation of DNA sequencing techniques from the mid-1980s that catalysed a major leap forward: this not only significantly increased productivity, but also made much larger genomes (especially the human) realistic sequencing targets.

These advances proved for a revolution that was to rock the foundations of molecular biology and make the gathered fruits of all sequencing efforts before it appeared entirely inconsequential. Together, they created a dramatic turning point: for the first time, dawned on scientists that the machines were shunting away from data production per se and onto the requirements of data management:

the rate limiting step in the process of nucleic acid sequencing is now shifting from data acquisition towards the organization and analysis of that data (Gingeras and Roberts, 1980).
