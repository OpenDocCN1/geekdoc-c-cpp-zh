We ignore this at our peril, otherwise visualisations are likely to be confusing or wrong, to conceal information, or to be pretty pictures – ultimately, to be effective, visualisations must be imbued with biological meaning.

## 5.8.4 How is my alignment?

Another interesting feature of Figure 5.11 is the lack of correspondence between the sequence alignment and the structural annotations: helices 4, 9 and 12 gaps. Perhaps rather fittingly, this brings us back full circle where we begin this section, with the observation that different alignment methods produce different results. We see examples of disparate alignments produced by tools such as ClustalW, T-Coffee, MUSCLE and so on; we get first hints that not all alignments are equal. Crucially, however, it isn't just a question of the alignment order or parameters – the conceptual framework that motivates and the basis for alignment is also important. If we overlook this critical point, problems are inevitable; for example, when we compare sequence- and structure-based alignments, we force ourselves to think about why this is so, so we emphasize that comparison of sequence- and structure-based alignments is common practice in bioinformatics.

Before we think this is because we're often attempting to understand the evolutionary histories of proteins, and proteins are generally known to retain structural similarity when their sequences are highly divergent. In principle, we could be a little more certain that residues from different proteins are homologous, if we can see them overlap in 3D (Lantoro et al., 2012); structure-based alignments have been considered the 'gold standard' (Daniels et al., 2012) – it was this that provided the impetus for benchmarking not just the quality of sequence-alignment algorithms, structure-based data-sets and their outputs, with reference to alleged gold-standard data-sets (e.g. Edgar, 2010; Lantoro et al., 2012, and references therein). In fact, Edgar has suggested that publication of the first benchmark data-sets, AlibiBase (Thompson et al., 1999), triggered a benchmark war³ (Edgar, 2010). He observed that:

Structure has intrinsic limitations as a standard for residue alignment, and hence that evaluation of alignment quality is more challenging than generally realized, and skepticism is appropriate for claims that method rankings can be reliably measured by current benchmarks (Edgar, 2010).

Let's now consider some of the reasons why. Normally, when we align sequences, we do so by creating a 2D grid or matrix, which we populate row by row, with the one-letter codes that represent the constituent amino acids (or bases, if we're aligning DNA or RNA) of the sequences we want to compare. When sequences have different lengths, we simply accommodate the discrepancy by adding gap characters, in order that equivalent residues can be brought into register – the operative word here is equivalent. When we stack one residue beneath another in an alignment grid, we're making some sort of assertion about their relationship: e.g., we believe they are structurally equivalent (have similar structural contexts), or we don't think they're equivalent at all, but just wish (or need) to represent them in this way for convenience. Different analysis tools, different alignment algorithms, assume different kinds of equivalence – and we need to understand this if we're able to interpret their outputs meaningfully. In the context of a 2D alignment grid, this means, for example, that we can align two residues

Aligning proteins in 3D is essentially different from this 2D-grid approach. Based on the geometric locations of Ca atoms in the polypeptide backbone, structural alignment programs tend to produce rigid-body transformations that align the structures in space, from which they generate sequence alignments with apparently homologous residue equivalences (Daniels et al., 2012). Clearly, the more divergent structures are, the more difficult it is to assess structural equivalence, and there are no unique criteria for structural similarity of individual residues; rather, structural equivalence must be defined in the context of a particular alignment protocol and requires arbitrary parameters as distance contours (Ectard, 2012).

To get a better feel for what this might mean in practice, return again to the example of two sequences which have different lengths. Recall that in the 2D grid, we simply inserted gaps to bring 'equivalent' residues into register. In 3D, however, sequence-length discrepancies manifest themselves as longer or shorter helices, longer or shorter strands or shorter loops – there are no gaps in protein structures. Structure-based and sequence-based alignments are not the same thing; the former employs a 3D-model of comparison, the latter a 2D model in which gaps have a very specific role. Consider an insertion of a single residue within an α-helix. In sequence alignment, this would demand a gap to be added to the sequence of the shorter helix, in order to bring equivalent downstream into register with those of the longer sequence. In the structure-based alignment, however, such insertion results in a 100° shift (in terms of angular separation – recall, α-helices contain 3.6 residues per 360° turn) of downstream residues; thus, ‘equivalent’ residues in the rest of the helix become mismatched. Structural, then, the consequences of even small insertions are significant. Another extreme example is α-helix 12 in Figure 5.11, which we’ve highlighted in Figure 5.12.

Using ClustW, seven residues have been inserted into the GCR2 sequence relative to the Nis helix.
