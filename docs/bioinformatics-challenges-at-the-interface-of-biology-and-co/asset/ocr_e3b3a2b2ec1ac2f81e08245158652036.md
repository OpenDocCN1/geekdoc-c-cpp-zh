The picture they paint is: maintaining public resources is a huge, complex, inter-minable task, a task that is much harder than it should, because the community
aoids using standards, and because funding-bodies don't provide enough money to
sustain the work!

One way to address the financial issues is to raise funds commercially, for\example by ‘dual licensing’: i.e., providing a resource for free users but academic
charging for it (or for associated consultancy services) in say, a commercial\environment. This is difficult for a number of reasons. First, defining what is meanty ‘commercial use’ can be hard; it might be that a privately owned pharma-company is ‘commercial’, but the status of many universities and research
institutions is often more complex. Second, policing such agreements is challenging,
an often relies on commercial organisations ‘doing the right thing’ and offering a
in return for using a resource that they could easily obtain for free (this ‘trust-based'
model worked well for a number of years for Swiss-Prot, but later had to
change), when NIH funding obtained to support it situated that the database
is made freely available. Third, and perhaps most importantly, it relies on the
resource’s creators (often academics) being sufficiently entrepreneurial to make such
an approach. There’s no doubt that such models can change. Founded in 1993,
Red Hat Inc., made a successful business providing commercial-port for its, open-source Linux distribution; but the number of companies that
hieved stability off the back of free software is small compared to those with
titional business models. In any case, it could be argued that scientists are
perhaps best to focus on science, rather than attempting to innovate in the dogeat of big business.

A seductive alternative view is that any sufficiently important and open resource
be maintained by the community. The reality seems to do that is also hard, and works only rarely and
then only for the most prominent projects. The foundations that maintain Wikipedia\and Firefox, for example, two of the world’s most prominent resources, are in a
constant state of fund-raising from users to keep these important projects alive; very
fewer projects have the resources necessary to be able to manage such major
fund-raising initiatives.

On balance, it’s hard to avoid the conclusion that the only reliable way to keep cyber-infrastructure in good shape is for it to be founded alongside core research that relies
so fundamentally upon it. The difficulty here is that most funding agencies have mission
statements that speak about improving the world through fundamental research:
mission of the NIH, for example, is

to seek fundamental knowledge about the nature and behavior of living systems and the
application of that knowledge to enhance health, lengthen life, and reduce illness and dis-ability.
