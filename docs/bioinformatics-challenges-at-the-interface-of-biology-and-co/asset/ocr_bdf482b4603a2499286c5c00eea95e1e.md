# Box 7.3 File proliferation

Standards are such a good thing that everyone wants to have! In most areas of bioinformatics, it is common to come across a legacy of file formats that store essentially the same types of information; having been developed by different institutions to fulfil their specific requirements, at a time when no standards existed for such things, most are mutually incompatible, despite being put to similar uses.

The area of sequence analysis is a case in point, having more than 30 different file formats for handling sequence-related information; many are variations on a theme or valentines of others; for some, their specifications are such that it's impossible for a computer (or a human) to tell them apart. Some of these file formats are listed in Table 7.2 (many more are listed in the EMBOSS database handbook, 2009). The highlighted green, are sequence database formats (‘swish’ and ‘experiment’ are derivatives of the EMBL entry format); at the centre of the table, highlighted blue, are input to or from sequence alignment tools; and at the bottom of the table, highlighted blue, are formats for storing multiple sequences (many of derivatives of the original NBRF/PIR format).

In the absence of any agreed standards, the consequence of this file-format proliferation means that tools are forced, at some considerable implementation cost, to support multiple formats for reading and writing data to achieve the most basic kind of interoperability with other systems – and all of these that routine bioinformatics tasks are consequently trickier and more convoluted than they should be.
