# Box 2.7 The role of tRNA in translation

A key molecule in translation is transfer RNA (tRNA), a single-stranded molecule in which base-pairing between short regions of complementary sequence within the strand give rise to stem-or hairpin-loop-like structures. These are most familiar to us in tRNAs characteristic clover-leaf structure, as depicted in Figure 2.18.

![](bbox=[0.237, 0.23, 0.693, 0.502])

The three bases at the heart of the central hairpin loop of the clover-leaf are termed the anticodon. tRNA acts as an adapter molecule. The anticodon connects to the mRNA and the growing protein chain. The sequence here, whose codon is AGC (reading from 5' to 3'), is that of the tRNA for alanine, otherwise referred to as tRNA-Ala (or tRNAAla). This can form complementary base pairs with the codon sequence GCU (reading from5' to 3' in mRNA), which is a codon for alanine in the standard genetic code, as shown below (remember, reading codons in mRNA (5' to 3') requires anticodon to bind in the opposite direction):

| CGA tRNA |
| -GC U - mRNA |

Organisms possess populations of tRNA that can base-pair with the 61 codons that encode amino acids. While these tRNAs have different anticons, and a variety of other sequence differences, all exhibit the same clover-leaf structure.
Many tRNAs can pair with more than one codon, due to the flexibility of the pairing rules at the third position in the codon, which has been dubbed wobble. The number of tRNAs varies widely between organisms. In eukaryotes, wobble tends to be less frequent than in prokaryotes, and hence a greater number of distinct tRNAs are required.
