General-purpose 'question-answering systems' that combine traditional keyword-based techniques with relevance-ranking, entity-recognition, data-mining, natural-language processing and ontologies are beginning to appear on the Web: Google's Knowledge Graph and Wolfram Alpha are perhaps the most prominent examples of such data. It is interesting to note that pasting our full sample question directly into Google Scholar returns, as in December 2015, a link to the paper by Goebel et al., 1996, which describes the 'Efficacy of a small dose of oral dexamethasone for outpatient cough'; Wolfram Alpha, on the other hand – admitted not optimised for biomedical queries – gets into a bit of a pickle, fails to understand the query, and instead decides that the best thing would be to provide a fascinating array of facts associated with the word efficient¹. Biomedical semantic search engines, like Queryle and GoPubMed (Dieze and Schroeder, 2009), attempt to apply these techniques which are likely to gain traction in the community. What is, however, that developing responsibility for finding relevant material to an algorithm – any algorithm carries some risk. The more complex the algorithm, the less we can be that it has done the right thing; more conservative approaches give us more manageable numbers of results, but how can we be that the most important thing hasn't been missed entirely?

In the move towards more 'intelligent' search engines, another – perhaps more dangerous – lies lies to trap the unruly scientist. Although systems that learn the kind of results might seem attractive (especially to online retailers wanting to sell products!), from a scientist's perspective, there is a fundamental difference between retrieving 'results that are relevant to my research' and 'results that reflect the way I think'. Whether algorithms can be created that match these scenarios is an interesting question; however, as social networks and search engines attempt to record more detailed information about our browsing habits, personalised, subjective ranking of results is likely to become increasingly common, and for scientists, dangerously subtle: searches that systematically reinforce the ideas that a scientist holds are likely to miss papers with divergent or contradictory views, potentially biasing arising from the research, and hence systematically doping the scientific record with further biases.

# 8.9 Semantic publication

Up now, we've looked at some of the problems of getting machines to read natural language, and we've looked at some examples of how hard it is for humans to write pure logic. A compromise that has been proposed in various queries makes it possible for authors to identify, and make machine-readable, the main scientific assertions embodied in their articles. Superficially, this seems like a sensible approach, given that a fair proportion of any paper is 'padding' of one sort or another – the kind of diligence of scientific writing, including abstracts, summaries, literature surveys, acknowledgments and so on.

The question is, how to achieve this? As we've discussed, doing so reliably in an automated, algorithmic way is extremely hard. For any piece of text, even if we could robust identify the various entities and processes mentioned in its sentences, and interpret its graph with enough accuracy to collate the various assertions, would we meanfully work out of which are part of the ‘scientific claims’ of the paper, and are they mere-products of scholarly writing?

The alternative to identifying assertions algorithmically is to enlist the help of article authors, knowledgeable editors, or perhaps the ‘community’ – so-called ‘crowdsourcing’ of information has, after all, been successfully used in many other areas. Getting authors of scientific papers to embrace the vision of producing content that is semantically rich, however, has so far proved difficult: they seldom offer official accession numbers or unique identifiers for the entities described in their papers, or part with anything but the barest minimum of data for ‘auxiliary materials’; and the prospect of them proving their core assertions in machine-friendly forms is, for the time being, almost unimaginable. For that of the resistance is cultural – in the main, authors have never had to do this before, now they see or understand the benefits of doing so now have the time to learn; part also comes down to journal policy, or lack of journal policy – if journals do not uphold standards and best practices, authors are unlikely to comply. Consequently, the various recent initiatives that have tried to increase the semantic richness of articles with varying levels of success. Let’s take a closer look.

## 8.9.1 Making articles ‘semantic'

One of the first attempts to make articles more ‘semantic’ (in the sense of being ‘readable’ by both human and machine) was the so-called FEBS Letters experiment. This was a pilot project in which the journal editors worked with a small group of authors and with the curators of the MINT molecular interaction database to integrate data in published articles with information stored in databases (Serhang and Gerstein, 2008). To make the project tractable, the initial focus was on identifying and highlighting Protein-Protein Interactions (PPIs) and Post-Translational Modifications (PTMs), as attempting to make all the biological information and concepts described in any given article, or set of articles, machine-readable was obviously impossible (Superga et al., 2008).

At the core of the experiment was the structure of the Structured Digital Abstract (S) – this was a ‘simple’ mechanism for capturing key facts in a machine-readable, XML-encoded summary, to render those facts using text-mining tools (Serheng and Gerstein, 2007). The process was straightforward: participating authors were simply asked to use Excel spreadsheets to collect PPI and PTM data, together with a range of experimental, biological and technical details referred to in their articles (information about the nature of the experimental evidence, characteristics of the interacting proteins and their biological roles, the PTMs required for interaction, protein identifiers with links to MINT and UniProtKB, etc. (Ceol et al, 2008)). The stated formulation makes fairly uncomfortable, but is necessary if the facts described in the paper are to be rendered computer-accessible. Inevitably, most of the encoded relationships in the paper to MINT entries because the pilot project was a collaboration between the Journal and the MINT curators. Nevertheless, this was to design the system that could be sufficiently robust to readily generalise other biological relationships, and would be sufficiently easy to use wide take-up.

In spite of the alleged simplicity of the approach, the experience of processing a handful of manuscripts was encouraging (Ceol et al., 2008). Only five authors agreed to participate, and although most of these needed very little to complete their spreadsheets, they needed significant assistance from the MINT curators. After completion of the pilot, take-up was slow, and in the following 10 months, 90 FEBS Letters were published with SDAs (Shutton et al., 2009). Ultimately, were they were to have judged a success, SDAs would become an integral part of MEDLINE abstracts. However, this hasn't happened, and most FEBS Letters articles still don't contain SDAs (to give an idea, of the 536 articles published in the 10 months to 2 May 2014, 77 SDAs).
