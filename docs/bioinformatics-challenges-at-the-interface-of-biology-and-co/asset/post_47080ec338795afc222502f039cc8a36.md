When searching PROSITE, these frequently occurring patterns may be 'switched off' (the Web pages provide an option to this), unless there's good reason to think that the query sequence may be phosphorylated, glycosylated, or whatever.

At one time, such patterns were called 'rules', to differentiate them from the family-specific expressions; although this term seems to have fallen out of favour, you may still find articles, books and other documents that use it. ## Fingerprints

As we've seen, the simplistic nature of consensus expressions has diagnostic limitations: they perform well in identifying short, conserved sequence features, but are efficient at designing members of divergent superfamilies. One of their principal drawbacks hangs on the phylogeny of using motifs. In practice, when we examine multiple sequence alignments, it is usual to find not one, but several motifs. Diagnostically, it makes sense to use many, or all - intuitively, to build a characteristic signature or 'fingerprint' of the family - intuitively, we know that the more information there is in a fingerprint (in this case, the more motifs), greater its potency. As diagnostic tools, fingerprints are more than collections of motifs - the order in which motifs appear in alignments and the distances between them are also key to the underlying analytical model. The concept of a protein fingerprint introduced in Box 3.4 adds to the concept and explain fingerprints are created and used in practice.

Figure 4.28 illustrates an analysis of hemoglobin alpha chains, with four motifs highlighted (the rectangular boxes). Seed alignments like this, ungapped motifs are excised and used to scan a sequence of UniProtKB from which sequence fragments have been removed. The database-scanning algorithm translates the motifs into a set of simple identity or frequency matrices - no scoring matrix is used to weigh the results, as shown in Figure 4.29. This means that the resulting matrices are relatively sparse, and hence that the searches are fairly selective. The full potency of the fingerprint method is not just from iterative development of the motifs, but from the mutual context provided by motif neighbours. This is important because fingerprints provide a biological context to motifs are matched in the correct order and with appropriate intervals between them (see Box 4.7). This sequences to be practised even when parts of a fingerprint are present, e.g., a sequence that matches four motifs may still be diagnosed as a family member, if they are matched in the correct order and between them are consistent with true neighbours members. This scenario is illustrated in Figure 4.33. The plot on the left-hand side shows the result of scanning the human hemoglobin (P69905) with the hemoglobin fingerprint, ALPHAHAEM (PR00612); strong matches are seen to each of the five fingerprints. By contrast, the plot on the right-hand side shows the result of scanning an Antarctic dragonfish hemoglobin (P230166) with the same fingerprint; here, only four of the five motifs are matched.
