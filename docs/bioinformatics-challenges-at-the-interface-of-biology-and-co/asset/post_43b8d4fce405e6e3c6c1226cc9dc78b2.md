
## 5.10 Problems with functions

Faced with the rich bounty of the genome projects, our imperative is to add value to the steadily accumulating raw data by enhancing them with ‘meaning’. What, for example, does this or what gene product do? Is its function? Fortunately, we have at our disposal the enormous power of today’s computers and a vast array of analytical tools to help answer questions. So do we use technologies to infer biological functions? Function prediction is now mostly performed using automated pipelines that exploit various types of database search; pairwise analyses are routinely performed with tools like BLAST or FASTA; similarity-based searches tend to be performed using resources like InterPro, and information is gathered from a variety of ontological resources.

A number of issues arise when applying annotation approaches. As we see, the family-based methods in their diagnostic power, some (like HHMs and profiles) providing broad, high-level functional diagnoses, others (like patterns and fingerprints) providing finer details. The databases associated with these approaches differ in terms of their coverage; more importantly, the fundamental concept of ‘family’ differs from them, making comparisons difficult. InterPro was developed to address many of these issues, and in fact goes a long way towards achieving this; nevertheless, it adds layers of complexity to ours.

As we saw in the previous section, one of the biggest problems is the practical definition of what constitutes a family, because the concept of ‘family’ encompasses sequence relationships, functional relationships, structural kinship and evolutionary ancestry. Depending on different perspectives, the nature of families necessarily differs. So, for example, there is general agreement that members of a family must have a demonstrable evolutionary relationship: this is usually established with reference to some specific sequence similarity cut-off (e.g., greater than or equal to 30 per cent.). But common descent (HOMOLOGY – see Box 5.4) may be inferred in the absence of significant sequence identity: some members of the globin family, for example, sequence identities of ~15 per cent. So where should we draw the relatedness line? Proteins can be on the basis of structural similarities, if they have the same secondary structures in the arrangement, with the same topological connections.

Determining membership on the basis of defined levels of sequence identity or gross similarity can lead to problems, because families may be highly similar in sequence but divergent in function, or similar in structure and function but divergent in sequence. ## 5.4 Issues with homology

Inference of homology is fundamental to the process of sequence annotation. Unfortunately, the meaning of this term is widely misunderstood, and has consequently been misused, polluting the literature with statements that equate homology and similarity – they are not the same. Sequences are said to be homologous if they are related by divergence from a common ancestor. A related term, analogy, denotes the acquisition of certain functions (folds or functions) via convergent evolution from unannotated ancestors: e.g., β-barrels in a variety of proteins (trypsin, the Tubby protein, etc.), despite their similar architectures, share no sequence or functional similarity; the enzymes chymotrypsin and subtilis share catalytic residues, with near-identical spatial geometries, but no other sequence or structural similarities (e.g., chymotrypsin and subtilisin in a 3-layer architecture).

The important point is that homology is not a similarity; it is an statement that sequences have a divergent rather than a convergent evolutionary relationship – unlike similarity, it is not quantitative. If we use the term “homology” when we really mean similarity (e.g., these sequences share 5 per cent homology), then we are pre-supposing a homologous relationship that is only 5 per cent amino acid identity, but they may not be homologous which could easily arise by chance.

Database search tools identify quantify similarity between sequences; it is for users of the tools to ascertain, whether the sequences are likely to have derived from a common ancestor. The same arguments apply when comparing structures: structures may be similar, but their common evolutionary origin is a hypothesis that then needs to be verified with additional evidence. When we compare homology and similarity, things are bound to go wrong. In the past, investigations of globular proteins led to the “common sequence/function/ancestry” paradigm by considering packing of hydrophobic cores. A given protein fold will have a hydrophobic interior; thus, two proteins sharing the same fold are frankly likely to share similar interactions. For hydrophobic segments of non-globular proteins, however, the development of membrane protein structures has meant that the implications of similarity haven’t been studied to the same extent; nevertheless, homology considerations have been ‘extended’ to Wong et al. (2010). This interest has particular ramifications for membrane-spanning signal TM helices, where sequence similarity necessarily results from physical requirements rather than ancestry.
