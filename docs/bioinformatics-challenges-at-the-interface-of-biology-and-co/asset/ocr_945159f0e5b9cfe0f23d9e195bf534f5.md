# Box 6.3 The inadequacy of Moore's Law

To see how the advantages gained by Moore's Law are lost by any time complexity slower than O(n), let's look at a specific (contrived) example. Table 6.6 shows what happens, over time, the relative volume of data increases exponentially (a trend exhibited by most of the main-databases) and the relative speed of processors increase, also exponentially (according to Moore's Law).

# Table 6.6
Comparison of the behaviour of linear, linearithmic and polynomial algorithms with increasing volume of data (left-hand side) and processor speed (right-hand side).

|---|---|---|---|---||
| | O(n) | O(n log n) | O(n²) | O(n) | O(n log n) | O(n²) |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 2 | 2.3 | 4 | 2 | 1.15 | 2 |
| 4 | 4 | 5.2 | 16 | 4 | 1.30 | 4 |
| 8 | 8 | 11.6 | 64 | 8 | 1.45 | 8 |
| 16 | 16 | 25.6 | 256 | 16 | 1.60 | 16 |
| 32 | 32 | 56.0 | 1024 | 32 | 1.75 | 32 |
| 64 | 64 | 128.1 | 4096 | 64 | 1.90 | 64 |

The left half of the table shows three classes of algorithms (linear, linearithmic and polynomial, as described in Section 6.4) behave when the volume of data changes; it increases in processor speed. We can see that the time taken by the linear algorithm increases proportionally with the size of the input while the linearithmic algorithm slowly falls behind; while the polynomial algorithm quickly begins to take a long time to complete.

The right half of the table then indicates that the speed of processor is also increasing, alongside the volume of data. When we take this increase into account, we can see the speed-adjusted time of each algorithm. The linear algorithm, once adjusted, takes the same amount of time to complete until forever (the increase in data and processor speed cancel each other out). The linearithmic algorithm's time slowly creep up, though at a manageable pace. The polynomial algorithm, however, started to behave badly; despite the exponential speed-up, it's complexity dominates and, over time, its performance will degrade.
