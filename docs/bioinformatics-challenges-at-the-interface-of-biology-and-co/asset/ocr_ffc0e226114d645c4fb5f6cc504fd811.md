By 1992, the PIR-PSD had shown steady growth, with increasing contributions from European and Asian protein sequences, most notably from the Munich Center for Protein Sequences (MIPS) and from the Japan Protein Information Database (JIPID). To reflect this situation, a tripartite collaboration was established, termed PIR-Information, to formalise these relationships and to establish, maintain and disseminate a comprehensive set of protein sequences (Barker et al., 1992). By this time, charging for the resource was no longer mentioned, possibly because of more formal distribution archive. Doubtless, the recent advent of browsers like Mosaic had a significant impact, suddenly and dramatically changing the way in which information could be broadcast and received over the newly established Web, at the cost of a mouse button. In 1997, PIR changed its name to the Protein InformationResource17 (George et al., 1997) and by 2003, with 283,000 sequences (Wu et al., 2003), the PSD was the most comprehensive protein sequence database in the world.

# 3.7 Swiss-Prot

With these events unfolding in the background, a newly qualified Swiss student (who, as a teenager, had an enthusiasm for exploration and the search for terrestrial life) signed up to undertake a Masters project – this was Amos Bairoch18 Bairoch had chosen a mass spectrometry project with ‘wet’ and ‘dry’ components, but immediately hit problems when he discovered that the new spectrometer that was been used for the experimental side of his project didn’t work properly. Not to lose time, he set to work developing protein sequence-analysis programs on the computer's system. These were the first steps towards creating the software that was later known to be PC/Gene, and was to become the most widely used PC-based sequence analysis package of its day (Bairoch, 2000).

Part of the uniqueness of this software was its focus on proteins at a time when the analysis of nucleotides was much more vogue. In the process of writing the software, Bairoch had to manually enter >1,000 protein sequences into his computer: some of these gleaned from the literature; many were taken from the Atlas, which had not been released in electronic format. This task was clearly tedious and highly error-prone. Realising this, and to help alleviate the burden for other scientists in future, he wrote a letter to the Bioinformatics Journal recommending that researchers publishing protein and peptide sequences should compute checks to facilitate the detection of typographical and keyboard errors’ (Bairoch, 1982). Within the letter, Bairoch explained how such a ‘checking number’ might be computed for an imaginary peptide, as illustrated in Figure 3.2. This recommendation was widely adopted by publishers, but he was at least to ensure that it was implemented in the database that he went on to create.
