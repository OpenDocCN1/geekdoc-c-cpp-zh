Hamilton volunteered to set up the 'master copy' of the American bank at the Brookhaven National Laboratory; meanwhile, Kendall agreed to help the European bank in Cambridge to extend the CCDC small-molecule format to accommodate protein structural data (Kend et al., 1972; Meyer, 1997). Events gave birth to the Protein Data Bank (PDB), a joint venture of the CCDC and Brookhaven.

The new resource was announced in a short news bulletin in October 1971 (Berman et al., 2000). Interestingly envisaged the PDB as a prototype for nucleotide sequence data library, although this resource was not to materialise for a further decade (Smith, 1990). The PDB became fully operational in 1973 (Protein Data Bank, 1973). By then, the corpus of it data had been established to house only nine hundred structures (Table 3.2).

Kendall knew, that for the resource to be successful, it had to buy-in from the entire community – it demanded but gaining sufficient community momentum to back the initiative was a slow process (e.g., note the absence from its 1970s of ribonuclease, whose structure, as we saw in Chapter 1, had been determined several years earlier).

In the years that followed, as the number of structures submitted to the PDB grew, the archive included its first example of transfer RNA (tRNA) structure. This stimulated a debate about whether 'Protein Data Bank' was an appropriate name (Bernstein et al., 1977). Despite the reservations, however the name stuck: in November 2015, still known as PDB, the resource included more than 8,000 acid and protein-nucleic acids, and is a testament to the pioneers of structural biology.

By 1977, the database contained 77 sets of coordinates relating to only 47 macromolecules, highlighting why Berstein tried to get news of the resource by publishing the same PDB article verbatim in May and November of 1977, and again in January 1978, in three different journals (Bernstein et al., 1977a,b; 1978); Whatever the real reasons, the PDB expanded gradually compared to the CSD (Kendard, 1997), and the number of unique structures remained small – to put this in context, by 1996, there were ~6,000 structures in the PDB
