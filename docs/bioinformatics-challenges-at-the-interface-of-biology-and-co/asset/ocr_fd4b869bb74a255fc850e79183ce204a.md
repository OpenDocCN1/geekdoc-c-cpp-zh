# Box 5. Issues with structures and structure prediction

In the wake of the genome era, bioinformatics was predicted to provide a variety of revolutionary tools, including those able to make accurate predictions of protein structures from their linear sequences (Wallace, 2001). The 3D structure prediction was spurred by Anfinsen's early studies on the folding of ribosome, which led him to assert that, in the case of small globular proteins, the native structure is determined by the amino acid sequence—Anfinsen's Dogma* (Anfinsen, 1973).

This simple idea stimulated the development of a range of structure-prediction methods, including knowledge-based approaches that use information from structure databases to build models, and computationally-intensive strategies that attempt to simulate the physical and chemical forces involved in protein folding. Despite more than 40 years of research, the problem of predicting protein structures from their sequences remains unsolved: homology and modelling techniques generally produce low resolution models; no method can guarantee accurate predictions for homologs. For some, ab initio methods generate models with 'resembling' the correct fold, but deteriorate for larger proteins (i.e., with more than 100 residues). It turns out that knowledge-based methods combine information from a variety of different approaches give best results; for modelling and recognition, most algorithms suffer from levels of uncertainty.

This shouldn't imply Prediction methods are accurate to atomic resolution because we dont fully understand the protein problem– how primary structure determines tertiary structure– we can't really use language sequences to create their fold. Another part of the problem is that the beautiful structures we enriched in PDB files are static snapshots of dynamic biological features that have frozen in time (although, aligned, structures do convey dynamic of the native fold of proteins). So, when we try to predict structures from our sequences, we are attempting to capture a moment in the complex dance of a protein– not surprisingly, this is difficult to achieve.

It was hoped that the Structure Initiative (PSI) would lessen our reliance on prediction by providing structures or models for all completed genomes. But as we see in Chapter 3, this lofty goal has been tempered by an acceptance that the structures we can determine are limited to those that respond to the available biophysical properties (proteins that crystallise, or are tractable by NMR, etc.); membrane proteins remain difficult, as they are intractable to conventional crystallisation techniques, and most are too large to be solved by NMR.

A key motivating of the PSI was that whether of all these protein structures diverge their molecular functions– but this was too over-simplistic. By January 2016, the functions of more than three structures determined by PSI (1,662/4,873) remained unknown; researchers are invited to find functional sites to help characterize their structures. It's important to put the value of protein folds in perspective. Protein folds that can be decorated in different sequences to conform different functions. Knowing both the fold and the function allows us to piece together the mechanistic processes which the structure excites its function at the molecular level. Structures provide one part of a complex puzzle. Without supporting information, knowing structure alone is not enough to tell us–for a good example, see how Zareminski et al. struggled to determine the precise binding function and role of a hypothetical protein, despite having determined its molecular structure (Zareminski et al., 1998). Knowledge of the scaffold of an uncharacterised protein was a useful piece of the functional jigsaw only in relation to other molecular, cellular and functional clauses.
