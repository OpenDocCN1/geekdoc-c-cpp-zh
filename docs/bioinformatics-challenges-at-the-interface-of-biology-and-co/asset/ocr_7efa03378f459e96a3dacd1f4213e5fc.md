Within each block, the constituent sequences were clustered to a percentage identity greater than specific cut-off value. Sequences in the same cluster were weighted as a single sequence, thereby helping to reduce any bias introduced by groups of identical and near-identical sequences (of which there were likely to be many block). The log-odds matrix resulting from a particular clustering percentage is referred to as number BLOSUM matrix: e.g., BLOSUM 62 (see Figure 4.19), which includes substitutions between sequences that are <62 per cent identical; BLOSUM 80, which includes substitutions between sequences that are <80 per cent identical. Note that, by choosing PAM matrices, the higher the BLOSUM number, the greater the similarity represented by the matrix.
BLOSUM 62 became a popular scoring matrix, often replacing PAM250 as the default in sequence analysis and alignment programs. This is probably because the Henikoffs published evidence that their matrices performed better in alignments and similarity searches than those based on accepted mutations, and in particular, BLOSUM 62 was the single best matrix (Henikoff and Henikoff, 1992; Henikoff and Henikoff, 1993).
Comparing PAM250 and BLOSUM 62 matrices shown in Figures 4.18 and 4.19, we can see that key trends are generally preserved in terms of which amino acids are the most and least unlike; nevertheless, the numerical details differ. This means that we use such matrices for database searches or sequence alignment, the details of the program outputs are also likely to differ slightly. When seeking relationships in the Twilight Zone, where the overall resemblance between sequences is slight, such disparities could cause spotting a distant relationship or missing it.
