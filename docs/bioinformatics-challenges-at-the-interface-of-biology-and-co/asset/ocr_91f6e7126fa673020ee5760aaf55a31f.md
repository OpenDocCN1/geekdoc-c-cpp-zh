# Box 2.3 Properties of amino acids

As we saw in Box 2.2, the amino acid side-chains have different structures, and confer on each amino acid different, but nevertheless overlapping; they also play a key role in determining how proteins fold.

We will look at some of these properties in turn. First, the relevance of amino acid size. Proteins have compact structures: they pack tightly, excluding water as they do. Substituting one amino acid for another without accounting for the size of their side-chains can cause changes, exchanging a large amino acid for a small one, for example, to disrupt the structure. There is significant variation in size between the amino acids: the largest, Trp has 3.4 times the volume of the smallest, Gly (measured in Å³; see Angstrom, and in Table 2.3 (data taken from Higgins and Atwood, 2005)).

An alternative measure of amino acid size is to consider its 'buliness' (i.e., the ratio of the side-chain volume to length), which provides a measure of the average cross-sectional area of the side-chain(s). We also consider the polarity of the amino acids in terms of the electrostatic force of an acid at its surroundings distances of 10 Å. The total force (in units scaled for convenience) gives a polarity index, also shown in the table. The polarity index (Zimmerman et al., 1968) effectively destabilizes charged and un amino acids.

Another property is the pH, defined as the pH of the amino acid at its isoelectric point. Acidic amino acids (Asp and Glu) have pH in the range 2–3; these amino acids would be negatively charged at neutral pH (pH of 7.0). By contrast, the basic amino acids (Lys, Arg and His) have pH in the range 5–6.
