The process of writing what's written on the page (or, indeed, any kind of data) into knowledge, however, relies far more than our ability to practise patterns: in our interactions through this book, for example, you the reader, and as authors, have made many of assumptions about how we communicate. Some of these are so obvious that they 'go without saying': we have written this book in English, so we're assuming (certainly if you've got this far!) that you understand this language; we have used Latin Script to represent the letters that form the words; and we have written numbers using Western Arabic Numerals, which, for the most part, we represented using the decimal number system. Until now, we didn't have to manage this, yet things have just fine. As authors, we're also making a swathe of other, much harder to define, assumptions about you as a reader (that you have a certain understanding of biology and computing, a certain interest in bioinformatics, and so on), all of which need to be true to some extent for this book to make sense.

An intelligent human, you're able to make sophisticated decisions about the clarity of the communication you read on; if we've assumed too much background knowledge and failed to explain something in enough detail for you, you can spot this and find some other source to bolster your understanding; if we've erred the other way and assumed too little, you can easily skip forward in the text to to new material. A hierarchy of meaning. Data are discrete, objective facts (rhodopsin is a protein; retinitis pigmentosa is a disease). Information gathers data together to create associations (mutations of the rhodopsin-encoding gene are implicated in retinitis pigmentosa). Knowledge applies experience and context to inform decisions, and create questions and hypotheses (can retinitis pigmentosa be cured by 'fixing' mutated rhodopsin-coding genes?)
