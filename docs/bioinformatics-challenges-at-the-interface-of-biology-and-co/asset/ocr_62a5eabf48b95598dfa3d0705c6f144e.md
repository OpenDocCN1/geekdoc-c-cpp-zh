The most prominent online resource for searching the biomedical literature is the National Library of Medicine (NLM) PubMed. This brings together 'gold standard' citation and classification data provided by publishers via the MEDLINE database, with citations from the NCBI Bookshelf, articles published by NIH-funded projects, full-text Open Access publications from the PMC database (some of which may not have been indexed through MEDLINE), and 'hot-off-the-press' in-process citations for articles that may need to be included in the main MEDLINE index. By December 2015, PubMed included +5 million citations.

Records in PubMed – at least indexed via MEDLINE – are classified according MeSh (Medical Subject Headings) terms, the NLM controlled vocabulary of biomedical concepts. PubMed's primary search encourages short keyword queries — Clarke and Wentz (2000) described as 'telegrams', we're challenged to choose whether two or three words we'd need to find in order to encapsulate a particular research question. For example, if our question was 'How effective is a dose of steroid for outpatient?' this might suggest, 'crop and outpatient'. Entered into PubMed main search box, the MeSh vocabulary expands this 'telegram' to a more complex query:

"crop[MeSh Terms] OR "crop[All Fields]" AND ("outpatients[MeSh Terms] OR "outpatients[All Fields] OR "outpatient[All Fields]""

The expanded query isn't hugely exciting: PubMed's processor has taken the term 'and' in our query (correctly), opted to search all the bibliographic fields (titles, abstracts and so on) in its records, plus all the MeSh classifications with those records, and has included simple plurals. More interesting, perhaps, what happens if we search for 'Torcetrapib' and JTT-705-705'? PubMed's processor expands this as follows:

"torcetrapib[Supplementary Content] OR "torcetrapib[All Fields]" AND ("dalcetrapib[Supplementary Content] OR "dalcetrapib[All Fields] OR jrt 705[All Fields]"

Notice that the code 'JTT-705' has been cleverly expanded to include Roche's brand-'dalcetrapib':

PubMed's search mechanism is a step beyond simple substring or keyword-matching, but as we can see from its popularity, the application of straightforward heuristics, the expansion of terms using a controlled vocabulary to include synonyms and related concepts, and some simple 'stemming' of words to deal with plurals, goes a long way towards producing a powerful literature-search mechanism. Understanding and exploiting every bit of its behaviour can yield excellent results.

It could be argued that PubMed's approach includes some basic 'semantic' elements, not least because it uses a controlled vocabulary to classify concepts and to expand queries. Nevertheless, the phrase 'semantic search has come to mean something more ambitious, and is used to describe mechanisms that attempt to generate relevant results by understanding both the meaning of the contents of a searched corpus and the intent of the user.
