## Linking data and scientific literature

### 8.1 Overview

Throughout this book, we've been exploring the problems that arise when trying to bridge the gap between forms of representation that are meaningful to humans and those that are comprehensible to machines. In this chapter, we focus on how these issues affect how scientists, as both producers and consumers of knowledge, interact with the scientific literature and with its underlying data. In particular, we examine how records in biological databases are interwoven with prose from articles to create meaningful knowledge archives; conversely, we review how attempts are being made to weave research data more tightly back the thread of scientific discourse.

By the end of this chapter, you should have an appreciation of the history of the scientific literature, how it's currently used to annotate and enhance biological data-bases, and in turn, it reflects back to the content of those databases as authoritative data records. You've developed an understanding of the difficulties of linking data with the often tacit knowledge captured in centuries of narrative; you should also have gained insights into which the literature is changing to meet the needs of the modern scientist, and the role of technology in this change. Ultimately, you should have an appreciation of some of the advances that are beginning to be made in this field, and some of the barriers to progress.

### 8.2 Introduction

The story of this book concerns the gap between what bioinformaticians are trying to achieve – bringing meaning to biological data – what consumers can do to help us, today and in the future. In many ways, it's the story of the gulf between human knowledge, intuition and intuition, on the one hand, and the unforgiving low-level pedagogy of computers, on the other, and of the problems that emerge when we bring these two different things to support one another. We have seen, from numerous perspectives, understanding what a computer can and can't do of its own accord.

## Bioinformatics challenges at the interface of biology and computer science

From the perspective, of biological sequences, we've looked at tools and databases, algorithms and data structures, and teased out the relationships, possibilities and pitfalls of using computers to bridge the gap between biological data and the Web, combined with tools and knowledge; we have created a new 'knowledge infrastructure', both with potential and with problems. We've only hinted so far is the relationship between this new knowledge infrastructure and a much older one that existed – for the most part, fullyformed – before the invention of the first computer: the scientific literature.

As we saw in Chapters 1 and 3, early databases, like PIR and Swiss-Prot, were created by enthusiasts who painstakingly scoured the literature for papers containing information about newly sequenced proteins, and manually transcribed the amino acid sequences therein into electronic formats. To augment and add value to the raw sequence data, we would, however, possible, to gather assorted facts about the protein (its subcellular location, its function, its post-translational modifications, and so on; by large, they also record the records they used as information sources. Today, biological database annotators (or biocurators, as they're all known), still spend amounts of time scouring the literature for facts to support, refute or somehow add meaning or explanation to particular database records – they do this database entries are regarded as more authoritative and meaningful when they can publish experimental evidence.

The task of excavating specific facts from the literature can be quite tricky. As part of the process, biocurators have to cope with all of the vagaries of natural language, coupled with the sometimes-confounding nature of field-specific scientific jargon, and with the frequent ambiguities of biological terminology: By way of illustration, consider the following scenarios:

- 1 retrieved a list of article titles and abstracts from PubMed, a biocurator interested in the function of Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 2 (STAT2) has to decide whether the rather equiv conclusion of the abstract,"our data suggest a role for V in Nanog regulation networks and reveal a novel role for STAT2 in regulating Nanog expression" [our emphasis] (Wu et al., 2013) is sufficiently compelling to warrant reading the full article to light on this putative function, or whether time would be better spent on 'concrete' papers.
- 2 seeking articles about opioid receptor function, a biocurator may have to decide from the opaque toute, 'Exposure to chronic mild stress prevents kappa opioid-mediated reinstatement of cocaine and nicotine place preference' [our emphasis] (Al-Hasani et al., 2013) whether this specialist part is really likely to sequester facts pertinent to receptor function, and is hence worth taking time to read;
- 3 reading a paper about Mini Chromosome Maintenance (MCM) protein complexes in Drosophila in search of information related to mouse DNA replication licensing factor, MCM4, a biocurator encountering sentences such as, Co-immunoprecipitation demonstrate the existence of at least two distinct types of 600-kDa complexes, one that contains DMC46 and one that appears to contain both DmMCM2 and Dpa (a CDC55 homologue); and ‘Mutants in MCM2, MCM3, MCM5/CDC46, CDC47, and CDC55 arrest with incompletelyplicated DNA, indicating a role for these genes in DNA replication’ may describe whether Dpa in the fly is the ‘same’ as the murine gene MCM4, and whether yeast CDC54 protein is the same as the murine gene product – no explicit link to MCM4 is made either in the abstract or in the body of the paper (Su et al., 1996).
