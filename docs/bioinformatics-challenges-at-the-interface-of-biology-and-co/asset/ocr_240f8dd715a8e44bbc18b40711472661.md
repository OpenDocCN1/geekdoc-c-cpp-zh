All the potentials of an individual may be inscribed in polypeptide chains that are actually synthesized, or could be synthesized, by the cells under certain circumstances, and in the structures that control the actual and potential rates of this synthesis (Zuckerkland and Pauling, 1965).

With great precision, they envisaged biomolecular sequences as ‘documents of evolutionary history’; nevertheless, they recognised that extracting these sequestered histories would require more sequence information than then available. As the field of molecular evolution dawned, our hunger to understand more about the functions and structures of biological macromolecules provided the impetus for further sequencing efforts. Margaret Dayhoff, who had a keen interest in discerning relationships was among the first scientists to approach this work systematically. In the early 1960s, to facilitate both her own research and the work of others in the field, she began to collect protein sequence information from scientific papers. This compounding of sequences was eventually published as a book – the Atlas of Protein Sequence and Structure (Dayhoff et al., 1965), often simply referred to as the Atlas. Interestingly, she wrote a letter in 1967, that, observed,

There is a tremendous amount of information regarding the evolutionary history and bio-chemical function in each sequence and the number of known sequences is growing explosively [our emphasis]. We feel it is important to collect this significant information, correlate it into a unified whole and interpret (Dayhoff, 1967; Strasser, 2008).

With the creation of the first Atlas, that ‘explosive growth’ amounted to 65 sequences!
