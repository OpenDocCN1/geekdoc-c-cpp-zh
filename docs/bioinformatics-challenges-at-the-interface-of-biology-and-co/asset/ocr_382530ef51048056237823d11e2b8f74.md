Pseudocode for the task of finding the largest in a list of numbers, where we keep track of the maximum value in the variable max. We begin by assigning to it the first number of the input list, and then in the 'each' loop, we iteratively compare it with every other; at each step, if the number found is greater than max, we update max to have this new value. Once the loop is complete, and we've visited the entire input list, max is guaranteed to hold the largest of its values.

Software engineers must choose (or design) algorithms to solve the problems they're given. Ultimately, they'll implement them in a programming language; before that step starts, some thought is needed.

Although most algorithms are considerably more complex than the simple example introduced above, they are still mathematical constructs, and hence are described and investigated mathematically. These formal methods of investigation are used to quantify the efficiency of algorithms, prove or disprove their correctness, and even predict likely outcomes. Many of these formal methods are beyond the scope of this book, but an appreciation of a number of issues is important when thinking about solving problems with computers.

# Not all problems are computable

Some problems simply cannot be solved, although it're rare to come across in practice. One example of a non-computable problem is described by the halting problem, which states that it's impossible to decide whether algorithms will actually complete in a finite time, or will continue running forever; for non-halting algorithms, the only way to know they won't halt is to run them and wait and see, which will obviously take forever as well.
