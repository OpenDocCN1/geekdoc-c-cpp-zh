Thus, in as little as 12 months, the hedged claims in the original paper can be seen to have crept towards established facts, increasing the likelihood that authors citing this second generation of work will accept them as ‘canon’. This phenomenon of undue credence arising from repeated citation of previous articles that lack scientific foundations has been referred to as ‘evidence by citation’, or more, the Woozle effect’. Ideally, of course, authors should find and diligently rehearse the papers they cite, following each claim to its original source, and draw their conclusions about the strength, context and validity of particular assertions. However, for ‘hot topics’ in the life sciences, the rate at which articles are published keeping abreast of developments is exceptionally challenging; cutting corners is therefore extremely tempting. Overall, then, if unpicking the tangled threads that relate scientific claims is difficult for competent humans, how much so will it be for machines? As Latour put,

... you can transform a fact into fiction or a fiction into fact just by adding or subtracting references (Latour, 1987).

Most scientific articles, historical and contemporary, are riddled with such computational hazards: i.e., sentences that are readily comprehensible to literate humans but are impenetrable to machines, because they use terms that have multiple meanings that make sense only if we understand the broader context, or they include scientific assertions that have been smothered by cautious phraseology, or that have been inappropriately amplified – Woozled – by careless citing.

## 8.6 Research objects

The problems outlined in the previous section challenge the value of the scholarly literature as a mechanism for sharing and repeating scientific endeavours, especially in the ‘digital age’. As we see, articles are riddled with uncertainty, inconsistency and ‘fluff’, attributes that render their latent nuggets of knowledge largely inaccessible to computers. If we be able to process the literature computationally, to encode the scientific data and know it in machine-readable form, radically new approaches are required. One commentator has noted:

> I believe that the academic paper is now obsolete as the fundamental sharable description of a piece of research. In the future we will be sharing other form of scholarly artefact, which is digital and designed to rise and drop easily into the tooling of e-Research, better suited to the emerging practices of data-centric researchers. These could be called Knowledge Objects or Publication Objects or whatever: I shall refer to them as Research Objects, because they capture research (De Roure, 2009).

In this statement, De Roure encapsulated a vision that had circulating in the 'eResearch/eScience' community for some time. Specifically, he proposed that 'Research Objects' should be repeatable, believable, reproducible, repurposable and able. At the time, he speculated that these were 'necessary and sufficient' properties, but later extended the idea to include six more characteristics (De Roure, 2010), categorising the resulting 12 as ‘intrinsic’ to Research Objects, or relating to their 'social life' or to their use 'practice'. He formally set out ‘12 Rs of Research Objects as shown in Figure 8.5.

It's tempting to think that many of these properties are already inherent in digital, and that simply moving from paper-based methods (traditional lab books, articles and journals, manuals of scientific protocols, etc.) will necessarily need to satisfy all of '12 Rs'. Superially, there is truth in this: we use URLs to make things referenceable; at their heart, computer code is deterministic so repeatable and reproducible; the very nature of the web that content is refreshable; we know that libraries of code are frequently shared and used in ways their authors never intended, so are reusable and repurposable; 'version control' systems for source code provide programmers with recovery and repair; and the speed at which news (or the latest video of a kitten piano) spreads around the world demonstrates the Web’s astonishing retrieveability.

But scratch the surface of these properties, and their limitations, as direct secures to more traditional mechanisms for recording and sharing scientific knowledge, begin to show through. airo's original Swiss-Prot floppy disk, for example had it not, sadly, misled years ago could almost never be read by modern PCs, most of which have no floppy-disk disc achieving a literally iconic status on 'save' buttons in graphical interfaces; it is only because consistent effort has been expended over the years to keep the database safe and current that it's accessible in its current form today.

Some of the limitations of digital resources are ‘social’: the author of a open-source software properly ‘published’ it as a gesture of goodwill to the community, without either the resources or the support to use it indefinitely. Others are more fundamental: hardware is constantly being improved, but inevitably degrades and eventually fails (conventional wisdom suggests that the life expectancy of hard drives is between three and five years – the blink of an eye in the context of the long-term scientific record); operating systems are constantly updated to patch security flaws, or to introduce new features, and often can be deprecated features of operating systems can no longer be compiled; and, in turn, data formats that relied on defunct software become unusable.

And so the conundrum: how to move the relatively stable paper-based scientific literature, which has served well for centuries, to a model of knowledge representation that is robust in the face of rapidly changing technology, and capable of supporting scientific discovery when confronted with increasingly complex data and prose. The consequences of failing to achieve this are likely to be immediate, significant and expensive (Scott et al., 2008); Begley and Ellis, 2012). Although we are a long way from a complete solution, there are many interesting developments in this area, which we'll explore in the remainder of this chapter.
