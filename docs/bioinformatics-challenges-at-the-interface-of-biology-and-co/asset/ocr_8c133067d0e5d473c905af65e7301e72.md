Taxonomies are seductive intellectual artefacts, and there's something deeply satisfying by being able to tame a web of unruly ideas by arranging them in a neat hierarchy. However, to accept, that the desire to do so is nothing more than human predilection. Throughout history, life scientists have been concerned documenting the multiplicity of living systems in what has been described as a sort of ‘biological stamp collecting’ (Hunter, 2006). Although creating taxonomies may give a sense that we have mastered a particular domain, unfortunately the natural world, and all the biochemical and evolutionary forces that shape it, yields concepts that fit perfectly into such tidy structures. In the particular, the notion of ‘hierarchy’ used makes them fragile and difficult to adapt over time, as properties ‘higher up’ the tree have significant influence on how concepts are arranged lower down. Thus, mistakes in classification, or the discovery of things that don't conveniently fit anywhere in the hierarchy, cause disruption.

So let move beyond simple taxonomies and imagine that we want to extend our system to explicitly represent the one-letter symbols used to encode amino acids in most sequence formats. So we need a more nuanced way to capture and represent knowledge.

Until now, we’ve drawn our acid classification as a tree-like graph, albeit with the ‘root’ at the top and the ‘leaves’ at the bottom; but remember, this is merely a visual convention that was used for illustrating the taxonomy we created. By adding a different type of ‘edge’, representing the relationship has the one-letter code, we can create a basic thesaurus – a more general structure that allows things to be given multiple names or labels, and other relationships to be explicitly recorded. Many of the so called ontologies in the life sciences are actual examples of vocabularies, taxonomies, or thesauri. However further level of specification can be added to these to create what are often referred to as ‘axiomatically rich ontologies’, which are assuring increasing importance in bioinformatics (although, somewhat ironically, there is no commonly agreed name for such things). In all the structures we’ve examined so far, knowledge is captured by enumerating classes and relationships between them. Using an ontology to write rules, and a reasoner to check that they are logically consistent, it’s possible to add axioms to a body of knowledge, either to avoid having to list everything explicitly, or to impose logical constraints. For example, we write axioms that:

+   1. Glutamic acid and aspartic acid are ‘negative’
2. Arginine and lysine are ‘positive’
3. Things can’t be ‘positive’ or ‘negative’
4. Anything that is either ‘positive’ or ‘negative’ is ‘charged’
5. All hydrophilic residues that are not ‘charged’ are ‘neutral’.
