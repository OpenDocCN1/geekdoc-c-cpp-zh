# 5.4 Problems with names

In the Preface, we mentioned that getting humans to agree on what words means is hard; getting them to understand when they're using the same word to mean different things is harder; getting a machine to understand the difference is harder still. For machines, precision is vital; unfortunately, however, adherence to standard nomenclatures has, at best, been in the life sciences.

Consider the protein, ‘DNA replication factor MCM4’, shown in Table 5.1: this has five different protein and six different genes in three different species. With bit of work, humans can unravel this kind of intricacy relatively easily; but once we involve computers in the loop of comprehension, the task becomes more complicated, and the level of precision required to specify a protein or gene becomes much more difficult to define. For example, if we use a text-mining algorithm to search the literature for protein ‘proliferation abnormal’, we would miss other names of the same protein (cell division control protein, CDC2 homolog, P1-CDC21); MDC21, DCS44, etc. Only if the software had been programmed to use comprehensive synonym dictionaries or if we search for the gene *dpa*, would we be sure we haven't missed a relevant gene or protein like *Mcm4* or *dpa* in the fly? To be absolutely confident in our results, of course, we'd need access to the ultimate gene and protein repository for all species, the creation and maintenance of which would be a logistical nightmare, given the dearth of nomenclature standards or, more to the point, our failure to adhere consistently to those that do¹.

Inconsistent use of identifiers and accession numbers throws up related issues. Let's briefly codify over protein. This protein first entered the PIR database with identifier (ID) OOSH, accession number (AC#)03155. Subsequent changes to its sequence and annotation led its AC to A93264, A90319 and to A3047. Later, the same protein also appeared in Swiss-Prot with ID OPSD$SHEEP, AC# P02700².
