
## Chapter 3 Biological databases

RCSB remains the 'archive keeper', with sole write-access to the PDB, controlling its contents, and distributing new PDB identifiers (IDs) to all deposition sites.

In November 2015, the PDB holdings^9 amounted to 114,000 structures of these; ~106,600 structures deduced primarily by X-ray diffraction, Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and electron microscopy; the rest were structures of nucleic acids and nucleic acid-protein complexes, and of carbohydrates. This is much larger than the structures deposited in the PDB 44 years earlier; nevertheless, growth of the resource has been slower than might have been predicted in the wake of high-throughput structure determination. In fact, it became apparent that high-throughput studies fought with difficulties, and the experimental pipelines had high attrition rates (Yon and Jothi, 2003); for example, some proteins couldn't be purified in sufficient quantities to use; of those that could be purified, some remained disorderly; and, of those that did crystallise, some poor quality crystals that would produce diffraction patterns. Consequently, the rate of high-throughput structure determination didn't match the original projections, and the initial early years of the PSI produced fewer than 3,000 structures^10.

As you now know, the total figure of ~106,600 protein structures mentioned above includes a considerable amount of redundancy. We can get an index of how much redundancy by clustering PDB's contents by sequence identity. Table 3.3 shows the result of this for the November 2015 holdings. At 30 per cent identity, there are ~24,000 clusters; even at this level, many of the proteins will still have the same fold, because proteins are more conserved than their sequences. More rigorous studies, using the fold-classification databases as benchmarks, show that the number of unique folds in the PDB is currently somewhere between 1,300 and 1,400 (see Box 3.1 for further details).

Even if we consider the full redundant figure, the number of structures is very small relative to the number of known protein sequences — or, to put this another way, the sequence–structure deficit is large (see Figure 3.3). This means that, at the time of writing, there is still no 3D information available for most proteins.

In practice, 3D for the majority of newly determined sequences has been inferred by comparison with proteins whose structures have already been determined. For the millions of sequences now pouring from genome-sequencing projects, high-throughput structural initiatives will remain a vital part of the continued efforts to augment the PDB and provide structural insights on a large scale.

The PDB was created in the era that interest in collecting protein sequences was growing. With the sequence repositories, the database evolved long before its format is therefore a reflection of the technology current at that time. As a result, the 40 years have born a patchwork of developments to try to make the data more self-contained, and to try to make the format sufficiently robust to sustain its usefulness in the 21st century.

## 3.4 The EMBL nucleotide sequence data library

Despite the advances in protein sequence- and structure-determination technologies between the mid-1940s and 1970s, sequencing nucleic acids had remained problematic. The key issues related to size and ease of molecular purification. It was possible to sequence tRNAs, mostly because they are short (typically less than 100 bases long), and hence individual molecules could be purified; but chromosomal DNA molecules are in a completely different bulk-part, containing millions of bases. Even if such molecules could be broken into smaller chunks, the longest fragment that could then be sequenced was ~500bp.

Progress on these problems came in the mid-1970s, when the new technique allowed sequencing of much longer fragments. This subsequently permitted sequencing of even larger genomes. This led to the discovery of the human genome (Sanger et al., 1982). These advances proved not only shifting from data production towards the organization and analysis of data: for the first time, dawned on scientists that the rate limiting step in nucleic acid sequencing is now shifting from data acquisition towards data management (Ginger and Roberts, 1980).

This realization had profound consequences across the globe, as it was generally agreed that a centralized bank was inescapable as a tool for managing nucleic acid information. From this point, the clock was ticking, and the race was to establish the first public nucleotide sequence repository. The first past was the European Molecular Laboratory (EMBL) in Heidelberg, which set up the EMBL data library. After an initial period, the first of 568 sequences were made in June 1982. The aim of this result was not only to make nucleic acid sequence data publicly available, and free exchange of data, but also to provide a European focus for computational and biological services (Hamm and Cameron, 1986).

Nevertheless, from the outset, it was clear that maintenance of such a centralised database, and its instant services, would require international collaboration. In the UK, of the EMBL data library were maintained at Cambridge University, together with the user-manual, indices and associated sequence analysis, and retrieval software. This integrated system also provided access to the GenBank library of sequences that was being developed in parallel with Los Alamos National Laboratory (Kanesha et al., 1984).
