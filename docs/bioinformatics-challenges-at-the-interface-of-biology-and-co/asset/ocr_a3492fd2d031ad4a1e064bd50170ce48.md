# Afterword

In this book, we've wandered through the history of bioinformatics and computer science, exploring some of the discoveries and challenges that have arisen in the overlaps between these disciplines, and stopping to peer into some of the gaps that exist where they haven't quite made a perfect seal. Our starting point was an archipelago of research islands. For the first leg of the journey, the challenges facing the early pioneers involved caj the primitive computing technology of the day to store, retrieve and display the data that were generated from isolated, largely manual experiments. We've ended up in a much more interconnected world in which vast amounts of data can be generated automatically, where the relationships between biological types are becoming as significant as the topics themselves, and where the challenges involve using today's technology — itself a highly interconnected computing infrastructure — not simply to store and retrieve data to understand and extract insight and meaning from the information we've collected.

There's no doubt that we've come a long way. However, as impressive as the vista of our achievements may seem, it's also clear that this isn't the end of the journey; and it's perhaps sobering to realise that many of the challenges alluded to in this book remain unsolved. In a 2014 'technical perspective' written thirty years ago or after the first computational tools for gene and protein sequence analysis were created, James Hutchins lamented,

Although various programs are available for exploring the properties of sets of genes, transcripts, and proteins, the ideal software tool, in my opinions, has to be created (Hutchins, 2014).

He went on to list five desirable properties of such utopian software. Essentially, he said that this tool should be:

+   1 free: publicly available, cross-platform, and open source, with database architecture and algorithms described in peer-reviewed publications;
2 comprehensive: able to draw on a wide variety of leading database resources;
3 updated: regularly, coordinated with releases of major sequence and functional databases;
4 smart: capable of automatically recognizing ID types and thus determining relevant species and relationships between genes and their products;
5 flexible: allowing a choice of analytical methods and output formats.
