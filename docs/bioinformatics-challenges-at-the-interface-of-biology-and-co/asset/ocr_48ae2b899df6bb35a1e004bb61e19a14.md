Regardless, since 2000, gene-prediction approaches have improved, particu- genomic data-sets have been re-analysed, and gene counts have been subject to iterative refinement. When the draft human genome was made available, the esti- number of genes found to 30,000-35,000; five months later, the numbers count up again, to 65,000-75,000 (Wright et al., 2001). Almost a decade later, the number of predicted human genes weighed mostly between 18,877 and 20,251 (Pertea and Salzberg, 2010).

Aside from the difficulty of accurately locating intron-exon boundaries, another issue is what constitutes a gene has differed to whether it should be considered a heritable unit relating to an observ- phenotype, or a packet of genetic information that encodes RNA, a protein or several proteins – as we see in Chapter 1, genes may encode multiple splice forms. Alternative splicing was first noted in the calcitonin of the human gene the six exons of the human gene isoforms that span exons 1-4, and a third that spans exons 1-3, 5 and 6, skipping exon 4 – marking its significant difference from the others, the third is termed calcitonin gene-related peptide. The extent of alternative splicing in some genes makes this calcitonin appear tribal: e.g., the complexity of the Drosophila Dcam gene led to a predic tion it may have 38,016 splice variants (Schucker et al., 2000)! To further matters, genes can exist in multiple copies, and they don't have to be expressed (it's little wonder, then, that a definition and set of guidelines had to be provided for the gene-prediction sweep to indicate what would actu ally count a gene in the contest). Needless to say, getting computers to handle such convolutions poses enormous challenges.
