# Chapter 6 Algorithms and complexity

We see, that when choosing or designing an appropriate algorithm to solve a particular problem, we must take into account a range of criteria that may not be apparent on first inspection. Most, include:

+   * **Size of input**. This directly affects the speed of the algorithm: the more data there are processed, the longer the processing times.
* **Scalability of input**. Beyond the speed of scalability: if the input will always be on a small scale, time and space complexities can be practically ignored; however, the algorithm could receive a wide range of inputs, then complexity becomes an issue. Thus, it is prudent to take into account the possibility of future scales of data, in order not to choose an algorithm that will eventually become a hindrance.
* **Nature of input**. Sometimes, the exact nature of the input (whether a list is already sorted) can dramatically affect the efficiency of an algorithm; it may be necessary to consider whether those cases are likely to occur often.
* **Frequency**. If an algorithm must execute only once a month, its efficiency in time may not too big an issue. However, if it must be executed every time a user moves the mouse pointer, every time the computer screen refreshes, or whenever some highly frequent event occurs, efficiency in time becomes one of the most important restrictions.
* **Maintainability**. Even if an algorithm is efficient in time and space, if it's highly complex in terms of its required data structures and required processing, it may be advisable to choose a less efficient but less complex alternative. Complex code leads to more bugs, potentially losing stability and maintainability.

Of course, everything we've discussed so far about choosing or designing algorithms has revolved around the deceptively simple task of sorting a sequence of items. In many ways, this is one of the simplest faced by a software developer; yet, choosing the right algorithm can be almost an art form.

As the lens of bioinformatics is being brought to bear on increasingly complex and larger amounts of data, the issues described in this chapter have become importantly. With the quantities of data now being produced by high-throughput technologies, the complexity of algorithms has started to become an issue. We must do everything we can to mitigate these issues if we expect our software to perform well in this new world.
