# Preface

## 0.1 Who this book is for, and why

As you pick up this book and flick through its pages, we'd like to interrupt you a moment to tell you who we think this book is for, and why. Let's start with the why. One main thought prompted us to tackle this project. There are now many degree courses in bioinformatics throughout the world, and many excellent accompanying textbooks. In general, these texts tend to focus on how to use standard bioinformatics tools, or on how to implement the algorithms and get programming languages that underpin them. The 'how to use' approach is appropriate for students who want to be familiar with the tools of trade quickly so that they can apply them to their own interests; the 'what's behind' approach might appeal more to students who want to go on to develop their own software and databases. Books like this are extremely useful, but there's something missing – bioinformatics is not just about writing faster programs or creating new databases; it's also about coupling solid computer science with an appreciation of how computers can (or can't) solve biological problems.

During the years we've been working together, we realised that this is seldom, if ever, tackled head-on – it's an issue rooted in the nature of bioinformatics itself. Bioinformatics is often described as the interface where computer science and biology meet and overlap, as shown in Figure 0.1.

![](img/1af42ff402ef4720886c50af0bb8e9b5_11_0.png)

Figure 0.1 The traditional view of bioinformatics.

While reading, you'll find that we've emboldened some of the more obscure or subject-specific phrases that we've used. These terms are defined in the glossary section.

![](img/1af42ff402ef4720886c50af0bb8e9b5_12_0.png)

Figure 0.2
Mind The Gap.

However, the reality is modern bioinformatics has become a discipline of its own right — bigger, broader and more complex than this seemingly trivial overlap might suggest, and bringing with it many new challenges of its own. The reality, then, tends to look more like Figure 0.2.

A common difficulty for bioinformatics courses is that they often have to cater for mixed audiences with diverse backgrounds in biology and computer science. As a result, generally isn't enough time to cover all of the basic biology and all of the basic informatics, and even less time to deal with some of the emerging issues in computer science and how these relate to bioinformatics. It's hard to do justice to each of these disciplines on its own, let alone to tackle the problems that emerge when they're brought together.

For teachers and students, this book is therefore an attempt to bridge the interdisciplinary gap, to address some of the challenges for bioinformatics at the interface of biology and computer science. Compared to other textbooks, it offers rather different perspectives on the challenges, and on the relationship between these disciplines necessary for the science of bioinformatics in the future. It will appeal, we hope, to 'how to write that program'; it will speak to students who want to look beyond 'how to use this tool' or discover why the things we want to do in bioinformatics are not straightforward as they should be; it will resonate with those who understand how we can use computer science to make such tasks straightforward than they currently are; and it will challenge the thoughtful to reflect on where the limits are in terms of what we want computers to be able to do and what's actually doable.

## 0.2 Who has written this book

The authors of the book have backgrounds in bioinformatics and computer science. We've worked together successfully for many years, but early in our relationship we found it harder to communicate what we imagined it could or should be. It wasn't just about meeting a different discipline head and having to grapple with new terminology — the real problem was that we often thought we understood each other, only to find later that we actually meant quite different things, despite having used what appeared to be the same vocabulary. Learning new terminology is relatively easy; learning to spot that the thing you mean and the thing you mean is much harder! It's a bit like trying to read the bottom line of an optician's test chart - the scale of the problem only comes when viewed from another perspective: the optician provides the correct lens, and clarity demands.

The scale of communication problem was thrown into focus when we began developing software together. Initially, there was the usual learning curve to negotiate, as we each had to understand the jargon of the other's discipline. When our software eventually emerged, we were invited to give a talk about it. It was only when we tried to write it, and stood back and looked at the work in a different way, that a kind of fog lifted - thinking we'd been speaking the same language, we'd actually reached this point by understanding different things by using the same words. This revelation provided a catalyst for the book: if we could easily have appeared understanding through misunderstanding of basic concepts, could bioinformatics be a similar house of cards, waiting to dispel? We reasoned that our scientific perspectives, focused on straightforward bioinformatics applications, might help to shed a light on this question. So, let's now briefly introduce our backgrounds – this should explain why the book is written in the way that it is, and why it contents what it does.

### 0.2.1 The biophysicist

The biophysicist isn't really a biologist and certainly isn't a computer scientist. She was engaged in her postdoctoral work on protein sequence and structure analysis when the field of bioinformatics was just emerging; some of it rubbed off. The chapters of the book derive mostly from those early experiences, when the term bioinformatics was more or less synonymous with sequence analysis. Thus, sequence analysis provides an important backdrop for the book. Of course, it is not the only theme, either of the book or of bioinformatics in general – it's a convenient place to start, a place that's both historically and conceptually easy to build from, and one that's arguably more relevant than it was when the discipline began. To maintain an appropriate focus, then we use sequence analysis as our springboard and, in deference to their expertise, we leave other aspects of bioinformatics to the relevant specialists in those particular fields.
