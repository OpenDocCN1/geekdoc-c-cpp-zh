Most children are taught, from a very young age, to learn by modelling this situation in the language of maths. Doing this involves transforming the real-world quantities (apples) into pure mathematical objects (numbers) – something we eventually learn to do without thinking – then, using mathematical relationships, to describe the fruity theft. That model will then look like this:

9 - 2 = ?

From here, it's a straightforward task to apply the rules of maths to solve the model, and in this case, discover the missing number 7. The final step, the reverse of the first, is to translate this solution back to the real-world scenario in order to discover that Sarah is now in possession of seven apples. As simplistic as this example clearly is, neatly shows the two complementary tasks of problem solving: modelling, the process of describing a problem in abstract terms; and processing, the process of manipulating the model according to its rules in order to find a solution. For anything but the simplest of problems, modelling requires sufficiently careful thought; any non-trivial problem will require multiple processing steps in its solution. It is the description of these complex processes that we call algorithms.
