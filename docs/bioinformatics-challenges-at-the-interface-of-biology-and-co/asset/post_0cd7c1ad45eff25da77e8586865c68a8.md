## 5.8 Problems with alignments

So far in this chapter, we've been looking at some of the issues relating to nomenclature, database IDs and ACKs, database entry versions, and structure variants, and so on. Here we begin to touch on slightly more abstract problems. In the field of protein sequence analysis, a common task is to compare sequences by creating an alignment of some sorts. This stands straightforward, from the necessary input and output formats, to the underlying alignment algorithms, to the ways in which we visualise and interpret the results. Let’s take a closer look.

## 5.2 Membrane proteins

Cells and organelles are shielded from their environments by membranes. The protection afforded by such compartmentalisation, is crucial. Yet, biological processes are dynamic and depend on the regulated transfer of molecules between cells and organelles. Integral membrane proteins play critical roles as heroes, mediators, between cells and organelles. Among membrane proteins are three important classes – transporters, receptors and enzymes whose structural and functional differences are determined by how many times they span the membrane. Transceptors transport 'signals' rather than substates across membranes. Many have 7TM architectures, which normally mediate cell responses by binding a ligand of some kind G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), which belong to the general class of 7TM receptors, have various modes of activation, illustrated in Figure 5.7. Some binding ligands within their 7TM scaffolds (a); other binding ligands within N-terminal regions (b), which the ligand interacts with through extracellular loops (c); the others are unusual in having ligands covalently within the TM framework. In each case, the change in the membrane or the change in conformation triggered by ligand binding, causes further changes to percolate through the scaffold and disrupt the bound trimeric G protein. The dislodged G proteins disperse, in turn, terminating their message to other cellular components (d), stimulating intracellular signaling cascades.

Because the primary structures of membrane proteins usually snake back and forth the membrane (hence, some receptors are referred to as 'serpentine'), tell-tale signs are left in their sequences – i.e., hydrophobic regions around 20 residues long. The likelihood of a sequence being a membrane protein is therefore often predicted (inevitably, not always reliable e.g., see Wong et al., 2010) by the appearance of multiple TM domains – seven in the case of GPCRs.
