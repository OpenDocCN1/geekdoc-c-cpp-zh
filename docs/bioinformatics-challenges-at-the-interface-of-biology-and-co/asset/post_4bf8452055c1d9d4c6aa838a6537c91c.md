
## Chapter 4 Biological sequence analysis

Up now, we've discussed in very simple terms how biological sequences have been annotated and stored in databases, and how database records can be linked together, via unique accession numbers and database identifiers, to allow navigation between them. We now need to think about how annotations, particularly those used to help structurally and functionally characterise sequences, are actually derived using different analysis techniques.

## 4.3 Tools for deriving sequence annotations

Many analysis tasks require researchers not simply to navigate between related database entries but, to seek relationships at the level of the amino acid (or nucleotide) sequences themselves. Typically, this might involve searching a database with an uncharacterised sequence, or sequence fragment, to which no annotation has yet been attached, to uncover clues to its likely function, structure or evolutionary history. In the following sections, we explore how the degree of similarity between sequences is typically quantified and the nature of their relationships surmised.

### 4.3.1 Methods for comparing two sequences

To determine how one sequence is to another, the naive approach is to align them, one below the other, and to introduce additional 'gap' characters, if the sequences differ in length, to bring the character strings into vertical register. In the simple example shown in 4.13(a), two short, highly similar sequence fragments are compared: fragment 1 is one amino acid shorter than fragment 2. To create the optimal alignment between sequences requires a single gap to be inserted after the 7th amino acid, as shown in 4.13(b). To deduce the alignment scores, we simply count the number of identical residues between the sequences: in this example, the unaligned sequence is 7 and the aligned score is 19.

Usually, we want to compare longer sequences — often, many hundreds of amino acids in length. The comparison process is consequently much more complex, involving the alignment of many non-identical residues and the positioning of gaps, generally of different length, in a variety of locations. Computing the optimal alignment of identical matches between the sequences by inserting gaps in an unrestricted way; however, this would not yield a biologically meaningful result. In practice, we introduce scoring penalties (to minimise the number of gaps that are opened) and extension penalties (to inhibit their length, open): the alignment score is then a function of the number of identical residues and the gap penalties incurred during the alignment.

In this discussion, we've considered only residue identities and scored them using a unitary matrix: i.e., a matrix that assigns a score of 1 to identical residue matches, and 0 to non-identical matches (see Figure 4.14). Unitary matrices are sparse – that is to say, most of their cells are populated with zeros. Consequently, their diagnostic power is poor, because all matches carry equal weight and non-identical matches contribute to the score, even if they're biologically meaningful. Ideally, to improve diagnostic performance, we would be able to boost the potential of weak but biologically significant sequences so that they too can contribute to the overall score, without the risk of amplifying noise. This tension is the very essence of sequence analysis – the desire to be able to distinguish low-scoring, biologically relevant matches from high-scoring, mathematically significant but biologically irrelevant matches.

In the next section, we briefly look at some of the characteristics of the two most popular series of similarity matrices: the PAM and BLOSUM matrices.

## Box 4.2 Visualising the effects of unitary and similarity scoring

A simple device to help envisage the similarity shared by two sequences is the dotplot. A dotplot is essentially just a matrix (Maizel and Lenk, 1981) in which the sequences being compared are arranged along the x and y axes, and the matrix cells are populated by the scores of successive pairwise residue comparisons. Using the unitary scoring matrix, all identities score 1, and all non-identical matches have zero score. For, for identical sequences, the plot is generalised by an unbroken diagonal; but comparing identical sequences is seldom useful! For the most realistic scenario, where two similar sequences are compared, the plot will be characterised by a broken diagonal, where the interrupted regions indicate the locations of the residue-matches. Within the plot, a few features are noteworthy. The first, principal diagonal broken in two places: the initial interruption denotes the insertion of a serine in the y-axis relative to the x-axis; the second denotes a substitution of glycine for alanine in the y-axis. Importantly, the insertion causes not only a break in the diagonal but also an offset – the of offsets the number of gaps required to line the two sequences into register. The second sequence is shown in the plot as a pair of off-diagonal diagonals. Cluster inspection reveals that there denotes a repetition at the C terminus of a two-letter 'word'; HG – histidine-glycine pair—that’s seen first along the sequences. The dotplots are very efficient and powerful tools for pinpointing the positions of internal repeats within sequences. The final point is to note, that aside the main diagonal and a few off-diagonal matches, most elements of the matrix are empty – the matrix plot gives an al-diagonal view of the degree of identity between the two sequences, and indicates how they can be lined i. e., by joining the dots on the principal (black) diagonal, taking into account mis-matches and offsets resulting from indel.

As mentioned, the scoring potential of unitary matrices is relatively poor, because only identity matrices are used. This prompted the introduction of a variety of substitution matrices, such as the PAM and BLOSUM series, which allow biologically feasible amino acid replacements also to be counted. To illustrate the effect of similarity scoring, compare the dotplots in Figures 4.15 and 4.16; the latter use the same pair of sequence fragments as in Figure 4.13, but with similarities scored using the BLOSUM 62 substitution matrix. It is immediately apparent that the matrix is no longer sparse, but is densely populated with negative (red) and positive (blue) scores – very few matrix elements are empty, as before (Figure 4.16).
