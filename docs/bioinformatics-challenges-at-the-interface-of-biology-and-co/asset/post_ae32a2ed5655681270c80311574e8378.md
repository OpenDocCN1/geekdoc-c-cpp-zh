Such complexity presents significant challenges to computational systems, even if we know the particular building-blocks that comprise a protein, if we don't understand their biological context, we may not be able to infer the protein's overall function. For example, if we identify the function of a module or domain, this tells us about the function of the complete protein; alternatively even if we know components of a mosaic or multi-domain protein, then we can easily predict whether one of the modules/domains is missing; and modules in different proteins don't always perform exactly the same function.

Appreciating the complexity of biological systems and understanding the detailed evolutionary processes that have generated biochemical, cellular and developmental innovations is ever if we are able to make meaningful inferences of protein function (see Box 5.6). Comparative genomics has shed light on still complicating factors: functions may be redundant; non-orthologous displacement can replace genes with unrelated but functionally analogous genes; horizontal gene transfer among genes from different phylogenetic lineages (xenology); and lineage-specific gene loss can eliminate assembly. Genomes harbour obstacles that defy an intuitive correlation between sequence, structure and function, and render reliable, fully-automatic computational assignment of protein function virtually impossible.

## Box 5.6 Biological complexity

During the last decades, advances in computational and experimental technologies have allowed us to glimpse our own genetic recipes. Yet, paradoxically, the methods we use to deduce the functions of the encoded gene proteins are primarily based on quantification of sequence similarities and often, apparent inheritance of GO terms. How has this situation arisen?

The answer lies partly in the complexity, uncertainty and unpredictability of biological systems. Biological systems are hierarchical, where higher-order systems are built from simpler components. Each level of the hierarchy is constrained by its components, but new properties may also emerge, each imposing additional constraints on the system. This has important consequences: the necessity to analyse complex entities at all levels; it is the realisation that complex systems aren't predictable. In other words, with some knowledge of level of the hierarchy, we can only make limited predictions at that level.

Complex systems have emergent properties, which are more than just the sum of their parts. Rashevsky encapsulate this when he argued that no collection of descriptions or methods of organisms can be assembled in such a way to capture the organism itself (Rashevsky, 1954). Today, biophysicists, confronted with the complexity and uncertainty of biological systems, and the consequent of modelling them computationally, are re-discovering that biological systems are bags of detail that cannot provide pictures of the whole. Overwhelming is the degree of complexity of such processes (blood coagulation, inflammatory reactions against foreign bodies, the immunological defense mediated by the complex system, etc.)

For the biologist, it is generally possible to make a prediction, or ever an inspired guess, the nature of such molecules and their structural relations with other systems (Jacob, 1977).

Another complex system is signal transduction, mediated by the ubiquitous GPCRs. GPCRs constitute one of the largest and most diverse groups of cell-surface molecules, and provide a beautiful example of Jacob ‘molecular tinkering’ – these highly successful proteins have adapted a common heptarchical framework to manage a vast range of physiological processes, including the perception of light, taste, smell and pain, the effects of numerous therapeutic agents, the attraction of motive cells by chemotaxis, signalling and regulation of mitosis, the entry of viruses into cells and so on. Such diversity is achieved via interactions with a wide variety of ligands, including peptide and non-peptide neurotransmitters, hormones, growth factors, pheromones, odors and light.

The G proteins with GPCRs are constitutively-associated heterotrimers, with α β and γ subunits. The model for how this works is like this: when the receptor is charged by its ligand, a conformational change is triggered; this induces a conformational change in the α subunit, causing GDP to be released and replaced by GTP. GTP-binding induces the α subunit to dissociate from the receptor and complex to interact with intracellular molecules (‘second messengers’ cAMP and calcium) to then pass the message by altering behaviour of other cells.

Among a range of intracellular molecules, through the possible mechanisms which they transduce environmental signals. For example, the adaptor molecule arrestin couples GPCRs to the activation of Src-like kinases and the formation of multi-molecular complexes. Structural components (e.g., poly-carotene regions, PDZ, and SH3 domains) also facilitate GPCR coupling to a variety of signaling molecules. Overall, this diversity is elicited by GPCRs due to the complexity of mechanisms by which they transduce signals. Most of these responses don't depend on a single route, but the integrated result of an intricate network of cytoplasmic pathways. Individual responses depend on receptor levels, coupling specificity and the nature of signaling molecules. As systems complexity, it becomes increasingly difficult to predict their behaviour. Perturbations in such systems have multiple effects on the level of genes, transcripts, and metabolites. In biological systems, it is therefore inevitable that we should try to reduce them to more manageable components (traditionally, science has been reductive). Yet, by doing so, we alter the contexts in which they operate, which is leading to increasing levels of uncertainty in the predictions we then make.
