Dayhoff had to continue gathering sequences from literature sources and from bench scientists, and wasn't particularly interested in matters of provenance (Eck and Dayhoff, 1966); the Los Alamos team, by contrast, advocated collaboration with journal editors to deposit their sequences into the database as part of their routine publication process. This latter approach was especially compelling, as it would allow scientists to assert priority, and to keep their research results until formally published and their provenance established; perhaps more importantly, it was unencumbered by proprietary interest in the data. At the time, it was considered paramount that the data and associated software tools be free and open – that the highly secured area of what may have appeared to some as The Atomic City¹ had been chosen as the home for the new international database were therefore somewhat ironic.

As an aside, it's interesting that the vision of free data and software was advocated so strongly at this time, not least because there was no funding model to support it. Thirty years later, the same arguments are vehemently opposed with regard to free databases, free software and free literature (e.g., Lathrop et al., 2011); but even now, database funding remains an unresolved and controversial issue. Returning to our theme, the final nail in the coffin of Dayhoff’s proposal was that the NBRF had only limited means of data distribution (via modems). The Los Alamos, on the other hand, had the enormous benefit of being able to distribute their data via ARPANET, the computer network of the US Department of Defense. Together, these advantages were sufficient to swing the pendulum in favour of the Los Alamos team, who became the custodians of the new GenBank.
