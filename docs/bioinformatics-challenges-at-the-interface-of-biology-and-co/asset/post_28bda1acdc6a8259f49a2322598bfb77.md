## Afterword

In this book, we've wandered through the history of bioinformatics and computer science, exploring some of the discoveries and challenges that have arisen between these disciplines, and stopping into some gaps that exist where they haven't quite met. Our starting point was an archipelago of research islands. For the first leg of the journey, the challenges facing early pioneers involved the primitive computing of the day to store, retrieve and display data that were generated from isolated, largely manual experiments. We've ended up in a much more interconnected world in which vast amounts of data can be generated automatically, where relationships are becoming as significant as topics themselves, and where challenges involve using today's technology — not simply to store and retrieve and extract meaning from the information we've collected.

There's no doubt that we've come a long way. However, as impressive as the vista of our achievements may seem, it's also clear that this isn't the end of the journey; and it's perhaps sobering to realise that many of the challenges alluded to in this book remain unsolved. Thirty years ago when computational tools for gene sequence analysis were created, James lamented that the ideal tool has to be created. He went on to list five desirable properties of such utopian software, essentially he said:

1. free: publicly available, cross-platform, and open source, with architecture described in peer-reviewed publications;
2. comprehensive: able to draw on a wide variety of leading resources;
3. updated: regularly, coordinated with releases of functional databases;
4. smart: capable of automatically recognizing relevant species and relationships;
5. flexible: allowing a choice of analytical methods and output formats.

Bioinformatics challenges at the interface of biology and computer science: Mind the Gap. First Edition. Teresa K. Atwood, Stefan Pfeiffer and David Thorne. Published 2016 © 2016 by John Wiley and Sons, Ltd. Companion website: www.wiley.com/en-us/atwood/bio

While the desirability of the items on Hutchins' wish-list could easily be dismissed as 'obvious', the list nevertheless provides a useful vehicle for exploring the obstacles that lie ahead on the next stage of the bioinformatics journey, for understanding why we are where we are, and why many things are still unsolved.

Taking these one by one, from a technical point of view, there are interesting challenges associated with each of the five properties: with today's programming languages and frameworks, creating cross-platform software is much harder than building something that works for one operating system; being able to access a 'comprehensive' array of third-party databases dealing with multiple schemes; benefiting from database 'updates' necessarily involves evolution of the tool with the growing scale of data or to adapt to new features; 'smart' behaviour likely implies the use of complex semantic technologies; 'flexibility' requires rigorous adherence to software-engineering principles in order to create tools that are extensible and refactoring to accommodate new requirements. All of these are technically harder than achieving but are necessary to create tools that can be shared and have any possibility of living beyond the lifetime of a given project. The interesting point here is that these are, from a technical perspective, just hard to achieve but we have seen through, not impossible.

The reason we are not awash with tools and systems that embody Hutchins' utopian wish-list is much more complex and subtle, and we, suggest, to do with money, politics and ultimately, human nature with any specific technical hurdle. Hurdling each of the five points is the implication that tools have to be maintained and updated in order to keep them in sync with other tools or to prevent them from rotting as underlying operating systems evolve and programming languages and frameworks rise and fall in popularity. Achieving this requires not just initial effort to use the best software-engineering paradigms and the latest interoperability standards, but sustained, long-term effort – that requires sustained, long-term funding.

The paradox is perhaps revealed in Hutchins' first point: funding bodies and users want software and data to be 'free' – i.e. free in the sense of not having to pay for them, however software and data need to be maintained and supported indefinitely so that they remain reliable resources on which to base current and future research. In 2005, Scott McNealy, then-CEO of Microsystems, quipped 'Open Source is free like a puppy is free' – in other words, while you may be able to acquire your little canine component without paying any money, or if you don't it and pay the vet's bills, you can't expect it to live long. And, just like puppies, figuring out how to make software and databases free and sustainable presents almost insurmountable challenges, resulting in many of them being abandoned by the wayside.

To give some idea, in 2004, Bairoch and his colleagues reflected on how tough it really is to develop and maintain high-quality databases for the life sciences (Bairoch et al., 2004). They enumerated six observations that they deemed essential for well-behaved developers to:

* Your task will be much more complex and bigger than you ever thought it could be.
* If your database is successful and useful to the user community, then you will have to dedicate all your efforts to develop it for a much longer period of time than you would thought possible.
