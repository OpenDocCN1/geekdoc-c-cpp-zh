This process of being able to exchange extends to all aspects of our lives; we are quite adept at dealing with the tacit and implicit assumptions, as well as the ambiguities, that arise in our day-to-day communications.

Computers, have none of these faculties: once in a while, it's important to remember that, for all their sophistication, the only thing that computers can really do is shuffle electrons around a silicon chip. The fact that patterns of electrons can be used to represent numbers; that numbers can be used as letters; that letters can form words which, in turn, represent concepts that are meaningful to us is merely by a series of conventions. In order to be able to solve biological problems, we need mechanisms for turning the tacit assumptions and implicit conventions that underpin the ways in which we identify, represent and exchange data, information and knowledge-into explicit machine-readable formats.

In this chapter, we explore how the fruits of the biomedical sciences – both the raw data collected by experiment, and the data and information that result from their analysis – can be retrieved and again for further use, and we will consider how computers can be used to represent and reason about ‘knowledge’. We will discuss the best technologies available to us, many found pervasive in the field, and why some approaches are better than others. In particular, we will look at the gap between the ideal solutions available to modern computer science and the reality of what’s actually used in today, and what gulf exists.
