You've written the introduction, and now realise a diagram is needed to help illustrate some point or other. You create the diagram in your favourite drawing package, and it's the first in your document, so you call it Figure1.png, and insert it into the right place in your text. A little later, you realise this diagram is a bit too complicated to be in the introductory material – you need a simpler version early on, but the one will serve well later on to explain some of the detail. So you draw your simplified diagram, and are about to say about it when you realise there's a problem: you have already called it Figure1.png, but now this is going to be the first in your document. So what do you do? You rename the previous diagram before saving this new one – but you don't know where so perhaps call it FigureX.png. Or you could call your new illustration Figure0.png or Figure1.png or go BeforeWhatWasFigure1.png; now you recognise that the more you edit the document, the more files will become meaningless as they diverge from the order in which they appear in your report. A filename naming convention, designed to make it easy for you to keep track of which file goes, has suddenly become more of a hindrance than help.

In this scenario, the filename as an *identifier* that allows you to tell your word-processor which thing (the diagram) you want to include in a particular in your report. The word-processor, of course, doesn't really care what you called the file – it just needs a way of figuring out which file you mean. The problem arises because your choice of filename explicitly encodes the *location* of the figure with respect to your document (at least, that was the intention). So when location changes, there's a mismatch between what the filename appears to ‘mean’, and where the figure actually ends up. As the document is edited – perhaps the figures are reused in a different order in a different document – the ‘meaning’ of the filename becomes increasingly muddled. So imagine a scenario where Figure1.png is now Figure 10 in your report, and Figure 14 in another (and a filename of OriginallyFigure1ButNowFigure10InReportAndFigure14InReportB.png is probably unhelpful).

The problem we're seeing is caused by the fact that the identifier (filename) encodes some information about the object it identifies (the location of the illustration in the report); when the nature of the object changes, that relationship is broken. In this scenario, the meaning is being interpreted by a human, and the job of uniting the names and locations is a manual one, which, though tedious, is manageable. But the issues become pernicious when computers start interpreting the identifiers, and on such a large scale.

For example, *Arabidopsis protein*, originally deposited in TrEMBL and given identifier Q9C923³, was manually annotated and became GCR2ARATH4 in UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot. However, the protein had later been erroneously annotated as a GPCR, and was later found to be a Lanc-like protein (which makes it an enzyme, not a membrane protein); so, GCR2 (which presumably stands for the second GPCR to have been found in *Arabidopsis*, after the discovery of GCR1 – which is a GCR1P) is misleading if a human or machine tries to infer meaning from the structure of the identifier.
