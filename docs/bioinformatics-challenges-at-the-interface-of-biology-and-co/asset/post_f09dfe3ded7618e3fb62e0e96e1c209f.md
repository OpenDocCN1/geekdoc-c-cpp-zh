All the potentials of an individual may be inscribed in polypeptide chains that are actually synthesized, or could be synthesized, by the cells under certain circumstances, and in the structures that control the actual and potential rates of this synthesis (Zuckerkland and Pauling, 1965).

With great precision, they envisaged biomolecular sequences as ‘documents of evolutionary history’; nevertheless, they recognised that extracting these sequestered histories would require more sequence information than then available. As the field of molecular evolution dawned, our hunger to understand more about the functions and structures of biological macromolecules provided the impetus for further sequencing efforts. Margaret Dayhoff, who had a keen interest in discerning relationships was among the first scientists to approach this work systematically. In the early 1960s, to facilitate both her own research and the work of others in the field, she began to collect protein sequence information from scientific papers. This compounding of sequences was eventually published as a book – the Atlas of Protein Sequence and Structure (Dayhoff et al., 1965), often simply referred to as the Atlas. Interestingly, she wrote a letter in 1967, that, observed,

There is a tremendous amount of information regarding the evolutionary history and bio-chemical function in each sequence and the number of known sequences is growing explosively [our emphasis]. We feel it is important to collect this significant information, correlate it into a unified whole and interpret (Dayhoff, 1967; Strasser, 2008).

With the creation of the first Atlas, that ‘explosive growth’ amounted to 65 sequences!

During the next decade, the advent of automated processes overtook time-consuming manual peptide sequencing and dramatically increased the rate of sequence determination. In the meantime, another revolution was taking place, spurred by the elucidation of the first protein almost structures using the technique of X-ray crystallography: those of myosin and hemoglobin (Krewend et al., 1958; Muirhead and Perutz, 1963). Building on the sequencing work, this advance set the scene for a new era in which structure determination was to take centre stage in our understanding of the orophysical mechanisms and evolutionary processes. Suctive was this that many more structural studies were initiated, and the number of deduced structures burgeoning.

In parallel with these developments, advancements in sequencing technology in the late 1970s meant that, for the first time, DNA sequences could be determined, and their protein products deduced from them, relatively quickly. Incredibly, if it had taken 8-10 years to determine the sequences of few small proteins (insulin and ribonuclease), dozens of protein sequences could now be rapidly deduced by translation of sequenced DNA.

The technologies that gave rise to manual peptide-sequencing strategies, then to automated peptide and DNA sequencing, and to protein structure determination at atomic resolution, were responsible for producing the first waves of sequence and structural data. Key to handling this expanding information was the recruitment of computers to help analyse and store the accumulating data. Initially, newly determined sequences were published in the literature to make them available to the wider community. In this form, any researcher wishing to exploit the information had to obtain a copy of the original article, and then to type the sequence(s) into a computer by hand, a process that now seems unbelievable.

Eventually, because collating data into electronic repositories would make it more efficient and easier to store and use the data in future. This realisation led to the birth of the first electronic databases. At this time, the idea that molecular information could be managed using electronic repositories was not only very new, but also very daunting. Consider that technologies we take for granted today (email, the Internet, the World-Wide Web) had not yet emerged; therefore there was no simple way to distribute data from a central database, other than posting tapes and disks to users on request. This method of data distribution was fraught with difficulties; it was cumbersome and slow; it was also relatively costly, and, alarmingly, some of the first databases to pioneer did adopting and/or data-sharing policies that threatened to drive away many of their potential users.

From these tentative awakenings, few first biomolecular databases emerged. From the agonizing slow trickle of determining a single sequence per decade, to a speed of thousands of sequences per second (a rate that will seem inconsequential years from now), sequencing technology has revolutionised the pace of data acquisition, and thrown up new challenges for the field of bioinformatics.

Of course, mathematics and the process of ‘computing’ answers using mathematical notations, evolved considerably over the centuries, and its history is far too complex to recount and discuss; besides, for the most part, its details are not relevant to the story we tell. There are, however, a couple of notable exceptions. One was by Brahmagupta in the 7th century CE, the idea of an ‘algorithm’: a formal description of a sequence of steps, that carried out in order, accomplish a specific mathematical task. Another was the realisation, in the 3rd century BCE, by Pythagoras, another Indian mathematician, that numbers could be represented in binary notation as patterns of true or false values, on or off states, or simply by the presence or absence of zeroes. Centuries later, the foundation of computer hardware, with binary numbers being captured, as the presence or absence of holes in a punched card, and as the presence or absence of a change in a valve or transistor. Binary also spawned the idea of ‘logic’ in a formal, mathematical sense, with the concept of algorithms, became the cornerstone of modern software methods.
