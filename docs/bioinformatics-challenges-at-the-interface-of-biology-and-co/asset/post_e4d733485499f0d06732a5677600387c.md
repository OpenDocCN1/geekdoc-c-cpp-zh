
## Chapter 5 The gap

Release 50 of InterPro indicated that there were 107,165 members of the rhodopsin-like G protein-coupled receptor superfamily (IPR00276). This figure was derived from scanning UniProt version 2015_02 over the relevant diagnostic signatures in PROSITE (G_PROTEIN_RECEPT_FI_1, PS002372), PRINTS (GPRCRHODOPSIN, PR002734) and Pfam (7tm_1, PF00001). Drilling down into the partner databases, however, showed that the Pfam HMM matched 42,866 sequences, the PROSITE pattern had 2,213 true-positive matches, and 4,400 partial matches (6,660 in total) – a combined total short of 107,165. These figures are summarised in Table 5.4.

The situation is further complicated when InterPro 50 introduced a separate rhodopsin-like GPCR superfamily entry (IPR07452) that had 114,321 members, a figure derived from the PROSITE profile G_PROTEIN_RECEPT_FI_2 (PS05026). Visiting the PROSITE profile itself, however, revealed 2,433 true-positive matches, plus an 41 extra hits that hit many families. This diversity of numbers and the similar, yet different, superfamily appears baffling. Why are the numbers and the names different? What does it mean?

Explaining some of the numerical discrepancies is relatively straightforward. InterPro takes its partner database models and scales them against whatever is the current version of UniProtKB; but there is added twist – the way in which InterPro implements its algorithms differs from the way the partners implement them. Stepping aside this, the most important factor is likely to be which version of UniProtKB they used. As mentioned above, for the examples in Table 5.4, the quoted InterPro results were derived from UniProtKB 2015_02; the PROSITE pattern and profile were derived from UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot 2015_02; the PRINTS data, were derived from much older versions of Swiss-Prot (a composite of versions 55.0 and 38, respectively, with sequences tagged as fragments removed); the Pfam HMM was derived from UniProtKB 2012_06. What is clear that the the databases for PRINTS and PROSITE are significantly smaller than the source used by InterPro (note that PROSITE is based only on the Swiss-Prot of UniProtKB, smaller number of matches are to expected).

We want to be sure of the extent to which InterPro's implementation of the partner database models affects the overall numbers. The discrepancy between InterPro entries ‘G protein-coupled receptor, rhodopsin-like’ (IPR00276) and ‘GPCR, 7TM’ is perhaps more than the first 107,165 and the latter 114,321 sequences. The question is, why database entries with similar names different of sequences? The answer lies in the difference between the PROSITE pattern and profile designed (according to their IDs, G_PROTEIN_RECEPT_F_1 and G_PROTEIN_RECEPT_F_2) to identify members of the ‘G-coupled receptor families’. As we expect, the profile is more permissive – pernicious than the latter. Consequently, in order to ‘maintain’ rhodopsin-like GPCRs, it also matched 41 taste receptors. These were not included in the reported 2,433 true-positive set, but were annotated in the database as ‘unknowns’. According to Pfam, the taste receptors belong to a separate family within the GPCR_A clan, designated TAS2R (PF05296) – a list of Pfam’s GPCR_A clans is given in Table 5.3.

What we have observed is that because the InterPro profile matched sequences not matched by Pfam or PRINTS, the InterPro curators (rather than attempting to resolve the discrepancy) simply made a separate entry, with highly similar name but different accession number. This is unfortunate. The result is especially confusing, as in Inter release 50, IPR07452 had 48 percent overlap with IPR00276, the set of sequences containing 99.9 per of the set. The additional sequences matched by IPR07452 include the taste receptors matched by the profile, and, in addition, a variety of vomeronasal receptors, proteome IX farnyltransferases, uncharacterised proteins and other curiosities – but not note, that, overall, this is a much cleaner result compared to the version of this entry formerly found in InterPro release 48.0, which was confounded by bacterhodopsins, heat-shock sensory proteins, rhodopsins, halorhodopsins, archaea-hodopsins, and even chromonasal c oxidase subunit II. If there is some debate about the relationship of vision and taste receptors to rhodopsin-like GPCRs, there is certainly no doubt about rhodopsin and its associates – they may share a 7TM scaffold, but they are not G protein-coupled receptors. Adding to the muddle, is is acknowledged in the annotation of entry IPR07452, which states that it spans the transmembrane regions of rhodopsin-like. This is a classic result.

If the situation for this one GPCR is confounding, what might we find? In December 2015, a search of InterProt with keyword ‘GPCR’ yielded 120 matches, including GPCR, family 2-like (IPR01798), GPCR, family 2, secretin (IPR002144), GPCR, family 3-like (IPR00832). Given this small sample, the classification of the sequences will not be simple to ascertain. Part of the problem is that databases have become dependent on automatic search tools; most not have time to annotate the results in detail, especially when families and superfamilies are large, like the GPCRs. To understand evolutionary relationships for this important group, we really need to consult more detailed analyses, such as those of Nordstrom et al. (2009), to put results on a more solid biological footing.
