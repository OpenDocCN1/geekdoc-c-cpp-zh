# 7.3.4 Cool URIs don't change

In a 'style note' from the W3C, Tim Berners-Lee describes a 'cool URI' as being 'one which does not change', pointing out that there's no technical reason (because the insolvency of a domain name's) for a URI to become invalid or forfeit the wrong thing. The note provides a list of things not to include in a URI, such as a document's authors, subject, status (draft, final, etc.), filename extension, and so on, all of which are likely to change over time. Noting 'that the change detected, putting any identifier in name is asking for the object one or another', he implies that 'cool URIs' are essentially opaque. As tempting as it might be to create a few characters that humans can remember, it's best to accept that nothing's more than a few of characters matters and machines are happy with a happy resource 101127123875t7nfil they are with /protein/gcr/rhodopsin.

# 7.3.5 Versioning and provenance

Almost inevitably, an entity for which an identifier will get modified in some way, given enough time: it may be updated, or deleted; it may be superseded by a newer entity, or related to some other entities. All these things are possible; each has occurred times throughout the history of every major bioinformatics database, and there's nothing special about this moment in time that prevents it from happening again; but what does this mean for our identifier?

The simplest thing that can be said is that our new-modified identifier longer points at what we used to point at; depending on the nature of the change, this may invalidate the identifier. In this case, we have a choice: discard it, with any information we have stored about it, or continue to use it to represent our (now out-of-date) entity? The answer depends on the nature of the change and the data we're identifying; either way, we run the risk of losing some crucial information. The difficulty here is the lack of context about what the change actually means. A database record may be deleted because a new version has been created where that replaces it, or it may have simply retired. In the framework, how are we to know which new version resides? Even if we still have access to the updated version, why was it not have to correct spelling mistakes, to add literature citations, to fix a sequence error, or to completely reclassify a protein; somewhere on that spectrum, we could argue that the new version no longer represents the 'same thing' as the old; but this distinction may not be obvious.
