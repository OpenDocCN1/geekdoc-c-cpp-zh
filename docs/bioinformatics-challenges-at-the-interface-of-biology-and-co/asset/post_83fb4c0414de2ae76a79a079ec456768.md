Within each block, the constituent sequences were clustered to a percentage identity greater than specific cut-off value. Sequences in the same cluster were weighted as a single sequence, thereby helping to reduce any bias introduced by groups of identical and near-identical sequences (of which there were likely to be many block). The log-odds matrix resulting from a particular clustering percentage is referred to as number BLOSUM matrix: e.g., BLOSUM 62 (see Figure 4.19), which includes substitutions between sequences that are <62 per cent identical; BLOSUM 80, which includes substitutions between sequences that are <80 per cent identical. Note that, by choosing PAM matrices, the higher the BLOSUM number, the greater the similarity represented by the matrix.

BLOSUM 62 became a popular scoring matrix, often replacing PAM250 as the default in sequence analysis and alignment programs. This is probably because the Henikoffs published evidence that their matrices performed better in alignments and similarity searches than those based on accepted mutations, and in particular, BLOSUM 62 was the single best matrix (Henikoff and Henikoff, 1992; Henikoff and Henikoff, 1993).

Comparing PAM250 and BLOSUM 62 matrices shown in Figures 4.18 and 4.19, we can see that key trends are generally preserved in terms of which amino acids are the most and least unlike; nevertheless, the numerical details differ. This means that we use such matrices for database searches or sequence alignment, the details of the program outputs are also likely to differ slightly. When seeking relationships in the Twilight Zone, where the overall resemblance between sequences is slight, such disparities could cause spotting a distant relationship or missing it.

## 4.3.3 Tools for global and local alignment

By now, it should be evident that we create and score sequence alignments by invoking particular mathematical models whose behaviour we can manipulate by modifying a range of parameters (such as penalties for opening and extending gaps, amino acid substitutions, and so on). To this extent, there is no right or wrong alignment; there are different models that reflect biological processes.

Two general ways of thinking about algorithms involve consideration either of the degree of similarity across the full sequence lengths or of the similarity confined to specific regions of the sequences: the former results in a global alignment, the latter produces a local alignment. It is important to appreciate these different perspectives: often, it is the features that give rise to local similarity (e.g., binding sites, active sites, hydrophobic regions, structural or functional domains) that we most interested in (see Box 4.4); under these circumstances, algorithms that attempt to optimise alignment over the full sequence lengths may produce more sensitive and biologically meaningful results than those that attempt alignment.

It isn't in the scope of this book to describe in detail algorithms for global and local alignment; instead, we will mention some of the most popular local alignment methods: **Dynamic programming methods**, those of Needleman and Wunsch (1970) and Smith-Waterman (1981), compute the optimal alignment between two for a given scoring system in a time that is proportional to the length of the sequences being compared. When scaling up to a full database search, the computation time increases in proportion to the database size, making calculations slow. To address this problem, approximate (heuristic) algorithms are used to reduce the time to find (not necessarily optimal) alignments. Among the first mentioned algorithms is FAST, developed in 1985 by Lipman and Pearson as an efficient modification of the earlier Wilbur and Lipman algorithm (1983); later, this yielded the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (more commonly known as BLAST), developed by Altschul et al. in 1990; this, in turn, yielded to subsequent innovations, as 'gapped BLAST' and **PSI-BLAST** (Altschul et al., 1997).

The algorithm in BLAST is based on identifying 'k-tuples' that are short words, or sequence fragments, that occur in the sequences being compared, and are detected by sliding a window of length k over the query sequence and each database. The value of k is important, because it determines the speed and sensitivity of the search: e.g., a value of 1 will take into account identities between amino acids, and so on; however, the greater the k, the faster the search but its sensitivity. ## Box 4.1 Identifying local features

As mentioned in Section 4.3.4, in the process of sequence analysis, we are often interested in identifying features that give rise to local similarity, binding sites, active sites, hydrophobic regions, and so on. A method that found quite practical in the detection of local sequence features is the hydrophobic profile, created using a sliding-window technique. Here we illustrate how these can be applied to the detection of transmembrane (TM) membranes.

We saw in Chapter 2 that amino acids have overlapping, context-dependent properties, which can be measured in a variety of ways. A property of particular interest is hydrophobicity or hydropathy because this can be used to determine the likelihood of a protein being global (soluble) or membrane-bound (insoluble). A major driving force in the folding of globular proteins is the hydrophobic effect; the tendency of amino acid residues to sequester in the protein interior, away from surrounding water. In the structures of globular proteins, hydrophobic residues may be relatively far apart in sequence, but never capable of forming stable cores during the dynamics of protein folding. This characteristic of proteins in the sequence bears witness to the nature of the structure(s) is particularly noticeable for membrane proteins, where architectural elements like lipid milieux form, sometimes vast, TM scaffolds, which are predominantly hydrophobic in nature. How do we identify these computationally?
