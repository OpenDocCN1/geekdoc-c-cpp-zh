# **OBO: the Open Biological and Biomedical Ontologies**

The OBO ontologies play a central role in knowledge engineering in the life sciences; being rather than just languages or formats, they require a little more context to explain them. Scientists are using a common language for some of the things they talk about—the prime example must be SI units. Similarly, biologists have, for a long time, had a common language for talking about the sequences of amino acids and nucleotide bases—the long strings of letters written in the format protein and nucleic acid sequences. The single-letter codes for acids and nucleotide bases also allowed development of computational techniques for measuring the similarity between two or more protein or nucleotide sequences.

In the post-genomic era, as genomes became available from many organisms, this common language also enabled measurement of similarities between genomes of organisms. What wasn't possible was comparison of what we know about others e.g., what their genes did and where they did. Each organism community developed its own bespoke vocabulary for talking about organism-specific products, creating incompatible silos of information. A group of scientists realised that a common language or vocabulary was needed to tackle this problem.

Thus born the Gene Ontology, GO (The GO Consortium, 2000), a controlled vocabulary for talking about a gene product in a cell, its molecular function and the processes in which it participates. Now, more than 40 genomes use GO for describing the 맞아 attribute of gene products. This was largely brought about the GO Consortium—the GO was given freely, and was open to import from anyone who used it. All of this was seen to be a Good Thing. As other scientists started their own ontologies (Sequence Ontology (Elsebeek et al., 2009), CheBI (Hastings et al., 2013),.—see Box 7.8 for other others), a need was perceived to coordinate these activities to avoid chaos—from the Open Biological Ontologies Consortium (Smith, 2007). One of their guiding principles was to use a common syntax (and semantics) for their ontologies. GO had its own format, which became the OBO Format; the other recommended representation was OWL. Over the years, as the need to use reasoning to facilitate the maintenance of large, complex ontologies has grown and been realised—they have become aligned to the extent that OBO Format (BOBO) is now essentially an alternative syntax for OWL.
