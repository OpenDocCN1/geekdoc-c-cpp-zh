# 5.10 Problems with functions

Faced with the rich bounty of the genome projects, our imperative is to add value to the steadily accumulating raw data by enhancing them with ‘meaning’. What, for example, does this or what gene product do? Is its function? Fortunately, we have at our disposal the enormous power of today’s computers and a vast array of analytical tools to help answer questions. So do we use technologies to infer biological functions? Function prediction is now mostly performed using automated pipelines that exploit various types of database search; pairwise analyses are routinely performed with tools like BLAST or FASTA; similarity-based searches tend to be performed using resources like InterPro, and information is gathered from a variety of ontological resources.

A number of issues arise when applying annotation approaches. As we see, the family-based methods in their diagnostic power, some (like HHMs and profiles) providing broad, high-level functional diagnoses, others (like patterns and fingerprints) providing finer details. The databases associated with these approaches differ in terms of their coverage; more importantly, the fundamental concept of ‘family’ differs from them, making comparisons difficult. InterPro was developed to address many of these issues, and in fact goes a long ways towards achieving this; nevertheless, it adds layers of complexity to ours.

As we saw in the previous section, one of the biggest problems is the practical definition of what constitutes a family, because the concept of ‘family’ encompasses sequence relationships, functional relationships, structural kinship and evolutionary ancestry. Depending on different perspectives, the nature of families necessarily differs. So, for example, there is general agreement that members of a family must have a demonstrable evolutionary relationship: this is usually established with reference to some specific sequence similarity cut-off (e.g., greater than or equal to 30 per cent.). But common descent (HOMOLOGY – see Box 5.4) may be inferred in the absence of significant sequence identity: some members of the globin family, for example, sequence identities of ~15 per cent. So where should we draw the relatedness line? Proteins can be on the basis of structural similarities, if they have the same secondary structures in the arrangement, with the same topological connections.

Determining membership on the basis of defined levels of sequence identity or gross similarity can lead to problems, because families may be highly similar in sequence but divergent in function, or similar in structure and function but divergent in sequence.
