## 5.1 Overview

In this chapter, we drill down into some of the issues that have arisen as the slow dawn of bioinformatics has given way to the roller-coaster of modern biology. In particular, we examine the consequences of the piecemeal evolution of biological databases and software tools as they've been forced to respond to each new data 'crisis', and to adapt tackle bigger, more complex problems. Specifically, we look more deeply at issues inherent in finding and reconstructing genes from genomic data, problems of sequence and database versioning, the challenges in interpreting sequence alignments, the difficulties in defining protein families and functions, and the thorny problem of nomenclature.

By the end of the chapter, you should have an appreciation of the importance of metaphor and semantics; you should also have insights into how semantic technologies are gaining traction in addressing the challenges of high-throughput biological data management and analysis.

## 5.2 Bioinformatics in the 21st century

In what has been coined the 'heroic age' of complete genome sequencing, bioinformatics was hailed as the harbinger of unparalleled advances in the biomedical sciences. The human genome was expected to yield abundant drug targets, allowing 21st-century biotechnology to expand the number and kinds of drugs available; along with bioinformatics, it was predicted to bring breakthrough products in the fight against cancer, AIDS, Alzheimer's and other killer diseases. The bioinformatics revolution was foreseen as a new era of pharmaceutical research – personalised medicine – where adverse drug reactions would be eliminated, and therapeutic strategies would be tailored to individual genotypes. Bioinformatics would also provide tools to tackle the complexity of holistic research systems – and the methodology to make accurate predictions of protein structures from their sequences, and to design drugs by simulation of small molecules docking to protein architectures (Wallace, 2001). Overall, bioinformatics would dramatically improve our understanding in areas such as the regulation of gene expression, protein structure determination, comparative evolution and drug discovery (Roos, 2001).

# Box 5.1 Issues with structure

In the wake of the genome, bioinformatics was predicted to provide a range of tools, including knowledge-based methods that use information from databases to make accurate predictions of protein structures (Wallace, 2001). Despite more than 40 years of research, the problem of predicting protein conformation from the amino acid sequence remains unsolved: for some, ab initio methods generate low resolution.

It was hoped that the Structure Initiative (PSI) would lessen our reliance on prediction by providing structures or models for all completed genomes. By January 2016, the functions of more than three structures determined by PSI (1,662/4,873) remained unknown; researchers are invited to find functional sites to help characterize their structures. It's important to put the value of protein folds in perspective. Protein folds that can be decorated in different sequences to conform different functions. Knowing both the fold and the function allows us to piece together the mechanistic processes which the structure excites its function at the molecular level. Structures provide one part of a complex puzzle. Without supporting information, knowing structure alone is not enough to tell us–for a good example, see how Zareminski et al. struggled to determine the precise binding function and role of a hypothetical protein, despite having determined its molecular structure (Zareminski et al., 1998).
