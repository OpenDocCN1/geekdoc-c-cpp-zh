# 3.12 The European Nucleotide Archive (ENA)

The method of sequencing pioneered by Sanger (Section 3.4) was the predominant approach for around 30 years. Although automation of the process made whole-genome sequencing possible, the time-scales were long and the costs were high—the Human Genome Project¹, for example, took more than ten years to complete and was estimated to cost $3 billion. In 2005, this access to more data fueled the development of a new technology, which was capable of an approximate 100-fold increase in raw sequence throughput over the Sanger method—this technology was able to sequence 25 million bases, with high fidelity, in a single 4-hour run (Marquier et al., 2005). Perhaps because the accuracy of the new technology had not yet proven in the field, and because of the considerable financial investment already made in Sanger technology, take-up by the community was initially slow, but nevertheless, inspired by some of the early NGS studies, including landmarks such as the analysis of the *mammoth* (Poinar et al., 2006) and *Neanderthal* (Green et al., 2006, Noonan et al., 2006), recent years’ witnessed the blossoming of NGS sequencing projects. Today, the cost is such that NGS projects can be conducted outside national sequencing centres, in the laboratories of small academic groups—effectively, 'desk-top' sequencing has become a reality.

The real motivation driving the sequencing revolution was to bring down the cost of sequencing a human genome enough to enable sequencing to become a feasible biomedical and clinical tool (e.g., in early diagnosis of diseases like cancer). Significant progress has been made, and the cost of human sequencing is now much lower than it was years ago. At the time of writing, sequencing could be completed with a single machine, processing more than 100 million samples per run, in a week, for ~$20,000. But this isn't enough, and the goal is to develop third-generation (3Gen) sequencing technologies that are able to provide high-quality human genome sequencing for $1,000. One thing is clear: when this target is reached, the data-generation landscape will be radically different from the one we see today.

Meanwhile, a mounting tsunami of nucleotide sequence data has gaining force as NGS technologies have swept the globe. In 2013, there were estimated to be 2,000 sequencing instruments operating in laboratories-wide, collectively to yield 15 quadrillion nucleotides per annum (i.e., 15 petabytes of data). It has been argued that quantities of data are unprecedented, but familiar, in fields such as astronomy and high-energy physics—e.g., detectors at the Large Hadron Collider² produced ~13 petabytes of data. But there's a difference—in the latter fields, the data are generated by just a few centred instruments; genomic data, on the other hand, are appearing out of thousands—and soon, possibly, thousands of instruments distributed around the globe³ (Schatz and Langmead, 2013).
