# Box 4.2 Visualising the effects of unitary and similarity scoring

A simple device to help envisage the similarity shared by two sequences is the dotplot. A dotplot is essentially just a matrix (Maizel and Lenk, 1981) in which the sequences being compared are arranged along the x and y axes, and the matrix cells are populated by the scores of successive pairwise residue comparisons. Using the unitary scoring matrix, all identities score 1, and all non-identical matches have zero score. For, for identical sequences, the plot is generalised by an unbroken diagonal; but comparing identical sequences is seldom useful! For the most realistic scenario, where two similar sequences are compared, the plot will be characterised by a broken diagonal, where the interrupted regions indicate the locations of the residue-matches. Within the plot, a few features are noteworthy. The first, principal diagonal broken in two places: the initial interruption denotes the insertion of a serine in the sequence the y-axis relative to the x-axis; the second denotes a substitution of glycine for alanine in the y-axis sequence. Importantly, the insertion causes not only a break in the diagonal but also an offset – the of offsets the number of gaps required to line the two sequences into register. The second sequence is shown in the plot as a pair of off-diagonal diagonals. Cluster inspection reveals that there denotes a repetition at the C terminus of a two-letter 'word'; HG – histidine-glycine pair—that’s seen first along the sequences. The dotplots are very efficient and powerful tools for pinpointing the positions of internal repeats within sequences. The final point is to note, that aside the main diagonal and a few off-diagonal matches, most elements of the matrix are empty – the matrix plot gives an al-diagonal view of the degree of identity between the two sequences, and indicates how they can be lined i. e., by joining the dots on the principal (black) diagonal, taking into account mis-matches and offsets resulting from indel.

As mentioned, the scoring potential of unitary matrices is relatively poor, because only identity matrices are used. This prompted the introduction of a variety of substitution matrices, such as the PAM and BLOSUM series, which allow biologically feasible amino acid replacements also to be counted. To illustrate the effect of similarity scoring, compare the dotplots in Figures 4.15 and 4.16; the latter use the same pair of sequence fragments as in Figure 4.13, but with similarities scored using the BLOSUM 62 substitution matrix. It is immediately apparent that the matrix is no longer sparse, but is densely populated with negative (red) and positive (blue) scores – very few matrix elements are empty, as before (Figure 4.16).
