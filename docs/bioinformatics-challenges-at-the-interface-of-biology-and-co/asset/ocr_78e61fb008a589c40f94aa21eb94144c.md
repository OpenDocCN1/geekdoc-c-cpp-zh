Thus, in late as 12 months, the hedged claims in the original paper can be seen to have crept towards established facts, increasing the likelihood that authors citing this second generation of work will accept them as ‘canon’. This phenomenon of undue credence arising from repeated citation of previous articles that lack scientific foundations has been referred to as ‘evidence by citation’, or more, the Woozle effect’. Ideally, of course, authors should find and diligently rehearse the papers they cite, following each claim to its original source, and draw their conclusions about the strength, context and validity of particular assertions. However, for ‘hot topics’ in the life sciences, the rate at which articles are published keeping abreast of developments exceptionally challenging; cutting corners is therefore extremely tempting. Overall, then, if unpicking the tangled threads that relate scientific claims is difficult for competent humans, how much so will it be for machines? As Latour put,

... you can transform a fact into fiction or a fiction into fact just by adding or subtracting references (Latour, 1987).

Most scientific articles, historical and contemporary, are riddled with such computational hazards:i.e., sentences that are readily comprehensible to literate humans but are impenetrable to machines, because they use terms that have multiple meanings that make sense only if we understand the broader context, or they include scientific assertions that have been smothered by cautious phraseology, or that have been inappropriately amplified – Woozled – by careless citing.

# 8.6 Research objects

The problems outlined in the previous section challenge the value of the scholarly literature as a mechanism for sharing and repeating scientific endeavours, especially in the ‘digital age’. As we see, articles are riddled with uncertainty, inconsistency and ‘fluff’, attributes that render their latent nuggets of knowledge largely inaccessible to computers. If we be able to process the literature computationally, to encode the scientific data and know it in machine-readable form, radically new approaches are Required. One commentator has noted:

> I believe that the academic paper is now obsolete as the fundamental sharable description of a piece of research. In the future we will be sharing other form of scholarly artefact, which is digital and designed to rise and drop easily into the tooling of e-Research, better suited to the emerging practices of data-centric researchers. These could be called Knowledge Objects or Publication Objects or whatever: I shall refer to them as Research Objects, because they capture research (De Rolle, 2009).
