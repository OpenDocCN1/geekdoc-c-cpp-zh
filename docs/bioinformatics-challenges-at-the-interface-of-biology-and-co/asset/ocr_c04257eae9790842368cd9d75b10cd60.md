# 7.3.3 Identifying things on the Web

In the early days of the Web, there were two standard and largely distinct ways of identifying things: Uniform Resource Locators (URLs), and Uniform Resource Names (URNs). URLs were identifiers of content that carried within them to find resources that had specific locations on the Web; they could be used to locate a resource by requesting instructions from a user's computer, across the Internet, to a specific server elsewhere in the world, which could return them respectively. By contrast, the less-known URNs were identifying things, often objects in the real world (like books or poetry) that had no real 'digital location', to which we might want to refer in a machine-readable and unambiguous way (say, distinguishing a phone number from a database identifier). It became generally accepted that the difference between these concepts was merely that one was 'name' and 'address' of an object, while the other was just its 'name', and the two ideas were merged into what is now known as a Uniform Resource Identifier (URI).

A URI, a recommendation described by a set of standards by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), and endorsed by the WWW Consortium (W3C), is the accepted standard for identifying 'on the Web'. URIs unify different aspects of identifiers, allowing the encapsulation of namespaces, uniqueness and even resolution mechanisms in one standard form. A URI is really just a string of ASCII characters, fixed with a colon-defined scheme. In other words, at the most abstract level, a URI is nothing more than:

```
scheme:somecharacters
```

This convention means that a computer can inspect the characters up to the colon, determine what scheme the URI is using, and, based on that, decide how to interpret the rest of the characters in the URI. The most common form of URI is undoubtedly the 'Web address' (which, in the early days of the Web, we'd have called a URL, but now has the more lumen name of 'URI' that uses the http: scheme). This form of URI is so common that we have become attuned to paring in the form of 'http://blah.com/thing' as being likely to identify a Web page about 'thing' hosted by the Blah Corporation (so so much that we often drop the 'http' prefix in the knowledge that people will take that as read). There are many other resources, such as fax numbers, geographical locations, email addresses, items, world coordinates, and so on (see Box 7.1).

Following the colon after the scheme comes the rest of the URI, the exact structure of which depends on the scheme in question: for the case of a fax number, for example, it is the international dialing number for fax machine (e.g., fax:+44145736502); for email, the other form, is mailto:a.person@example.com. The specification of this structure is found through the Internet Assigned Authority (IANA) registry, usually deployed by some standards body like the IETF. ```
http://example.com/protein/gpr/rhodopsin
