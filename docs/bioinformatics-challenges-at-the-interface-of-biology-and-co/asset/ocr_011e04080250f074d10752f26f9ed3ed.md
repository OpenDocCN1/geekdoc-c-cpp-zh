Such comprehensions pose problems for text-mining algorithms to those mentioned above for Mc4, with its multiple synonyms. For example, suppose we want to find all instances of olive rhodopsin in the literature, and use the current UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot identifier, OPSD$SHEEP. Unfortunately, the wouldn't be comprehensive because it would miss all examples of OPSD$SHEEP and OOSH. Alternatively, we could use the current UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot accession number, P02700, but again, a search would miss any of the other possible numbers listed in Table 5.2. As before, to be confident of achieving comprehensive results, we'd need to use all of the identifiers or all of the accession numbers known to us. Fortunately, UniProt provides an archive of protein sequence histories, including changes to their identifiers and accession numbers. Nevertheless, reconstructing those histories accurately requires significant human effort.

# 5.5 Problems with sequences

Related to the nomenclature issues mentioned above are more subtle problems. For example, consider this: when we refer to the sequence of olive rhodopsin, which sequence do we really mean? This might sound like a pedantic question. After all, we probably just the current, most up-to-date version; but the *current version* of a sequence is a historical artefact – the current version of olive rhodopsin in 1986 was different from the *current version* in 1991. In the mid 1980s, the former is likely to have been a version of the sequence based on the 1986 PIR entry (AC# A03155, OOSH) – see Figure 5.2; during the 1990s and 00s, however, the latter is likely to have been a version based on the 1991 PIR entry (AC# A30407, ID OOSH) – see Figure 5.3.
