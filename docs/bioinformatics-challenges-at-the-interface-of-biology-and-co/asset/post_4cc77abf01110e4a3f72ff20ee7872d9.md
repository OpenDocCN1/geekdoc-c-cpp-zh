As an aside, it's fascinating (and perhaps humbling) to note the similarities between these biological interactions and the internal workings of modern computers: the processes involved in creating proteins from DNA templates bears more than a passing resemblance to the execution of code on a silicon chip. As much as we may recognise the genius of the humans of silicon age for discovering how to store information in digital form that it can be copied without error, and how to represent instructions such that they can be interpreted reliably and repeatedly by machines, it is clear analogous mechanisms evolved millions of years ago.

The biological processes of transcription and translation are extremely complex, involving the interactions of a multitude of protein and nucleic acid molecules. We will not describe these processes in great detail here, as they are dealt with in many biology textbooks – some information is, however, given in Section 2.2.5 and Boxes 2.4–7. For now, let's explore the building-blocks and alphabets of the key molecular players in the central dogma.

## 2.2.3 Fundamental building-blocks and alphabets

We start by looking at DNA – deoxyribonucleic acid. We can think of DNA as having a four-letter alphabet, each letter denoting one its constituent nucleotide bases: two are known as purines (adenine (A) and guanine (G)), and DNA is usually found as a double-stranded molecule – the famous double helix – its two strands wind around each other and held together by chemical bonds opposing A and T, and C and G bases (for the structures of these molecules, please refer to Box 2.1). The comment strands run in opposite directions and have complementary sequences, as that, where one has G, the other must have C, and where one has A, the other must have T:

```
5'-T-G-A-C-C-G-A-T-3'
3'-A-C-T-G-G-C-T-A-5'
```

In RNA, Uracil (U) replaces thymine: the structures of uracil and thymine are similar, but U lacks the methyl (CH3) group linked to the ring of the T molecule. A and G, which chemical structures contain a double ring, are purines; C and U, whose chemical structures contain only a single ring, are pyrimidines.

The basic-blocks of nucleic acid chains (the units in which their lengths are measured) are nucleotides: these comprise one base, one sugar molecule, and one phosphate moiety. In DNA, the deoxyribose sugars are linked together via phosphate moieties attached to carbon atoms 3 and 5 of their five-membered carbon rings. This arrangement is shown in Figure 2.3. The final carbons at the end of each DNA strand are termed 5’ and 3’, and denote the direction in which genetic information is.

DNA is famous for its double-stranded, helical structure (which forms the basis for numerous logos and is an essential eye-component of science articles and fiction programmes!). Although its physical structure plays a vital role in the processes that lead to the creation of proteins, its intertwined strands are a relatively mundane construction: its sugar and phosphate groups essentially form a scaffold on which its bases are anchored in sequence and held in place by chemical bonds between adjacent A and T, C and G bases on opposing strands – this is sometimes referred to as Watson-Crick base-pairing, as shown in Figure 2.4.

For real DNA sequences, with many thousands, or hundreds of bases, this saving is immensely valuable. As mentioned earlier, much of the sequence data stored in databases comes from sequencing DNA (most from genome-sequencing projects). The process of sequencing DNA is error-prone, and ambiguities arise. It's not in the scope of this book to discuss how DNA is sequenced, but it is worth mentioning one of the sources of error. During the process of sequencing, the concentrations of individual bases are read out in the form of chromatogram traces where each base is represented by a particular fluorescent trace, as shown in Figure 2.5. Computers attempt to interpret or 'call' the bases from these traces, a step that involves digital processing and decoding using appropriate tools (e.g., Phred base-calling). Base-calling is not a perfect process. When no single base (represented by a single colour dye) can be called with confidence, it is useful to be able to indicate this in some kind of simple, but meaningful way.
