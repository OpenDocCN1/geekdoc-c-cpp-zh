## 8.7 Data publishing

Historically, scientists have tended to keep their research data secure under wraps until the time they can publish their results in a scholarly journal, cocooned in a suitable erudite narrative, so they can receive appropriate approbation from the community. The fear of being 'scooped', of seeing others making insightful leaps to exciting discoveries from one's own hard-won experimental results – and getting not completely unheard. We only have to remember Watson and Crick's controversial exploitation of Rosalind Franklin's X-ray data to determine the structure of DNA (made possible without her, Wilkins gave Crick a copy of her MRC research report), and recall that Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize in 1962 for their work on nucleic acids (after Franklin's), to understand that scooping can have significant ramifications.

Such fears, in the past, been reinforced by several other factors: the absence of any academic incentive to publish intermediate data, the focus on 'the paper' as the only object of record that attracts kudos, and the lack of technology for publishing and forming attractive data. However, scientists' tendency to withhold so important results until they've been able to place an official flag on the scientific landscape by publishing their findings in a 'reputable journal' is increasingly recognised as a series of progress. Recent studies (e.g., Piowar and Vision, 2014) have shown that 'papers' with publicly available datasets receive a higher number of citations than similar studies without available data. And if – alongside the moral argument that most research is conducted with money from the public – such incentives aren't enough to convince scientists to part with their hard-won data, funding bodies (including the NIH, RCUK and European Commission) have begun to enforce rules governing the timeline and openness of data generated by projects requiring their funding.

More recently, publishers like PLoS have put in place similar mandates that require, for any paper to be considered for publication, their referent data to be made openly and publicly available. Multimodal repositories for domain-specific data-sets, such as Dryad (Vision, 2010), PANGEA (Grobe et al., 2010), DataOne (Michener), and general repositories like figshare, and institution-specific repositories, are being created to meet this growing need for publishing alongside narrative; although the assortment of carrots and sticks is growing, there remain complex political and technological challenges that will need to be addressed before it becomes commonplace to publish raw data alongside articles. While it may be easy to deposit a sequence alignment, the newly determined 3D structure of a biological chemical entity in a suitable database, it's considerably less clear how, or by what mechanism one could make terabytes of video or other large bespoke data sets available. Having recognised the costs of not doing it, the momentum behind data publishing is growing, and it seems inevitable that articles of the future will be commentaries that illuminate full data-sets, rather than mere edited highlights, as is the practice today.

## 8.8 Separating scientific wheat from chaff – towards semantic searches

By its nature, the scholarly record grows with time. It may be that old or 'ancient' articles are less relevant today; nevertheless, persist as part of the scientific record. If there was ever a time when scientists could read and comprehend everything published in their field, this is neither true nor possible today. If you need evidence of the truth of this, a biological term, and search PubMed for articles that mention it: e.g., in December 2015, 'cancer' returned >3.2 million articles; 'HIV' >294,000; 'rhodopsin', >10,000; and even 'Forceptab', a branded drug that never even made it to market, still weighs with >300 articles.

As scientists, we want to know the latest facts that may influence our research; and, in a world where the rate of publication is out-pacing our ability to find and read them, we want to know, 'which of the staggering number of articles we should read?' It turns out that answering this is hard.
