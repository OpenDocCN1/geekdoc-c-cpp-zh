The problem we're seeing is caused by the fact that the identifier (filename) encodes some information about the object it identifies (the location of the illustration in the report); when the nature of the object changes, that relationship is broken. In this scenario, the meaning is being interpreted by a human, and the job of uniting the names and locations is a manual one, which, though tedious, is manageable. But the issues become pernicious when computers start interpreting the identifiers, and on such a large scale.

To avoid this problem, we have come to terms with something that, on first encounter, seems counter-intuitive: the best identifiers are generally those that are meaningless. Or, put another way, the only thing an identifier should do is to identify a thing. In computer-science terminology, identifiers that are created (or, at least, intended to be treated) are described as being opaque identifiers: we can't see through them to gain any meaning or properties of the objects they identify. The problem with opaque identifiers, of course, is exactly that on their own, they don't mean anything; therefore, to use them, some additional mechanism is required that will get us to the things to which they refer. The process of following an identifier to find the thing to which it refers is called dereferencing or resolution, an act that makes sense within the context of a namespace: the number 356024, dereferenced via PMC, give access to a paper by Coussens et al. (2004); dereferenced via the local telephone directory, it would put us in touch, with a good Chinese Takeaway in Glossop. The thing important to remember is that dereferencing an identifier within a particular namespace may require using different mechanisms: fetching a PubMed article may require a different mechanism than fetching a molecular structure from the PDB, or from looking up a phone number in a local directory.

## 7.3 Identifying things on the Web

In the early days of the Web, there were two standard ways of identifying things: Uniform Resource Locators (URLs), and Uniform Resource Names (URNs). It became generally accepted that the difference between these concepts was merely that one was 'name' and the other 'address', while the two ideas were merged into what is now known as

A URI, a recommendation by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), and endorsed by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), is the accepted standard for 'on the Web'. URIs unify different aspects of identifiers, allowing the encapsulation of namespaces, uniqueness and even resolution mechanisms in one standard. A URI is really just a string of ASCII characters, fixed with a colon-defined scheme.

```
scheme:somecharacters
```

This convention means that a computer can inspect the characters up to the colon, determine what scheme the URI is using, and, based on that, decide how to interpret the rest of. The most common form of URI is undoubtedly the 'Web address' (which, in the early days of the Web, we'd have called a URL, but now has the lumen name of 'URI' that uses the http: scheme). This form of URI is so common that we have become attuned to paring in the form of 'http://blah.com/thing' as being likely to identify a Web page about 'thing' hosted by the Blah Corporation (so so much that we often drop the 'http' prefix in the knowledge that people will take that as read). Following the colon after the scheme comes the rest of the URI, the exact structure of which depends on the scheme in question: for the case of a fax number, for example, it is the international dialing number for fax machine (e.g., fax:+44145736502); for email, the other form, is mailto:a.person@example.com. There are many other resources (see Box 7.1).

```
http://example.com/protein/gpr/rhodopsin
```
