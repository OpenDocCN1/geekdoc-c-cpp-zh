To avoid this problem, we have come to terms with something that, on first encounter, seems counter-intuitive: the best identifiers are generally those that are meaningless. Or, put another way, the only thing an identifier should do is to identify a thing. In computer-science terminology, identifiers that are created (or, at least, intended to be treated) are described as being opaque identifiers: we can't see through them to gain any meaning or properties of the objects they identify. The problem with opaque identifiers, of course, is exactly that on their own, they don't mean anything; therefore, to use them, some additional mechanism is required that will get us to the things to which they refer. The process of following an identifier to find the thing to which it refers is called dereferencing or resolution, an act that makes sense within the context of a namespace: the number 356024, dereferenced via PMC, give access to a paper by Coussens et al. (2004); dereferenced via the local telephone directory, it would put us in touch, with a good Chinese Takeaway in Glossop. The thing important to remember is that dereferencing an identifier within a particular namespace may require using different mechanisms: fetching a PubMed article may require a different mechanism than fetching a molecular structure from the PDB, or from looking up a phone number in a local directory.

Conceptually, to use opaque identifiers, we use the system presented in Figure 7.2. Given a qualified identifier (namespace + name), we can use our knowledge of the particular namespace's mechanism to dereference it to a location. It's then a matter of fetching from that location the entity in question.

Notice that at any point, the resolution method could return a different location, because the structure of the PMC website changed. Equally, the resolution method itself could change over time, perhaps owing to technical changes to the underlying data, but the identifier remains the same: only the location of the resource/or resolution mechanism by which we find the resource changes – the resource, which we eventually get to it, should remain the same. This system allows us to decouple identification and access, making our databases, and other systems, more flexible and robust to future changes. However, it does require us to carry out this dereferencing every time it's needed, and inevitably involves some cost in time, performance and effort, which should be balanced against the resilience that we use opaque identifiers. In our simple example, we could decide on a naming scheme where each image is named by a monotonically increasing number (1.png, 2.png, etc.), and maintain a separate 'lookup file' that maps from the identifier to the latest image number; that would probably be a sledgehammer to crack a nut. In almost all real-world bioinformatics applications, however, the benefits of using opaque identifiers from the outset (and providing an efficient dereferencing mechanism), almost certainly outweigh the pain of any alternative approach.
