## 2.2.4 The protein structure

It is important to understand the properties of amino acids, and their overlaps, because, as mentioned earlier, they ultimately determine the functions and characteristics of their proteins. Traditionally, we consider protein structures to be built in a hierarchical way. The **primary structure**, is the simplest level, as is the linear sequence of amino acids in protein:

`MELLPTAVESQAQIIGRPPEWIWLLGALMTYFTVKGMGVSDPDAKKFYTLVIA...` (Wait, looking at the OCR: "MELLPTAVESQAQIIGRPPEWIWLLGALMTYFTVKGMGVSDPDAKKFYTLVIA...")

As a chain of amino acids is synthesised, the amino acids begin a complex dance, as their mutual repulsive and attractive interactions play out the folding pathway, maximising and minimising the hydrophilic and hydrophobic interplay with surrounding molecules. During this process, regions of local regularity are formed in the sequence, and these are referred as secondary structures. There are several types of secondary structure, the most common of which are so called $\alpha$-helix, $\beta$-strand, and $\beta$-turn – these are illustrated in Figure 2.11.

Sometimes, elements of secondary structure fold into higher-level stable units, which have been given an assortment of descriptive names such as $\beta$-barrel, $\beta$-propeller, $\alpha$-solenoid, Jelly roll, and so on. These are often referred as super-secondary structures or super-folds.

At the next level of the hierarchy is the tertiary structure, which defines the overall chain fold that results from packing of secondary (and super-secondary) structural elements. Often, proteins contain more than one. When proteins have several subunits (like hemoglobin, for example), the relationship between them is described by their quaternary structure. Examples of these higher-order structures are illustrated in Figure 2.12.

## 2.2.5 RNA processing in prokaryotes and eukaryotes

The two types of organism that we consider are termed prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Prokaryotes are single-celled organisms that lack a nucleus. Evolutionary studies have light on two main types of prokaryotic organism: the archaea and bacteria (archaeal cells can usually be distinguished from bacteria based on their gene sequence and gene content, many being specialised to conditions of extreme heat or salinity). In contrast, eukaryotes have nucleus; they also usually possess a cytoskeleton and contain many highly specialised organelles (mitochondria, Golgi apparatus, endoplasmic reticulum so on). Eukaryotes are more complex than prokaryotes, and their genomes are much larger.

One of aspect increased complexity is seen in the way that RNA is processed within these different organisms. As we see, RNA transcribed from a protein-coding region of DNA is termed messenger RNA, or mRNA (an overview of the molecular events, that first transcription is seen in Box 2,4, Figure 2.13). In prokaryotes, mRNA consists of a single sequence that encodes the protein product, flanked by untranslated regions (UTRs) – so called they are not destined for translation – at 5’ and 3’ ends. This simple structure is shown schematically in Figure 2.14 (a). Eukaryotic RNA transcription has a more complicated structure, as shown in Figure 2.14 (b). Here, RNA is first synthes in a precursor form (pre-mRNA), which must be processed before becoming a functional mRNA.
