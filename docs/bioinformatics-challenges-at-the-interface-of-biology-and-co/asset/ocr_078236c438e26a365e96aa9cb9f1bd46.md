One approach to capture this kind of information more explicitly is to list the types of residues that occur at each alignment position, using a kind of shorthand notation. **Consensus expressions** are often referred to as patterns, which nearly summarize patterns of residue conservation within alignments. Being simple to create, patterns like these were used to encapsulate conserved residues of protein active sites, binding sites, etc., and collection provided the foundations of the first protein family database, PROSITE. This was an important development because for the first time, consensus expression was available to the community to search with. Each pattern also allows us to characterise newly detected sequences using tools like this has been one of the major goals of bioinformatics since the first sequence databases were created. The appeal of this particular method was, and still is, its simplicity is its drawback and to the development of other, more sophisticated, approaches. These issues are explored in Chapter 4.

Diagnostically, sequence patterns suffer certain limitations. Specifically, matching a pattern is a binary 'match/no-match' event: hence, even the most trivial difference (a single amino acid) is a mis-match. As Swiss-Prot grew and accommodated more and more divergent sequences, the more evident and problematic this weakness became. Indeed, it generated a kind of circularity in which the more particular PROSITE patterns were to fail to diagnose or characterise them correctly, and the more work curators had to do update or revise the sequence patterns in order to better the changing contents of Swiss-Prot. This immense dependence on both the size and composition of Swiss-Prot to become one of the toughest challenges for PROSITE curators (and indeed for the curators of all protein family-based databases in future).
Many researchers were aware of the limitations of sequence patterns, and worked to develop more powerful 'descriptors' of protein families and functional units. Profiles built from comprehensive sequence alignments, and are much more tolerant of amino acid changes or substitutions than patterns; they’re much more flexible in how they handle the differences in the lengths of sequences that arise from insertions or deletions.
