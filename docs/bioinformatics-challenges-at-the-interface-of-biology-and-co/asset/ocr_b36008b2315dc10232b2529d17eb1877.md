The PRALINE colour scale, however, runs in the opposite direction: i.e., the mutational hotspots (the most changeable positions) are blue, while the mutational cold (least changeable positions) are red. The issue here isn't that one scheme is inherently right and another wrong; rather, it is about the mental effort required to assimilate the learning metaphor— some metaphors are more intuitive than others (because we learned when we were younger, because we use them in everyday life, or because our visual perception is wired this way); they can therefore make our lives much easier.

There is, however, a more subtle point here—it's tied up, with the colours perhaps we use as short-cuts for concepts like 'hot', but with the semantics we bring to them. For example, what does 'hot' mean to you? A standard definition will speak of degrees of heat or high temperature, however, or perhaps, the degree of spinach in a cup. A dictionary of language, however, will have a much wider semantic intent, capturing meanings such as 'angry', 'stolen', 'attractive', 'talented', 'well-known/popular' and, ironically, 'cool'! In the context of biological sequence and structure analysis, it could be argued that stages of conservation are the 'hot' things to look rather than mutations, because they highlight structural and functional features, and hence they should be red; but it could be argued that mutations are the 'hot' things to look for, given their potential to understand functional and genetic diseases like cancer³⁵. Each of us carries different semantic baggage, and hence what we consider 'hot' is subjective and task dependent. Different colour schemes may then appear to be more or less intuitive, however, will be easier or more difficult to use, depending on the interplay between our personal phenomenal and mental models.
