# Bioinformatics challenges at the interface of biology and computer science

The daunting march of just described came at a cost, challenging current IT infrastructures to the limit. Some of the oldest data in ENA date back to the 1980s, when the EMBL data library was first created. As an aside, it is ironic, that in those days, there were distribution headaches. Bairoch, for example, relates how difficult it was to transfer version 2 of the EMBL data library from computer tape to a mainframe computer and thence to his microcomputer, because the had no communication protocol to talk to a microcomputer—he therefore had to spend the night transferring the data, screen by screen, using a 300 baud acoustic modem (Bairoch, 2000). To put this in perspective, this version of the database 811 nucleotide sequences (with more than 1 billion bps)—is about the same amount of data that currently enters ENA every 1 or 2 seconds.

If ENA refused >20 terabases of raw NGS reads³⁵ in 2010, and, combined with 230 terabytes of disk, the space required to accommodate its >1,000 terabytes of nucleotides would be virtually unimaginable. The infrastructure required to store, maintain and such archive, and the cost of doing so, is beyond anything the originals of the first databases, or the developers of the new sequencing technologies could have conceived (as an aside, with a global estimate of $105 billion in 2012, rising to $120 billion in 2013 and consumption <40 GW (Venkatamani, 2012), a staggering figure—aligned to be equivalent to the output of ~30 nuclear power plants (Glanz, 2012). The strain on genotyping agencies has already become apparent, and how the community would react to predict the first onslaught on 3GGen technologies is unclear. In a hint of what might lie ahead, in February 2011, owing to budget constraints, the NCBI announced that it would be deconstructing its Sequence Read and Trace Archives for high-throughput sequence data in a phased closure over 12 months. Shortly afterwards, negotiations with the NIH yielded their interim funding for the archives until October 2011 (the resource was still accessible in February 2015, but the last software update was in 2013—an upgrade to the S BLAST service to make searching its 700 trillion bases more efficient). What is clear from this incredible trajectory that new funding and service strategies will be necessary to maintain these resources and provide continued access to the existing archives, now and in the future. The European ELIXIR framework for a bioinformatics infrastructure for the life sciences and the trans-US NIH initiative—Big Data to Knowledge (BD2K)—are likely to play key roles as data continues to unfold.
