# Afterword

While the desirability of the items on Hutchins' wish-list could easily be dismissed as 'obvious', the list nevertheless provides a useful vehicle for exploring the obstacles that lie ahead on the next stage of the bioinformatics journey, for understanding why we are where we are, and why many things are still not solved.

Taking these one by one, from a technical point of view, there are interesting challenges associated with each of the five properties: with today's programming languages and support frameworks, creating cross-platform software is much harder than building something that works for one operating system; being able to access a 'comprehensive' array of third-party databases dealing with multiple databases and schemes; benefiting from database 'updates' necessarily involves evolution of the custom tool, either with the growing scale of data, or to adapt to new features; 'smart' behaviour likely implies the use of complex semantic technologies; 'flexibility' requires rigorous adherence to software-engineering principles, in order to create tools that are extensible and re-factoring to accommodate new requirements. All of these are technically harder than achieving a but are necessary to create tools that can be shared, and that have any possibility of living beyond the lifetime of a given project. The interesting point here is that these are, from a technical perspective, just hard to achieve, but we have seen through this, not impossible,

The reason we are not awash with tools and systems that embody Hutchins' utopian wish-list is much more complex and subtle, and we, suggest, to do with money, politics and ultimately, human nature with any specific technical hurdle. Hurdling each of the five points is the implication that tools have to be maintained and updated in order to see whether, to keep them in sync with other tools, or to prevent them from rotting, as underlying operating systems evolve, and programming languages and frameworks rise and fall in popularity. Achieving this requires not just initial effort to use the best software-engineering paradigms and the latest interoperability standards, but sustained, long-term effort – that requires sustained, long-term funding.

The paradox is perhaps met revealed in Hutchins' first point: fundingbodies and users want software and data to be 'free' – i.e., free in the sense of not having to pay for them, however, software and data need to be maintained and supported indefinitely, so that they remain reliable resources on which to base current and future research. In 2005, Scott McNealy,then-CEO of Microsystems, quipped 'Open Source is free like a puppy is free' – in other words, while you may be able to acquire your little canine component without paying with any money, or if you don't it and pay the vet's bills, you can't expect it to live long. And, just like puppies, figuring out how to make software and databases free and sustainable presents almost insurmountable challenges, resulting in many of them being abandoned by the wayside.

To give some idea, in 2004, Bairoch and his colleagues reflected on how tough it really is to develop and maintain high-quality databases for the life sciences (Bairoch et al., 2004). They enumerated six observations that they deemed essential for well-behaved developers to:

+   * Your task will be much more complex and bigger than you ever thought it could be.
* If your database is successful and useful to the user community, then you will have to dedicate all your efforts to develop it for a much longer period of time than you would thought possible.
