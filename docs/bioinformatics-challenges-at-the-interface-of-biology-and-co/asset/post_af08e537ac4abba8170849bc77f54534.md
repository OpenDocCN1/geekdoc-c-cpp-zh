The ways in which data are reported are constantly changing. In scientific articles, data were included in the main body of the article itself. However, when data-sets grew past the point where they could reasonably be included in the main narrative without disrupting its flow, journals began publishing ‘auxiliary materials’. Now, in the era of ‘big data’ (usually, far bigger than can be printed even on ‘electronic paper’), this approach is inadequate; therefore, scholarly articles make reference to supplementary online resources instead, and many nuggets of scientific insight are at risk of being swamped and overlooked in the process. ‘big data’ has arrived, but to quote commentator, ‘big insights have now’ (Harford, 2014).

Of course, scholarly communication, in one form or other, pre-dates computers and databases by many centuries: from ‘letters’ to learned societies distributed to the community on paper, to today’s online journals, the act of writing and publishing ideas and discoveries for scrutiny by the scientific community has always been a fundamental part of the scientific method. In many ways, the process of submitting an article to a scholarly journal, of having it reviewed by peers, and eventually – if deemed worthy – published, has changed little over the years. Perhaps a 2012 ‘white paper’ (the Force 11 Manifesto) opens with the following comment:

A dispassate observer, visiting from another planet, would surely be found that, in an era of multimedia, 3D television and 24/7 social connectivity, scholars and researchers continue to communicate their thoughts and research results primarily by means of the selective distribution of ink on paper, or at best via electronic facsimiles of the same (Allen et al., 2012).

The increased reliance on publishing to validate scientific claims has meant that articles and data-sets are becoming more closely intertwined (Bourne, 2005). The modern scientific article has been described as a ‘story with data’ (de Waard, 2014), a phrase that nicely brings the two essential components of modern scientific writing: without the story, data are just a collection of facts with no interpretation or real meaning; without data, a story is in danger of being little more than an opinion – perhaps a fairytale?

## 8.5 The gulf between human and machine comprehension

As we see – indeed, it’s the underlying theme of the whole book – creating meaningful data on a large scale is not trivial, but requires an understanding of the needs of humans and machines. The problem is, humans and machines speak very different languages: the challenge lies finding ways to express concepts in ways that are meaningful to both. At one extreme are the subtleties and nuances of natural language, flavoured by the conventions and terminologies of specialist scientific disciplines; at the other, lie the brutal, pedantic capabilities of computers.

## Chapter 8 Linking data and scientific literature

To illustrate how far apart these things are, let's consider an example that sits somewhere near the midpoint between natural language and machine code. Consider the following statement, written in a 'logic notation':

```
$$i, j \in dom(s); i \leq j \Rightarrow s[i] \leq s[j]$$
```

To make sense of this, a human reader first has to decode the various symbols. In this toy example, this is fairly straightforward: $\forall$ means 'for all'; $dom$ refers to an abstract 'domain'; and $\Rightarrow$ means 'implies that'. The variables and 'less than equal to' symbols carry their familiar meanings. From left to right, the statement can be read 'literally':

For all pairs of things (which we will call $i$ and $j$) in a domain we'll call $s$, the value of $i$ being less than equal to the value of $j$ implies that the index of $i$ in the domain is less or equal to the index of $j$ in the domain.

The statement, therefore, asserts a relationship between values, and the ordering of those values in a particular domain; but what kind of relationship? Thinking of these as being integers perhaps makes the real meaning more clear: if a number is smaller or equal to another, then its ordering is earlier or equal to that of the other. Or, put more simply, 'the numbers are sorted in ascending order, allowing for duplicates'. Although it's fairly easy to learn the 'vocabulary' and 'grammar' of logic symbols, and to literally translate such statements, understanding the real meaning of logic notation—even for examples like this one—takes considerable practice in a particular way that is alien to most scientists.

From a machine's point of view, a comparable amount of work also needs to be done to make any real use of the statement. A computer can't execute such notation without support from automated-reasoning tools or sophisticated compilers to translate the logic into code that can be executed on a CPU—see Box 8.2. Even then, the notation says nothing about the details of the processing of sorting, the time constraints, and so on—that the result is a sorted list of things.

This example illustrates the enormous gap that exists between language that is comprehensible to humans and the kind of code that is executable by machines. Clearly, getting scientists to write academic articles that are comprehensible to machines is impractical. So what about the alternative: getting machines to 'understand' natural language? Perhaps not surprisingly, this is an enormously tall order.

Let's consider an utterly trivial piece of natural language: 'the cat sat on the mat'. A machine 'reading' would first have to identify the objects in the sentence and, with a suitable electronic dictionary, would then have to determine the relationship between them: in this case, 'sat' (again, using a dictionary), could perhaps determine that 'sat' is the past tense of the verb 'to be'. The preposition 'on' makes an association between the act of sitting, the cat and the mat; and the other words are articles, telling that the sentence refers to a particular cat and a particular mat. ### Box 8.2 Bubble sort in C

Written in C, code for bubble sort (here, operating on a small list of arbitrary numbers) that would satisfy the criterion of generating numbers 'sorted in ascending order, allowing for duplicates' looks like this:

```c
#include <stdio.h>
int main() {
    int array[5] = {8, 5, 1, 7, 3}, n, c, d, swap;
    n = 5;
    for (c = 0; c < (n - 1); c++) {
        for (d = 0; d < n - c - 1; d++) {
            if (array[d] > array[d+1]) /* For decreasing order use < */
                swap = array[d];
                array[d] = array[d+1];
                array[d+1] = swap;
            }
        }
    }
    printf("Sorted list in ascending order:\n");
    for (c = 0; c < n; c++) {
        printf("%d\n", array[c]);
    }
    return 0;
}
```
