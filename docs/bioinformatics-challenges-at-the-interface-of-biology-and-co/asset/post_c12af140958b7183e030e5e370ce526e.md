## Box 7.1 Scheme names

There are more than 150 'official' scheme names registered with the Internet Assigned Names Authority (IANA), and a small handful of unofficial but commonly used names. In bioinformatics, we frequently encounter:

- http, the HyperText Transport Protocol, and its Secure equivalent, used for interactions between Web servers and clients;
- ftp, ftps: the File Transfer Protocol, and its Secure equivalent, still quite often for moving large files around the Internet.
- ssh: Secure Shell, a protocol for allowing encrypted remote login between machines;
- git: a distributed version control system designed by Linus Torvalds, original creator of the Linux operating system;
- imap: Internet Message Access Protocol, a protocol for email retrieval and storage. Largely used POP (the Post Office Protocol), although the scheme remains unchanged;
- smtp: the Simple Mail Transfer Protocol, used for sending email (as opposed to IMAP, which is used for retrieving it);
- doi: the Digital Object Identifier (see Section 7.3), widely used in scholarly publishing, but not an IANA scheme at the time of writing.

### 7.3.4 Cool URIs don't change

In a 'style note' from the W3C, Tim Berners-Lee describes a 'cool URI' as being 'one which does not change', pointing out that there's no technical reason (because the insolvency of a domain name's) for a URI to become invalid or forfeit the wrong thing. The note provides a list of things not to include in a URI, such as a document's authors, subject, status (draft, final, etc.), filename extension, and so on, all of which are likely to change over time. Noting 'that the change detected, putting any identifier in name is asking for the object one or another', he implies that 'cool URIs' are essentially opaque. As tempting as it might be to create a few characters that humans can remember, it's best to accept that nothing's more than a few of characters matters and machines are happy with a happy resource 101127123875t7nfil they are with /protein/gcr/rhodopsin.

### 7.3.5 Versioning and provenance

Almost inevitably, an entity for which an identifier will get modified in some way, given enough time: it may be updated, or deleted; it may be superseded by a newer entity, or related to some other entities. All these things are possible; each has occurred times throughout the history of every major bioinformatics database, and there's nothing special about this moment in time that prevents it from happening again; but what does this mean for our identifier?

The simplest thing that can be said is that our new-modified identifier longer points at what we used to point at; depending on the nature of the change, this may invalidate the identifier. In this case, we have a choice: discard it, with any information we have stored about it, or continue to use it to represent our (now out-of-date) entity? The answer depends on the nature of the change and the data we're identifying; either way, we run the risk of losing some crucial information. The difficulty here is the lack of context about what the change actually means. A database record may be deleted because a new version has been created where that replaces it, or it may have simply retired. In the framework, how are we to know which new version resides? Even if we still have access to the updated version, why was it not have to correct spelling mistakes, to add literature citations, to fix a sequence error, or to completely reclassify a protein; somewhere on that spectrum, we could argue that the new version no longer represents the 'same thing' as the old; but this distinction may not be obvious.

For these reasons, reasoning and provenance are important, and should be integrated into databases from their first record. The following suggestions encapsulate what's need and why:

- If an entity is deleted, its identifier should remain valid, and at the very least, should resolve to something that explains why it was deleted, and by whom. Ideally, the old record should remain available, but if it simply vanishes, we're left clueless as to the reason.
- In an ideal system, every single revision of an entity be separately identifiable, with the provenance that explains the history in machine-readable format. This would make it possible to traverse that history, allowing us to build up a full picture of its evolution, and allowing a computer to choose the most appropriate revision for its needs.

With this, we could do some clever things. We could automatically update any data we've stored on our machines, if the changes are deemed sufficiently minimal. If the changes are more substantial, we can allow users to choose, in an informed way, which records to update, and which to potentially discard. We recommend new literature for records whose citations have changed. We can evaluate the historical path of our knowledge to gain useful insights, or to point to areas of our knowledge that need updating.

### 7.3.6 Case studies

Let's now explore identifiers that appear in the field of bioinformatics.

#### LSIDs

Life Science Identifiers (LSIDs) are URIs that use the urn: scheme, with a namespace identifier of LSID. With a complementary resolution protocol, they were invented as a way of unifying all the identifiers used by biological databases around the globe, in an attempt to tackle some of the issues we've been discussing in this chapter. The general format of an LSID, with an example from the International Plant Names Index (IPNI):

```
urn:lsid:<AU> <NS>: <LSID>: <VE>
urn:lsid:ipni.names:1234-1
```

As we can see, LSIDs include an authority (AU), a namespace (NS) within that namespace, and an optional version code (VE). They provide a single identifier format that could take over the job of all existing identifiers and a single point of resolution for them all. Unfortunately haven't been as successful as we might hope, because lately uptake has been slow, partly through simple inertia.
