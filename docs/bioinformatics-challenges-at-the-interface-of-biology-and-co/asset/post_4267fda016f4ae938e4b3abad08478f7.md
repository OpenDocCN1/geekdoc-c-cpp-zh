
# Bioinformatics challenges at the interface of biology and computer science

At this time, Swiss-Prot had the highest standard of annotation of any publicly available protein sequence database: from the outset, one of its leading goals had to provide critical analysis and annotation for all of its constituents. To this end, each entry was accompanied by a significant amount of manually gleaned information: this was derived primarily from original publications and review articles by an expanding group of curators, with input from an international panel of experts. This high degree of meticulous manual annotation was becoming a bigger and bottleneck, inhibiting the release rate of the resource. Eventually, faced with the relentless data flow from growing number of genome projects, this labour-intensive process became untenable.

To keep pace with the flood of data, a new approach was needed. The imperative was to release the products of genomic sequences into the public domain as swiftly as possible; the problem was how this could be done without compromising the high quality of the Swiss-Prot data, or eroding the editorial standards of the database. The solution was to create a computer-generated sequence, with a Swiss-Prot format, in which entries were derived by translation of coding sequences in the EMBL database library—this TREMBL, first released in October 1996 (Bairoch and Apweiler, 1996). TREMBL contained almost 105,000 entries, not far twice the size of Swiss-Prot 34.0, with which it was released in parallel.

Initially, TREMBL contained no annotations – it was essentially the result of an automatic pipeline that extracted coding sequences from the EMBL database and reformatted them. The idea behind its creation was twofold. In addition giving Swiss-Prot users access to the products of genomic data more quickly, the resource was intended to act as a coral in which to hold ‘raw’ sequences prior to their annotation by curators, in readiness for accession to Swiss-Prot. As such, it had two main sections: SP-TREMb, containing entries that would eventually go into Swiss-Prot, once annotated; and REM-TREMb, containing sequences that would never be used in Swiss-Prot (fragments of less than enough, synthetic sequences, etc.).

The raw state of TREMBL entries meant that most of work was necessary to upgrade them to the necessary Swiss-Prot standard. To truly accelerate this process, various tools and protocols were developed over the years to provide some level of annotation automatically: e.g., including information about the potential functions of proteins, their subcellular locations, the metabolic pathways in which they’re involved, active sites they may contain, factors, binding sites, domains they may possess, and so on. Initially, binding-site, domain and family-based information was derived from searches of databases like PROSITE, PRINTS and Pfam; later, information from the annotated protein family resource, InterPro, was also used. By February 2015, with >90 million entries, TREMBL2 was 163 times larger than Swiss-Prot, illustrating the vast gap that has opened between manual and computer-assisted strategies.

The scale of the problem is illustrated in Figure 3.3, which contrasts the growth of Swiss-Prot and TREMBL in the last 20 years or so, up to February 2015, and effectively demonstrates why complete annotation have become vital. Redundancy was becoming increasingly problematic – UniProtKB doubled in 2014 alone – as submissions of multiple genes for the same or similar organisms (especially for bacteria) poured into the resource. The high levels of redundancy were keeping the database contents, and increasing the amount of data to be processed. From April 2015, redundant proteins within species were removed, and redundant sequences stored in UniParc resulting in a dramatic decrease in the size of the resource: in November 2015, UniProtKB reduced ~54.5 million entries, a drop of something like 40 million sequences (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/uniprot/TremStats!).

# 3.10 InterPro

The development of TREMBL was spearheaded by the EBI by Rolf Apweiler in collaboration with Bairoch at the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics (SIB). In 1997, Apweiler, Bairoch and Attwood discussed the feasibility of uniting PROSITE and PRINTS. This time, the plans were much more ambitious: the goal was to create an instrument to analyse and annotate the growing numbers of uncharacterized sequences, essentially developing a tool to annotate TREMBL. The case for this proposal was much, especially as there were also ProDom to bring into place, and the first release of Pfam had been announced. A new proposal was submitted to the European Commission, and the vision of an integrated protein database was finally founded.

A release of the unified resource was made in October 1999, characterising 2,423 families and domains in SwissProt 38.0 and TREMBL 11.0 – which were also InterPro (Apweiler et al., 2001). By that time, PROSITE and the Features Database had undergone major changes: PROSITE had grown threefold to 1,370 entries, while the Features Database had grown fortyfold to 1,157 entries, and had been re-named ‘PRINTS’ (Attwood). 

The development of InterPro was a collaborative effort to integrate various protein-level databases. The goal was to provide a unified interface for protein families and domains, allowing researchers to search across multiple sources simultaneously. Over the years, the number of contributing databases grew, and the complexity of the data integration increased.
