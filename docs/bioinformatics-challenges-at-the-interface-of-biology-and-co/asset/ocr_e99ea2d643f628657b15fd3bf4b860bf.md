The question is, how to achieve this? As we've discussed, doing so reliably in an automated, algorithmic way is extremely hard. For any piece of text, even if we could robust identify the various entities and processes mentioned in its sentences, and interpret its graph with enough accuracy to collate the various assertions, would we meanfully work out of which are part of the ‘scientific claims’ of the paper, and are they mere-products of scholarly writing?

The alternative to identifying assertions algorithmically is to enlist the help of article authors, knowledgeable editors, or perhaps the ‘community’ – so-called ‘crowdsourcing’ of information has, after all, been successfully in many other areas. Getting authors of scientific papers to embrace the vision of producing content that is semantically rich, however, has so far proved difficult: they seldom offer official accession numbers or unique identifiers for the entities described in their papers, or part with anything but the barest minimum of data for ‘auxiliary materials’; and the prospect of them proving their core assertions in machine-friendly forms is, for the time being, almost unimaginable. For that of the resistance is cultural – in the main, authors have never had to do this before, now they see or understand the benefits of doing so now have the time to learn; part also comes down to journal policy, or lack of journal policy – if journals do not uphold standards and best practices, authors are unlikely to comply. Consequently, the various recent initiatives that have tried to increase the semantic richness of articles with varying levels of success. Let’s take a closer look.

# 8.9.1 Making articles ‘semantic'

One of the first attempts to make articles more ‘semantic’ (in the sense of being ‘readable’ by both human and machine) was the so-called FEBS Letters experiment. This was a pilot project in which the journal editors worked with a small group of authors and with the curators of the MINT molecular interaction database to integrate data in published articles with information stored in databases (Serhang and Gerstein, 2008). To make the project tractable, the initial focus was on identifying and highlighting Protein-Protein Interactions (PPIs) and Post-Translational Modifications (PTMs), as attempting to make all the biological information and concepts described in any given article, or set of articles, machine-readable was obviously impossible (Superga et al., 2008).

At the core of the experiment was the structure of the Structured Digital Abstract (S) – this was a ‘simple’ mechanism for capturing key facts in a machine-readable, XML-encoded summary, to render those facts using text-mining tools (Serheng and Gerstein, 2007). The process was straightforward: participating authors were simply asked to use Excel spreadsheets to collect PPI and PTM data, together with a range of experimental, biological and technical details referred to in their articles (information about the nature of the experimental evidence, characteristics of the interacting proteins and their biological roles, the PTMs required for interaction, protein identifiers with links to MINT and UniProtKB, etc. Ceol et al, 2008)).
