At one level, the three paradigms outlined above achieve the same effect – they provide a way of storing data in a robust and extensible format that avoids the need to develop bespoke readers and write hand-rolled formats. While in some sense, they are interchangeable, they are actually best suited for different purposes.

JSON is lightweight, quick to read and write, and is often sufficient for serialising the kind of data structures typically found in programs into a format suitable for persistent storage, or for representing the results of a function call in a serialised manner. Its lightweight and comparatively terse syntax, however, tends to mean that the contents of files are not 'self describing', and do not explicit namespaces or qualifiers, so a little extra effort is required on the part of the programmer to make JSON files suitable for exchange between applications. XML, by comparison, is much more verbose and has support for URIs, namespaces and such; reading and writing XML files is more costly (and slower) than JSON, but the results are typically better suited for exchange between applications. Finally, RDF is the most expressive and complex of these paradigms, and best suited for applications in which precision about data types and values is important, as those that aggregate and process data from multiple sources.

# 7.5 Giving meaning to data

Having identifiers for things, and ways of representing data that are readily accessible to machines, gets us only far: if we want to use machines to help us make sense of data and information, rather than merely record it, we need to head towards 'knowledge engineering'.

To build a knowledge-base that a computer can 'understand' or reason about, we need to start with a controlled vocabulary: an agreed set of tokens that are allowable in a particular context, that can be associated with particular meanings, and can only change by agreement from a central authority. Having established a controlled vocabulary, we can make richer associations between terms; for example, defining things as synonyms or opposites of others, and eventually defining rules or axioms that govern these relationships. If the associations and rules are captured from a controlled vocabulary, then we have the basis to build graphs for an unambiguous and 'computable' body of knowledge.

To illustrate the process of 'bootstrapping' a machine-readable body of knowledge, imagine a scenario where a team of bioinformaticians from various laboratories collaborates to build an integrated suite of protein sequence-analysis tools.
