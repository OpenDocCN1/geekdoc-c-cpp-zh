During the next decade, the advent of automated processes overtook time-consuming manual peptide sequencing and dramatically increased the rate of sequence determination. In the meantime, another revolution was taking place, spurred by the elucidation of the first protein almost structures using the technique of X-ray crystallography: those of myosin and hemoglobin (Krewend et al., 1958; Muirhead and Perutz, 1963). Building on the sequencing work, this advance set the scene for a new era in which structure determination was to take centre stage in our understanding of theophysical mechanisms and evolutionary processes. Suctive was this that many more structural studies were initiated, and the number of deduced structures burgeoning.

In parallel with these developments, advancements in sequencing technology in the late 1970s meant that, for the first time, DNA sequences could be determined, and their protein products deduced from them, relatively quickly. Incredibly, if it had taken 8-10 years to determine the sequences of few small proteins (insulin and ribonuclease), dozens of protein sequences could now be rapidly deduced by translation of sequenced DNA.

The technologies that gave rise to manual peptide-sequencing strategies, then to automated peptide and DNA sequencing, and to protein structure determination at atomic resolution, were responsible for producing the first waves of sequence and structural data. Key to handling this expanding information was the recruitment of computers to help analyse and store the accumulating data. Initially, newly determined sequences were published in the literature to make them available to the wider community. In this form, any researcher wishing to exploit the information had to obtain a copy of the original article, and then to type the sequence(s) into a computer by hand, a process that now seems unbelievable.

Eventually, because collating data into electronic repositories would make it more efficient and easier to store and use the data in future. This realisation led to the birth of the first electronic databases. At this time, the idea that molecular information could be managed using electronic repositories was not only very new, but also very daunting. Consider that technologies we take for granted today (email, the Internet, the World-Wide Web) had not yet emerged; therefore there was no simple way to distribute data from a central database, other than posting tapes and disks to users on request. This method of data distribution was fraught with difficulties; it was cumbersome and slow; it was also relatively costly, and, alarmingly, some of the first databases to pioneer did adopting and/or data-sharing policies that threatened to drive away many of their potential users.

From these tentative awakenings, few first biomolecular databases emerged. From the agonizing slow trickle of determining a single sequence per decade, to a speed of thousands of sequences per second (a rate that will seem inconsequential years from now), sequencing technology has revolutionised the pace of data acquisition, and thrown up new challenges for the field of bioinformatics.
