Their experiments included DOIs, keyword markup, hyperlinks to external resources, interactive figures, a document summary, with a tag-cloud and citation analysis, and much more. Shotton et al. augmented the paper with downloadable spreadsheets containing data from the tables and figures, and 'mashups' with data from other articles and Google Maps. The truth is that, while Shotton's study showed that significant value could be added to articles post hoc, the enhancements were both platform- and browser-dependent, and were confined to just one article. The obstacles to making knowledge hidden in published articles machine-accessible are legion. Most of the scientific literature is therefore unsemantic (and consequently, in Shotton's view, 'anthetical to the spirit of the Web); and it will doubtless be time before tools, policies and incentives to encourage authors to engage with this enormous challenge are finally in place. Notwithstanding the difficulties, however, inmates the gap between human- and machine-readable scientific content remains. The Internet and digital technologies have irrevocably changed the way in which scientific experts continue to use the literature, and publishers continue to seek innovations to satisfy the increasing demands of their authors and readers.

We've touched, on just a couple of the many approaches that have been used to enhance scientific documents, to make the data and knowledge they contain more accessible and reusable. Although no universal solution has yet materialised, some themes have emerged: most HTML or XML-based, most provide hyperlinks to external websites and term definitions from relevant ontologies, and so on. However, some researchers have imagined far-reaching possibilities, where data in online papers become 'more alive': Bourne (2005), for example, a dream of which readers could toggle a table of numerical values and their graphical representation, allowing immediate investigation of relationships latent in the data; or between a static image of a molecule and interactive 3D views, allowing real-time exploration of molecular interactions or active sites described in the paper, without the need for specialized knowledge of esoteric data-manipulation tools – perhaps the scenarios showcased in the 2009 article by Attwood et al.¹¹⁵, and described more in detail in the following sections.

It turns out, many of the article repositories, ontologies, machine-readable standards, and so on, already exist for integrating content with data in public databases. Part of the reason is probably simply that the community has grown up with static manuscripts: most researchers are just used to reading articles in PDF or HTML formats (Lynch, 2007) and don't see the need to change – breaking deep-rooted cultural habits is hard. ## 8.10 Linking articles with cognate data

Utopia Documents (Attwood et al., 2009; Attwood et al., 2010) is a ‘smart PDF reader’. It was developed, the many benefits of enriched HTML articles, like those mentioned above, most researchers still read and store papers as PDF files. PDFs have several advantages over HTML pages, not that they’re self-contained objects that can be ‘owned’ and stored locally – their originating websites can go offline, their original versions can be withdrawn by the journal, your subscription to that journal can continue to access whenever you choose. A more subtle point is that journals, over the years, have made investments in producing typographic (on paper and in digital PDF) that are aesthetically pleasing and easy to read – in consequence, at least on reasonably large screens, article PDFs are much easier to read than their HTML counterparts.

Utopia Documents (UD) behaves like a familiar PDF reader (Adobe Acrobat, KPDF, OS X View, etc.), but its functionality can be augmented with field-specific ontologies and plugins that use Web ‘on the fly’ to retrieve and display data related to the article. With this in place, the software transforms articles from static files of their printed counterparts into dynamic gateways to further knowledge, linking information (terms, phrases, objects) embedded explicitly and implicitly in text to online resources, and offering seamless access to auxiliary data and interactive analysis visualisation software – and all this without compromising the integrity of the underlying PDF file.
