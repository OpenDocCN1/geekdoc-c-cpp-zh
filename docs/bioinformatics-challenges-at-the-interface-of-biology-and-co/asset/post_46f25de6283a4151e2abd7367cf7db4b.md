FAST was widely used for several years, but its use was more or less superseded by the development of BLAST (Altschul et al., 1990), which benefited from greater efficiency and statistics, and later, by implementations of 'gapped-' and PSIBLAST. The BLAST algorithm is relatively straightforward, as is its central concept 'segment-' or word-pairs (rather like the k-tuples of FASTA) – when comparing two sequences, these are pairs of same-length sub-sequences that form an ungapped alignment. BLAST computes all word-pairs between a query sequence and those in a database, invoked by a scoring threshold. When two non-overlapping word-pairs on the same diagonal are found within a gapped alignment algorithm within distance of each other, an extension step is invoked using dynamic alignment programming to extend the path of aligned residues in both directions (Altschul et al., 1997) and yield the final gained alignment.

During this process, for the original implementations of BLAST, the best-scoring of all the extended segments (the Maximal Segment Pair, or MSP) was assigned a probability value (or p-value), denoting the probability of achieving at least one score greater or equal to that of the MSP by chance. Later versions of BLAST invoked the e-value, denoting the number of alignments with score greater or equal to the calculated alignment score likely to be found by comparing random sequences of the same length and composition as the query to a database of similar size and position. This allowed to ascribe a measure of statistical significance to retrieved matches very soon made BLAST the tool.

In Position-Iterated BLAST, or PSI-BLAST (Altschul et al., 1997), a Position-Specific Scoring Matrix (PSSM), or profile, is created from sequences above a specified threshold by an initial BLAST run. Successive BLAST searches are then made, each adding newly identified to the profile and increasing the sensitivity of subsequent searches. The appeal of PSI-BLAST is its speed and ease-of-use. By combining pairwise database comparisons with more sensitive, profile-based searches, it permits to provide the best of both worlds. However, it lacks the power of motif-based methods, and its iterative nature necessitates close supervision — if a false match is incorporated into the profile, subsequent searches will retrieve more false matches, as the profile is successfully corrupted. PSI-BLAST is nevertheless an important component in sequence analysis.

Today, a wide range of implementations of the BLAST and FASTA database tools can be swift and easy to use, but they are not the end of the sequence alignment line. Often, the results they provide broad strokes, invoking a generic family to which a query sequence belong; however, they are insensitive to the traits that differentiate closely related - the ones that allow curators, for example, to assign possible functions or structural features to newly deposited databases. To achieve this resolution usually requires additional analyses, including those seek to identify functional motifs or structural domains, or those designed to elucidate functional determinations between protein families. ## 4.3.4 Tools for comparing multiple sequences

As already mentioned, a number of different methods have been developed to help identify conserved functional sites, specific functional determinants that differentiate close family members, structural domains, and other characteristic sequence features. Many of these approaches hinge on the ability to make connections between two sequences; most necessitate the creation of multiple sequence alignments, only in this context can conserved family traits be seen. Multiple alignments provide succinct views of the relationships between members of families, allowing us to practise aspects that are similar, to identify features that are different, and to determine observed differences are meaningful.

In the previous section, it is not the goal of this book to provide detailed descriptions of the algorithms that underpin the process of multiple sequence alignment; instead, we'll briefly mention general approaches, before focusing on how alignments are exploited to create diagnostic signatures suitable for identifying generic functions and family-specific traits.

Computing multiple sequence alignment is non-trivial. As we saw in Section 4.3.4, dynamic programming algorithms guarantee to find the optimal alignment between two sequences. When dealing to multiple sequences, however, these algorithms become computationally prohibitive, and heuristics become necessary to reduce the time to find (not necessarily optimal) alignments. The majority of alignment packages are based on the 'progressive alignment' (Hogweg and Hersper, 1984), a greedy heuristic method that aligns sequences in pairs, following the branching order of a family tree or 'guide tree'. The progressive algorithm can be embedded in an iterative loop, where both guide tree and alignment are reestimated until convergence: example implementations of this approach include ClalW (Thompson et al., 1994), MUSCLE (Edgar, 2004), PRALINE (Simosis and Heringa, 2005), ProCompass (Do et al.), and MAFT (Kaft et al., 2005). A related approach is embedded in PRANK, a phylogeny-aware progressive alignment program that, uniquely, distinguishes insertions from deletions, and gives rise to alignments with very distinctive distributions (Lytnonen and Goldman, 2010).

The number of alignment tools available is overwhelming. Like many mentioned above, we use similar approaches. To obtain the choice between programs, several methods have been combined into a consensus package, called M-Coffee—as the name suggests, this is based on T-Coffee (Wallace et al., 2006). The program computes alternative alignments using eight or more of the most accurate programs, combining them into a single result that's consistent with the original alignments. In doing so, M-Coffee can produce 'better' alignments than the best individual method almost 70 percent of the time (Wallace et al., 2006). Nevertheless, this does not provide a panacea for the problem of computing reliable alignments for homologs. Some progress towards improving the alignment of distantly related sequences has been made by including structural information, albeit tethered to the usual caveat about the scarce availability of protein 3D structures, and the disparity between sequence and structure-based alignments (this issue is discussed further in Section 5.8.4).
