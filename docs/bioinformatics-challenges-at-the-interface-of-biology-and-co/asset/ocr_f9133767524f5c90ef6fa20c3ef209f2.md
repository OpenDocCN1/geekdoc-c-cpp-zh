# 0.3 What's in this book

# 0.3.1 The scope

Rather than offering an authoritative exposition of current hot topics in bioinformatics, this provides a framework for thinking critically about fundamental concepts, giving new perspectives on them, and hence trying to bridge the gap between where traditional bioinformatics is now and where computer science is preparing to take it in the future. It's an exploration of the philosophical divide between what we want computers to use in the life sciences and what it's actually possible to do today, with current computer technology. You might wonder why this is so interesting – don't all the other books out there have this sort of thing? And the answer is no, this book is different. It isn't about how to do bioinformatics, computer science or biology; it's about what happens when all these disciplines collide, how to pick up the pieces afterwards, and what what's likely to happen if we can't put all the bits together in the right place.

The problem is, when bioinformatics began to emerge as a new discipline in the 1980s, in a sense, a fix for what the biological sciences needed at the time and what computer science could provide at that time (mostly database technology and fast database algorithms). The discipline evolved in a pragmatic way, addressing local problems with convenient, more-less readily available solutions. So, the course is the very essence of evolution: there is no grand plan, no sweeping vision of the future – just just that needs to be solved, in the most efficient, economical way possible.

The ramifications of systems that evolve around: a system that was designed for the future, but just does the 'job', will almost certainly be an excellent tool for precisely that job, at that time; it's unlikely to stand to uses for which it wasn't originally devised. To be fit for new needs, the system will probably need to be modified in ad hoc ways, at each stage of the process gaining further complexity. Eventually, an evolving system is likely to be confronted by new developments, developments were not foreseen when the system began its evolutionary journey. At this juncture, we are at an all-to-familiar turning point: to move forward, either we must continue to 'do and mend' the system, to patch it so that it will continue to 'do the job', or we take a breath and slowly throw it away, and start on a completely new path.

Many systems in bioinformatics have arrived at this point; many will arrive there soon. So there is a good time to stop and reflect on how to move forward. But it would be foolish to try to do this without considering how bioinformatics reached this point in the first place. For this reason, the book goes, unashamedly, by reassembling a little of the history of where bioinformatics comes from; balance things up, it includes a posted history of computer science too. Then it looks at the things we want to do now, weren't even thought of back, and discusses why some of these things are so difficult (or impossible) to do with current systems. The book then crosses over to the other side, to look at the issues from a computer science perspective, and to explain what needs to happen in order to make some of the things we want to do doable. Finally it moves deeper into the emerging technologies, to consider some of the new problems that remain for the future.
