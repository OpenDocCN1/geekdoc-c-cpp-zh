Automating the process of DNA translation is complicated by the need to identify likely ORFs, which in prokaryotes, are normally the longest pieces of sequence interrupted by stop codons (although, sometimes, ORFs may overlap, and times the shortest). Next is the difficulty of knowing which of the six possible reading frames is the correct one (noting that coding sequences may occur in forward and reverse frames).

For eukaryotes, the situation is more challenging, because coding sequences may be interrupted by noncoding sequences (introns). Hence, we need to be able to correctly recognise the signals that denote intron-exon boundaries (e.g., GU at the 5' end and AG at 3') – this is hard computationally because the signals at intron-exon boundaries are extremely small, and introns may be very large and far apart. Decoding the correct frame, the correct number of introns and exons, their specific boundaries, and sewing them into contiguous coding sequences is thus a highly error-prone computational process.

Other issues include the use of the appropriate gene, as the 'standard' isn't quite universal, and the quality of the starting sequence – sequencing errors can lead to incorrectly assigned missed start/or stop codons, leading to truncation of a gene, extension of a gene, or missing genes.

To reduce reduce, additional evidence that may be used includes intrinsic sequence features, such as the GC-content characteristic of coding sequences, translation start and stop sites (ribosome binding sites, promoter regions, etc.). Most reliable of all is search a database to see whether the ORF encodes a known protein.
