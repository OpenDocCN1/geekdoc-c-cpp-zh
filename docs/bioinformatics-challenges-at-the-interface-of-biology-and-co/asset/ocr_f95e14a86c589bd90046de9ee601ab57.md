# Figure 4.30

Extended motif from Figure 4.29, and the frequency matrix derived from it, with amino acids grouped by their physicochemical properties.

For the hemoglobin chain fingerprint, 242 sequences were found to match all the motifs after the conserved tyrosine at the end of the motif thus 242, and again, the frequency distribution across the rest of the motif changes again – overall, however, the matrix remains relatively sparse.
The full potency of the fingerprint method is not just from iterative development of the motifs, but from the mutual context provided by motif neighbours. This is important because fingerprints provide a biological context to motifs are matched in the correct order and with appropriate intervals between them (see Box 4.7). This sequences to be practised even when parts of a fingerprint are present, e.g., a sequence that matches four motifs may still be diagnosed as a family member, if they are matched in the correct order and between them are consistent with true neighbours members. This scenario is illustrated in Figure 4.33. The plot on the left-hand side shows the result of scanning the human hemoglobin (P69905) with the hemoglobin fingerprint, ALPHAHAEM (PR00612); strong matches are seen to each of the five fingerprints. By contrast, the plot on the right-hand side shows the result of scanning an Antarctic dragonfish hemoglobin (P230166) with the same fingerprint; here, only four of the five motifs are matched. Although the sequence fails to make a significant match with motif 1, it's nevertheless a family member, by judging the locations and order of the motifs that it does.
