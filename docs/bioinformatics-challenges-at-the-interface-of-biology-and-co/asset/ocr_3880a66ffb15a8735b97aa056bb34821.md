# 5.8.2 What properties do my sequences share?

The task of understanding alignment means goes a deeper than the num-
ber and placement of gaps by different algorithms. As we saw in Chapters 3 and 4,
we align sequences in order to be able to compare them effectively, to detect shared
characteristics, or differences, to infer their ancestry and how they’ve evolved to
aquire specialised functionalities and structural modifications. So, what features do
tsequences in Figure 5.5 share?

Although these are or less ‘standard’ outputs, it’s actually virtually impossible
to ascertain what sequences have in common (it’s even harder than it appears in
Figure 5.5 because we’ve had to ‘sew’ some of the aligned blocks to get them
on one line). The difficulty lies in how they’ve been presented – mostly black letters on
a white background. In practice, outputs like this to be ‘consumed’ by
manual editing tools, which usually practise a range of visualisation options – most
use colour. But then use different colours to depict individual amino acids or
amino groups; as a result, comparing alignments can be challenging, as we can see
from Figure 5.8.

What’s clear from Figure 5.8 is the degree of confusion induced by the different
colour schemes (to make the comparison easier, the portion of the alignment is
same in each). Here, Kite and Zimmermann are simplistic/hydrophobic/
representations of more detailed scales (recall Chapter 2): results in one half of each
scale been coloured white (hydrophobic), while those in the other half have been
col green (hydrophilic). CINEMA uses the ‘standard’ amino acid property groups
and colours illustrated in Figure 2.8 ClusterX is a kind of hybrid scheme, in which
colours appear be used to denote general amino acid property groups and
de conservation: hence, Thr and Ser are sometimes coloured green, but some-
times; sometimes Arg and Lys are red, but sometimes not; sometimes Ala, Leu and
Ile are blue, sometimes Asp and Glu are purple, sometimes not.

The picture is further confounded by the that assigned to the amino
acid groups in the CINEMA and ClusterX schemes are almost complete oppo-
ts red denote an acid group in one, but a basic group in the other; blue denotes an
basic group in one, but hydrophobic character in the other; aromatic denotes an
group in one, but an acid group in the other; yellow denotes the sulphur-containing
amino acid Cys in one, but in the O, as shown in Box 5.3. With this kind of complexity,
interpreting alignment means in terms of the characteristics they are far from
trivial.

To make sense of the different results, a user must first understand the meta-
phor before the colour scheme. The ‘standard’ scheme implemented in CINEMA
s to try to reduce the cognitive effort required to understand alignment outputs,
by basing the colours on a scheme that many of us learned from our earliest physics
and chemistry lessons: as a result, we intuitively that red is oxygen-containing,
henidic acid (negative charge); that blue is basic (positive\charge); that yellow is sulphur containing; and so. In principle, therefore, we don’t
have to fight quite so to see what hides in the CINEMA alignment as we do in the
ClusterX alignment.
