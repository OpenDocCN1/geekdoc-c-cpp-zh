Viewed from the perspective of software engineering, even a simple task like finding the cubed power of an integer, is a complex multi-step process. This is partly a consequence of how memory works in modern computers, and partly because the processor's arithmetic abilities are limited to only a handful of simple (almost axiomatic) operations: addition, multiplication, and subtraction are provided, among others, but not as complex as cubic power operations. We must break down the power operation into a series of multiplications. First, the binary representation of the number to be cubed must be copied from its current resting place (likely the machine's RAM) to a register on the processor. Let's call this first register ax, as it acts to accumulate the result. The ax register's value must be copied into another register (bx) so that there are two copies of the original number. The ax register can now be multiplied by itself, overwriting its value with an intermediate result; then, the bx register (holding its copy of the original) can be multiplied with this intermediate result, once again overwriting the ax register with what is now the cube of the original input. This final result is now available to be written back to memory prior to the next calculation.

We see, then, that can be specified and understood conceptually by the simple notation x³, when translated into something approaching the reality of computing machines today, becomes a more complex set of tasks; and even this believes the true nature of the technology involved.
