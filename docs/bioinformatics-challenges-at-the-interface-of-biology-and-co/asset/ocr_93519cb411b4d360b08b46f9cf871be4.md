# 6.4 Evaluating algorithms

Once we've sufficiently appreciated the limitations of computers, we can begin design-algorithms to solve our problems. Many factors play a part in how an algorithm performs (i.e., *efficiency*) in relation to other algorithms and the amount of information it must process. This will be the focus of the following sections.

# 6.4.1 An example: a sorting algorithm

As an example on which to base our discussion, we will examine the deceptively simple task of sorting lists; in the world of bioinformatics, this may represent anything from similarity scores in BLAST result, computed energy values of protein fields. In everyday life, we don't often have to sort objects, although we all do it on occasion: ordering playing cards during a game; managing our physical collection; or for the computationally minded, compiling a shopping list in the order which products are likely to be found in-store. Regardless of how we decide what it means to be ordered, we can do things quite instinctively. The problem with many instinctive tasks is that it often harder to write instructions on how to perform them than it is to perform them. As an exercise, try to just do that: formulate a method of sorting lists of arbitrary length.

As it happens, there are many disadvantages to designing a sorting algorithm, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. Here, we'll introduce one of the simplest sorting algorithms: bubble sort.
