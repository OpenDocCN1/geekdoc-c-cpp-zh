## DOIs

In early 2000, a group of academic publishers came together to form Crossref, an independent organisation with a mission,

- to enable easy identification and use of trustworthy content by promoting the cooperative development and application of a sustainable infrastructure.

As a 'trusted' DOI Registration Agency, Crossref enabled the use of a consistent, electronically resolvable identification scheme across all manner of academic disciplines. Bringing together a critical mass of competing publishers to cooperate on a competitive task – one of such valuable to all concerned – was a gargantuan task, requiring deft political maneuvering. The process nearly found many times; however, the benefit of the DOI applied to scientific literature has numerous.

A Digital Object (DOI) is a qualified identifier used to reference some digital object in a way that's independent of its location on the Web. DOIs are primarily used to identify identical resources (articles or parts of) for use in citations and cross-references, but can theoretically be used to identify any locatable resource.

The pure form of the DOI, and an example,

```
doi: <RI><RT>/<ID>
doi: 10.1042/BJ20091474
```

Here, after initial doi: we see three levels of identification, beginning with the 10, which identifies the DOI registry (RI), continuing with the number 1042, which identifies the DOI registrant (RT), and finally, the string BJ20091474 (ID), which uniquely identifies an object within that registrant's namespace. This example refers to an article registered by the publisher Portland Press Ltd. with the Crossref registry.

Because pure DOIs are indistinguishable URIs, there needs be a central registry and resolution service to map them to locations on the Web. The International DOI Foundation provides such a service, taking a DOI and returning a URI.

```
http://www.biochemj.org/bj/424/bj4240317.htm
```

As a convenience, Crossref provides a dereferencing service that allows DOIs for articles registered with them to be written in the form:

```
http://dx.doi.org/10.1042/BJ20091474
```

which resolves to the same problem for URI in the biochem.org domain.

As mentioned previously, this DOI could change at any point, if the journal's website was registered, but the DOI will remain the same; so whereas linking to biochem.org directly easily break in the future, taking the small cost of extra step of dereferencing via Crossref, or the International DOI Foundation, provides an additional level of robustness against change.

Unfortunately, versioning and provenance are absent from the DOI system, so we need to be confronted with the same problems in literature as we are with biological databases. A single DOI often refers to the latest version of an article, no explicit information about whether it’s the submitted draft, a pre-version, or the published copy. Similarly, errata and retractions aren’t supported natively by DOIs. This leads to an ambiguity that can sometimes cause problems; however, given the breadth of the domain over which it operates, the DOI system works remarkably well.

## ORCIDs

Perhaps more than identifying digital objects like Web pages and database records, or inmates like books, is that of uniquely identifying people – not just authors of articles, but also those who’ve contributed to the creation of data, or who need to be credited for one reason or another for a scientific result. There are many more people than are publications; and more institutions with international levels of politics than humans have much more ambiguous names than do scientific articles (Spruce, 2007). It's estimated that around two-thirds of the six million or others in ONLINE share a family name and a first initial with least one other author (see Box 7.2), and then, on average, any particularly ambiguous name refers to either (Torvik and Smalheiser, 2009). Other authors also encountered this thorny problem in some (e.g., Farkas, 2008; Torvik et al., 2003; Aronson and Lang, 2010; Jonallagada and Topham, 2010).

Several attempts have been made to tackle this. In November 2010, the National Library of Medicine announced its intention to establish PubMed ORCID IDs; and the Dutch ‘Author Identifier’ has been used with some service in the Netherlands. However, domain-specific or national-identifiers have obvious limitations in an increasingly connected research environment. A large number of publishers launched identifiers of their own, such as the ‘ResearcherID’ from Thomson-Reuters and Elsevier’s ‘Scopus Author ID’, but the idea of trusting control such an important global enterprise has met with understandable resistance from the community, and ID systems have seen take-up outside their respective environments.

The perhaps most moving has been the creation of a not-for-profit organisation to manage the development of the Open Researcher and Contributor ID (ORCID). Formally, ORCID IDs are URIs with the ‘path’ component being a 16-character identifier consisting of nine-digits, separated in groups of four by hyphens (for example, Amos Bairoch, of many renowned bioinformatics databases, has http://orcid.org/0000-0003-2826-6444). ORCID IDs are often provided by publishers to ORCID: 0000-0000-xxxx. Technically, ORCID IDs are subsets of the International Standard Name Identifier’ scheme used of academic publishing to identify contributors to television programs, songs and newspapers, etc. Not only does ORCID have a pleasing acronym, but it appears to be attracting the kind of cross-publisher and international critical mass necessary to turn an initiative into a practical reality.
