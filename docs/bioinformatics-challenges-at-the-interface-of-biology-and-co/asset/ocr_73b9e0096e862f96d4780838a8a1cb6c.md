The G proteins with GPCRs are constitutively-associated heterotrimers, with α β and γ subunits. The model for how this works like this: when the receptor is charged by its ligand, a conformational change is triggered; this induces a conformational change in the α subunit, causing GDP to be released and replaced by GTP. GTP-binding induces the α subunit to dissociate from the receptor and complex to interact with intracellular molecules (‘second messengers’ cAMP and calcium) to then pass the message by altering behaviour of other cells.

For example the adaptor molecule arrestin couples GPCRs to the activation of Src-like kinases and the formation of multi-molecular complexes. Structural components (e.g., poly-carotene regions, PDZ, and SH3 domains) also facilitate GPCR coupling to a variety of signaling molecules. Overall, this diversity is elicited by GPCRs due to the complexity of mechanisms by which they transduce signals.

Most of these responses don't depend on a single biochemical route, but the integrated result of the functional activity of an intricate network of interconnected cytoplasmic signaling pathways. Individual responses depend on receptor expression levels, coupling specificity and the nature of signaling molecules. The more we discover, the more labyrinthine the mechanisms of signal transduction appear to given this richness of interactions – terms like serotonin, HTM,, receptors and – are sometimes used to avoid representing the breadth of biological routes they exploit.

As systems complexity, it becomes increasingly difficult to predict their behaviour. Perturbations in such systems have multiple effects on the level of genes, transcripts, and metabolites. In biological systems, it is therefore inevitable that we should try to reduce them to more manageable components (traditionally, science has been reductive). Yet, by doing so, we alter the contexts in which they operate, which is leading to increasing levels of uncertainty in the predictions we then make. The situation is still more challenging when we appreciate that biological systems are also highly dynamic and undergo continuous flux. Thus, for example the fact of particular interactions within metabolic pathways is unlikely to be determined by the binary control of gene expression (i.e., whether a gene is ‘on’ or ‘off’) rather, by a sequence of events that determines the level of expression relative to a particular point in time.

It follows that if we wish to gain insight into the ways of biological functions, search tools alone are unlikely to suffice. The manifestations of biological functions are complex and ever-changing. As tools like BLAST, FASTA and InterProt have been the mainstay of genome annotation efforts which reduce a complex problem to a more tractable one – that of identifying and quantifying relationships between sequences. But identifying relationships isnt the same as identifying their functions. Failure to identify this fundamental point has generated a number of problems for bioinformaticians and computer scientists, and demands that we adopt more rigorous approaches to the ways in which we describe function for humans and for to interpret.
