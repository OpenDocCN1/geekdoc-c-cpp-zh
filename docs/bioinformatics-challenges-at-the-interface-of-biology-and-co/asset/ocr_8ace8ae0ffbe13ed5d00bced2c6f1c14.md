This realisation had profound consequences across the globe, as it was generally agreed that a centralised bank was inescapable as a tool for managing nucleic acid sequence information efficiently.

From this point, the clock was ticking, and the race was to establish the first public nucleotide sequence repository. The first past was the European Molecular Laboratory (EMBL) in Heidelberg, which set up the EMBL data library. After an initial period, the first of 568 sequences were made in June 1982. The aim of this result was not only to make nucleic acid sequence data publicly available, and free exchange of data, but also to provide a European focus for computational and biological services (Hamm and Cameron, 1986).

Nevertheless, from the outset, it was clear that maintenance of such a centralised database, and its instant services, would require international collaboration. In the UK, of the EMBL data library were maintained at Cambridge University, together with the user-manual, indices and associated sequence analysis, and retrieval software. This integrated system also provided access to the GenBank library of sequences that was being developed in parallel with Los Alamos National Laboratory (Kanesha et al., 1984).
