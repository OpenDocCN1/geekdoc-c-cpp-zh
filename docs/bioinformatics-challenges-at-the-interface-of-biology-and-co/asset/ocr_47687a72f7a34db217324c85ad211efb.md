When two non-overlapping word-pairs on the same diagonal are found within a gapped alignment algorithm within distance of each other, an extension step is invoked using dynamic alignment programming to extend the path of aligned residues in both directions (Altschul et al., 1997) and yield the final gained alignment.

During this process, for the original implementations of BLAST, the best-scoring of all the extended segments (the Maximal Segment Pair, or MSP) was assigned a probability value (or p-value), denoting the probability of achieving at least one score greater or equal to that of the MSP by chance. Later versions of BLAST invoked the e-value, denoting the number of alignments with score greater or equal to the calculated alignment score likely to be found by comparing random sequences of the same length and composition as the query to a database of similar size and position. This allowed to ascribe a measure of statistical significance to retrieved matches very soon made BLAST the tool.

In Position-Iterated BLAST, or PSI-BLAST (Altschul et al., 1997), a Position-Specific Scoring Matrix (PSSM), or profile, is created from sequences above a specified threshold by an initial BLAST run. Successive BLAST searches are then made, each adding newly identified to the profile and increasing the sensitivity of subsequent searches. The appeal of PSI-BLAST is its speed and ease-of-use. By combining pairwise database comparisons with more sensitive, profile-based searches, it permits to provide the best of both worlds. However, it lacks the power of motif-based methods, and its iterative nature necessitates close supervision — if a false match is incorporated into the profile, subsequent searches will retrieve more false matches, as the profile is successfully corrupted. PSI-BLAST is nevertheless an important component in sequence analysis.

Today, a wide range of implementations of the BLAST and FASTA database tools can be swift and easy to use, but they are not the end of the sequence alignment line. Often, the results they provide broad strokes, invoking a generic family to which a query sequence belong; however, they are insensitive to the traits that differentiate closely related - the ones that allow curators, for example, to assign possible functions or structural features to newly deposited databases. To achieve this resolution usually requires additional analyses, including those seek to identify functional motifs or structural domains, or those designed to elucidate functional determinations between protein families. We will explore some of these methods in the next sections, but first, a warning.

proteins exhibit rich evolutionary relationships and complex molecular interactions, and hence present many challenges for silico investigations. Problems arise if we lose sight of the underlying biology and fail to understand the limitations of the methods we're using. Tools like BLAST and FASTA, and those we will discuss next, are essentially biological-unaware, character-string matchers. To make inferences of evolutionary relatedness and of precise biological functions, we must temper our expectations by considering how such tools can realistically depend on biology's dynamic soup of molecular and cellular components. When we extrapolate from a string of letters turned a database search algorithm to the assignment of a piece of functional annotation to a new database search, it's imperative to examine evidence from different quarters, and guided by the relevant biological context, then to evaluate its biological significance, irrespective of its statistical significance.
