# # Box 7.2 What's in a name?

It's proved difficult enough over the years to agree on identification schemes for biological objects; but at least, in these cases, we're only talking about reaching consensus on how names affect a particular scientific domain. When it comes to unambiguously identifying people on a global scale, the problem is far more complex: search any social network, as LinkedIn or Facebook, for your name, and you'll most likely find that you're not unique.

Similar issues arise when searching bibliographic databases for our own papers, or for our colleagues' papers—but how can we be sure we found the articles from our right? Table 7.1 shows the results of searching PubMed, at some random date in 2014, with the family names and first initials of the authors of this book and some of our colleagues. Accordingly, S.Pettier appears to be the least common name, and C.Tyler. The question is, how many of the retrieved articles were actually authored by us and our colleagues?

Adding a second name to such query usually significantly reduces the problem, narrowing the results to more reliable article sets; but there's a problem—neither Thor nor Marsh have middle names (or, at least, neither with a middle name). If we were to identify them unambiguously, we must fine-tune the search (e.g., instead of a middle name, we can provide the name of the city/university where the authors work). This appears to work for Thorne, suggesting that he was the author of original 104 articles. However, without going through the list by hand, can't be sure we've not missed something relevant in the discarded 90, perhaps for a different university or in a different city.

For Marsh we find that adding the 'Manchester' keyword doesn't work, as the list of articles still contains the work of 'D Marshes'. To get down to the right papers, we would have to further increase the specificity of the search by adding another query term—computer science. This is to do the trick. For Taylor, we've almost certainly discarded relevant articles in the 336 articles we've thrown away, and it would take much more sensitive queries to establish a representative set of papers.

This simple exercise shows how difficult it is to unambiguously retrieve the articles of a specific author from repositories like PubMed, given that so many authors share the same name. As we've seen, we can work around the issue using filters to narrow the search space; nevertheless, authors with European names like 'D Jones' (9,803 PubMed articles in February 2015) and 'D Smith' (16,742 articles), or with common Asian names like 'J Wang' (59,967 articles) and 'Y Li' (56,408 articles) present much tougher problems.
