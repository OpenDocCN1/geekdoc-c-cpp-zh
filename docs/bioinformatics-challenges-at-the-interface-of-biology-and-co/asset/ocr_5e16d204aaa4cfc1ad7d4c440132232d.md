In practice, producing a non-redundant database in this way is more challenging, because it can be hard to decide where to draw the line: should we eliminate sequences that are 99 per cent similar, on the assumption that they are probably the same (only the difference, being a sequence or retention of the initial methionine residue)? Or is this too conservative, and should we choose 95 per cent similarity cut-off? No matter which approach we use, the likelihood is that a composite database will still contain some level of redundancy and some amount of error – this is because the source databases contain non-systematic (usually human) errors, which are very hard to trace and can't be corrected automatically.

Consider NRDB, the default database of the NCBI's BLAST service (Chapter 4). This resource includes updates of Swiss-Prot and daily updates of GenBank, and however, its construction is fairly simple. This means that multiple copies of the same protein may be retained as a result of polymorphisms or sequencing errors; those that have been corrected in Swiss-Prot are re-introduced when sequences are re-translated back from the nucleotide sequences to GenBank, and many duplicates are deplicated fragments. The contents of the database are not redundant (strictly speaking, it is more appropriate to consider NRDB as a non-identical database).

Redundancy issues are tackled more systematically in UniProt (which amalgamates Swiss-Prot, TREMBL and PIR). In UniProt, similar sequences are clustered to yield representative sequences using three different sequence identity cut-offs: the resulting resources are UniRef100, UniRef90 and UniRef50, no pair of sequences in the representative set has over 90 or 50 per cent mutual identity; in UniRef, representative sequences and their sub-fragments are presented as a single entity.
