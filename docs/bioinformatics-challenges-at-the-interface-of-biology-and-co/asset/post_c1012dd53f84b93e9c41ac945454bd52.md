
## Chapter 2 The biological context

## 2.2.7 Conceptual translation and gene finding

Armed with a near-universal genetic code, we can use this information to understand more about the process of translation. However, at this point, we're going to make a brief context switch from the realms of 'natural' translation (what nature does) to the world of 'conceptual' translation (what computers do). There are various reasons for this. First, most protein sequences are mostly inferred from DNA; it's the products of conceptual translation that mostly populate our protein sequence databases – we'll not describe the biological translation process in great detail here, but encourage you to look at the overviews in Box 2.6 and 2.7. Second, most gene sequences are also inferred from genomic DNA; 'genes' delineated in nucleotide sequence databases are therefore mostly predictions. Outlining the process of computation, then, leads us to considering some of the issues of gene finding; this, in turn, prepares us for the fact that the apparently biological entities housed in databases – the genes and protein sequences – might not be the definitive objects we expect them to be. Introducing these concepts provides a useful foundation for the sequence analysis discussions in Chapter 4.

## Box 2.6 The process of translation

As with the process of transcription, until now we have talked only about abstract terms about translation, the process by which protein sequences are synthesised from mRNA templates (Figure 2.17 (a)); the biological event, rather like splicing, is mediated by another molecular machine, termed a ribosome. Ribosomes comprise a large and a small subunit (represented by the ellipses in Figure 2.17 (b)). In bacterial ribosomes, the small subunit contains so-called small ribosomal RNA (SSU RNA) in complex with ~20 proteins; the large subunit contains large ribosomal RNA (LSU-RNA), a smaller ribosomal RNA (SS rRNA, name), and around 30 proteins. In eukaryotes, the subunits are larger: they are much larger, and many more proteins contained in each subunit.

The mechanism of protein synthesis is illustrated with one codon at a time in Figure 2.17 (c). The ribosome binds to the mRNA, moving along one codon at a time. Adaptor molecules called tRNA, with an amino acid having been removed from the tRNA, bind to the mRNA within the ribosome. The amino acid is attached to the end of a growing chain. The uncharged tRNA is removed from the complex and may now be reloaded with another, identical, amino acid molecule. A new, charged tRNA then binds to the next codon in mRNA, and the ribosome moves one position forward.

As with transcription, specific codons are employed to do this. Each codon (AUG) is complementary to a start or stop codon. One end encoding the methionine, and on the other, signaling the start of protein synthesis – this is the first AUG codon; the ribosome begins its work at the first AUG codon; this is downstream of it initially binds to the mRNA. Bacteria contains a Shine-Dalgarno sequence, a conserved region of eight nucleotides close to their 5' end; this sequence facilitates binding to the ribosome. The first tRNA recruited is known as an Met tRNA. This is a modified methionyl-tRNA, in which a formal group has bound to the Met on the loaded tRNA. Modified Mets are only used when AUG codons behave as initiators; those occurring elsewhere in a sequence lead to the normal recruitment and incorporation of Met into the growing protein chain. Stop codons are straightforward to grasp – they are simply those that have no associated tRNA. On reaching a codon, a protein known as a release factor enters the ribosome instead of a tRNA, triggering the release of the complete protein chain.

Before proceeding, note that the code depicted in Figure 2.16 is based on natural translation from RNA; for conceptual translation from DNA, we must replace U with T. Let's suppose that we have a piece of genomic sequence. The first problem we encounter is we don't know which bits are coding. Coding regions are found in what we call Open Reading Frames (ORFs). So, what we have to do is look for ORFs: these with a start codon (ATG), and with a stop codon (TGA, TAA or TAG). The longer the ORF, the more likely it is a coding region, as noncoding DNA tends to contain relatively high densities of stop codons.

The next problem is that we don't know where starting. Remember, as we traverse the DNA strand from 5' to 3' end, the triplet could start at the first, second or third base, and the coding sequence (CDS) could lie in any of these three frames, as shown in Figure 2.19 (a). But there's another complication - DNA sequences contain two strands, either of which may encode a gene. So, in fact, there are six frames in which to look: three forward and three reverse. As we only have one piece of genomic sequence, we must therefore look in the reverse complement – Figure 2.19 (b): here, note that we find start codons both in the first forward frame and in the third frame of the reverse complement.

## Box 2.7 The role of tRNA in translation

A key molecule in translation is transfer RNA (tRNA), a single stranded molecule in which base-pairing between short regions give rise to hairpin-loop-like structures. These are most familiar to us in clover-leaf structure, as depicted in Figure 2.8.

![](img/1af42ff402ef4720886c50af0bb8e9b5_56_0.png)

The three bases at the heart of central hairpin loop of the clover-leaf are termed the anticodon. tRNA acts as an adapter molecule. The anticodon connects to the mRNA and growing protein chain. The sequence here, whose codon is AGC (reading from 5' to 3'), is that of the tRNA for alanine, otherwise referred to as tRNA-Ala (or tRNA). This can form complementary base pairs with the codon GCU (reading from 5' to 3' in mRNA), which is a codon for alanine in the standard genetic code, as shown below (remember, reading codons in mRNA (5' to 3') requires anticodon to bind in the opposite direction):

Organisms possess populations of tRNA that can base-pair with 61 codons that encode amino acids. While these tRNAs have different anticodons and a variety of other sequence differences, all exhibit the same clover-leaf structure. Many tRNAs can pair with more than one codon, due to the flexibility of the pairing at the third position in the codon, which has been dubbed wobble. The number of tRNAs varies widely between organisms. In eukaryotes, wobble tends to be less frequent than in prokaryotes, and hence a greater number of distinct tRNAs are required.
