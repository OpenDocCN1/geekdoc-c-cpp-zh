
## Chapter 1 Introduction

The recognition deserved to the pioneers of mathematics and computing that steered the path from these early crude machines to the laptops and servers of modern time is well beyond the scope of this book. Instead, we leap forward in time, past all the technology-related creativity inspired by the Second World War, to the early 1960s, and the coining of the term 'computer science' by analyst George Forster. By this time, the computer had many of the properties associated with contemporary machines: a Central Processing Unit (CPU) also able to manipulate numbers represented in binary and to execute a program toograph manipulations; some form of memory to store and retrieve data; back-up devices to retrieve the contents of memory when the device was switched off; connective infrastructure to allow the machine to communicate with devices other than itself; and various forms of input and output (screens, keyboards, etc.) to allow users to interact with the machine.

The first computers focused on solving mathematical problems (like finding large numbers) for which no equation existed that could be 'solved' by a human with pen and paper, but for which iterative algorithms could be devised to played the computer's strength of being able to mindlessly 'crunch' numbers according to prescribed rules. Over time, programs were written to perform calculations that were relative to pragmatic ethics: calculating payrolls and other business tasks, simulating engineering problems, and recording and searching over data individuals for all manner of purposes. Today, computers numerous mobile devices, to autonomously guiding the trajectory of space probes visiting the outer reaches of our solar system.

Of course, this unsashamedely selfish, whirlwind tour of the history of computer science overlooks a multitude of incredible developments and technological innovations of the past few decades. On the face of it, many of these appear to be merely improvements to existing ideas, resulting in something that is just a bit faster, smaller, bigger, greener, lighter—insert the adjective of your choice here—than before. Many of these improvements, however, are not the result of 'trying a bit harder': development of the next generation of particular technology has required considerable research and creative effort to overcome or circumvent what were previously unreachable limits of engineering or physics. Other inventions have resulted in turning points in the way we work: the first machines 'cheap' enough to have on one's desk, or enough to run off a battery in one's pocket; the early Internet as connective infrastructure for commerce and research, and the Web as more friendly endroit, with much broader appeal. Recently, social networking, wireless and mobile technology, and the ability to create and distribute audio and video, have changed how society interacts in ways that were inconceivable only a few years ago. Many of these technologies are now so integrated into modern society that we fail to recognise they exist, much less the effort and thought that led to their creation.

## 1.3.2 Computer science meets bioinformatics

So, what has allowed today's devices to quietly perform such device, incredibly complex tasks than the 'primitive' machines of the 1960s? Although faster, cheaper, smaller, less power-hungry, and in almost every way better, more sophisticated and interconnected than their older counterparts, the modern computer has very much the same basic components as its ancestors: its core remains a device for manipulating binary numbers. What has changed is our ability to use patterns of numbers to represent increasingly complex and sophisticated things.

## Bioinformatics challenges at the interface of biology and computer science

Using a computer to calculate mathematical results is, in some ways, easy – this, after all, is what computers do. Determining whether book someone is likely to buy next, based on the reading of people who have been established having similar tastes, or to bring this technological tale back to the life sciences – predicting which individuals are likely to suffer from particular diseases, based on their genetic profiles, are altogether complex problems for computers to solve.

Here, then, is a vast gulf between a computer’s ability to manipulate binary numbers, and our desire to use these machines to examine, understand and manipulate concepts – ostensibly, have no relationship to binary numbers at. Bridging this gap, by devising techniques for representing our increasingly sophisticated knowledge in ‘computable’ power, is a fundamental challenge of modern computer science, whose only solution is to transform bioinformatics. Next to the most prominent developments we've just talked about, but one that's already shaping how computers deal with data, and particularly data representation. In the early days of computing, flat-file databases, with their field-based searches, were the norm. In time, these were replaced by relational databases that captured some of the structure of the information they modelled; and now we have graph databases, which attempted to model the meaning of data through the use of controlled vocabularies, allowing ‘intelligent’ data-text-mining algorithms to scour them for nuggets of knowledge. This trend towards richer (and thus semantic) data representation reflects our ongoing quest to make the growing volumes of data accessible to us as knowledge, will touch innumerable aspects of our lives, our relationships with each other and with our planet, to our future medical practice and health.

Albert much of a thing in the realms of computer science, the flat-file database was the cornerstone of early bioinformatics, playing a pivotal role in housing the gradually accumulating quantities of biological information. But modern high-throughput biology changed all this: this data explosion caught many bioinformaticians off-guard, and brought a growing realisation that the technologies underpinning the earliest databases were simply not up to the job. If storing the now-vast quantities of biological data became increasingly demanding, reasoning over the data became more difficult still. Consequently, in the aftermath that followed, a gulf opened up between what we wanted to be able to do with bioinformatics on the one hand, and what we could actually do with it the other.

## 1.4 What did we want to do with bioinformatics?

Because the origins of bioinformatics were rooted in sequence analysis, the earliest years aimed to understand what biomolecular sequences could tell us about the functions of genes and their encoded proteins. Ultimately, researchers wanted to discover what amino acid sequences could tell us about the functions of genes and their encoded proteins. Perhaps more importantly, researchers wanted to know how sequence and structure could be used to elucidate the roles of particular genes and proteins in pathogenic processes.

Later, with the advent of the human genome, the goals became increasingly ambitious, and the focus of attention turned more and towards bioinformatics to revolutionise molecular medicine. Researchers wanted to identify the genetic determinants of rare syndromes and common, pervasive diseases like cancer, and paved the way to personalised therapies, where an individual's genome could be used to design better drugs with the minimum of side-effects.

Ultimately, the goal was to integrate all molecular and cellular data in such a way as to model pathways and, indeed, not just how individual cells work, but complete assemblies of cells work in organs, including the brain.
