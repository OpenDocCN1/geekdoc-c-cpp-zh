# Chapter 1
Synthesizer Fundamentals

The synthesizer is usually defined vaguely as an electronic instrument that produces and controls sounds. When discussing this with our colleagues, we agreed that the electronic density of this definition might be constraining. Why couldn't the didgeridoo or the Jew's harp be considered synthesizers? In fact, might not instrument other than the human voice be a synthesizer? Consider the American inventor Thaddeus Cahill to be the inventor of the modern synthesizer. In 1897, he applied for a patent for the Telharmonium or Dynaphone, a 200-instrument that used steam power electric generators to synthesize sinusoids—it was even polyphonic (capable of playing more than one note at a time). Leon Therin invented the Theremin in 1919, which included a touchless interface that controlled the amplitude and pitch of a sinusoid. Unlike the Telharmonium, the Theremin used electronic amplifiers to reproduce the sound rather than acoustic horns. However, inventors created unique instruments like the Ondes Marten, Trautonium, and Mixtartonium, to name few. The first use of the word synthesizer is in the Couplex-Givet Synthesizer of 1929. RCA marked the Electronic Music Synthesizer that used 12 tuning forks to control the oscillations. Max Mathews experimented with computers for rendering and controlling an audio signal in 1957, while Robert Moog popularized the hardware and is credited with inventing the first voltage-controlled synth in 1963. His synths many of his inventions and inventions such as the Moog ladder, which we will study in Chapter 7. In 1967, he marketed electronic modules that could be connected in systems to produce various sounds—he called these synthesizers. The driving force for these synthesizers was not electronic music but rather neoclassical composers of the 20th century. It is not uncommon to read about associations between composers and electronic engineers, for example Moog worked with composer Herbert Deutsch and Donald Buchla teamed up with composers Morton Subotnik and Ramon Sender to produce avant-garde compositions. Sergio Franco and Ken Pohlmann worked with composer Salvatore Martini and the SAL-MAR construction shown in Figure 1.1 in the 1970s.

Moog's synthesizers consist of various components, each with its own board, knobs, lights, and joints. The oscillator generates primitive waveforms—sinusoid, sawtooth, and square. The amplifier uses a voltage-controlled amplifier to modify the amplitude of a signal. The filter implements various basic functions such as low-pass and high-pass types. The envelope generator creates a varying output value that is used to control the amplitude or filtering of other modules. Combining the modules in various ways produces the most interesting sounds; the result is somehow greater than some of the parts. Each of Moog's modules had its own output, and control jacks were connected together using audio cables patch cables, which were adopted widely in use across record studios. This is the origin of the term patch, which we use often. A patch is a recipe for connecting and setting up the parameters of each module to form a specific sound. You hear the fully patch cord patch patch at all the time when around synthesizers. Moog's earliest synths were fully modular each module (including the keyboard) was on its own, disconnected from the others until someone physically connected them. Modular systems are the most generalized in this respect—they have the most number of combinations of modules (patches), which require that you physically connect each component.
