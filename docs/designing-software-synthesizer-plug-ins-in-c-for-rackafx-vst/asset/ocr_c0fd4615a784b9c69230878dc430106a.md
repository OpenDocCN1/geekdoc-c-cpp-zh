# # 1.11 Discretized Signals

If we discretize or sample the input, then we will also be discretizing that. we will likewise chop it into slivers. The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) or the Discrete Time Fourier Transform (DTFT) is the result of taking the Fourier transform of a sampled signal $f_s$. The sampled signal in Figure 1.19 (b) can be described mathematically as:

$f_s (t) = f(t)U_0(t - nT)$ (1.10)

The second term $U_0(t-nT)$ represents a train of unit pulses of width $T$. Figure 1.19 (b)'s infinitely skinny line-circle-on-top represents an ideal situation where the sample rate was infinitely fast. In reality, each sample has finite duration—$f_s = 44.1$ kHz the period is about 23 microseconds. So Equation 1.10 is really describing what we see in Figure 1.19(b) where we multiply the pulse by $f(t)$. The pulse height is the value of $f(t)$ at the center of the pulse.

Plugging Equation 1.10 into Equation 1.9 yields the DFT (or) sampled spectrum $F_s(ju)$:

> $F_s(jw) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} f(nT)e^{-jnwT}$ (1.11)

Equation 1.11 says that the discrete spectrum is a set of pulses that approximate the continuous analog curve. In fact, you might have seen something like Figure 1.19(b) in a calculus book where you approximate the area under a curve with a set of rectangular pulses. The DFT can also be thought of as a zero order approximation of the original signal. As the period becomes smaller, the approximation becomes more accurate. The spectral components rather than summing them yields more information. Figure 1.20 shows two real worlds and their signals. The first has been banished to the Nyquist rate. The second is a clip of music (Blink-182's 'Feeling'), also banished. Both are DFTs over a frame of 1024 samples. The shifted DFT is in the frequency domain to see the harmonic relations and amplitudes. You will notice some noises and notches in what should be a smooth contour; this comes from the fact that the signal is bounded. This obeys the original Fourier series, so you can define pulses or spikes at the harmonic intervals with empty spaces between them. The short clip of music is only quasi periodic and contains a rich of harmonics—there are no gaps and the pulses are all smashed together; in this case it is meaningful to draw the DFT as a curve and understand that the complete spectrum is a area under the curve. The music DFT is plotted in the frequency domain, an ultimate view of the spectrum that coincides with our perception of frequency. Note that in both cases, the positive frequency domain is shown. This coincides with we know that the negative frequency domain contains mirror image of the same data.
