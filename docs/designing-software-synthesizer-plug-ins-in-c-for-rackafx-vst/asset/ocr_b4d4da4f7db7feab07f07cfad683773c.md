# # Chapter 6

Envelope Generators (EGs) are used to shape the amplitude, pitch, and frequency content of the synthesized signal. The three applications are usually called Amp-EG, Pitch-EG, and Filter-EG, respectively. In this chapter we will investigate and design an envelope generator that will provide the basic functionality we need for all applications. An Amp-EG is referred to as a Transient Generator (TG) on other synthesizers. The transient generator shapes the time domain or decay of the signal. The EG is applied to a controlled amplifier. In analog systems, this is a Voltage Controlled Amplifier (VCA) on a digital signal, which is a Digitally Controlled Amplifier (DCA). In this chapter we will design the EG and its application to amplitude, pitch and frequency shaping. We will design a flexible EG that can provide an analog mode based loosely on the CEM3310 integrated circuit from 1979, and a digital mode that operates linearly-in-dB, to the decay and release curves of the Yamaha DX7.

# ## 6.1 Envelope Generators

The earliest analog generator is a variation of the charging and discharging of a capacitor. A capacitor is an electronic component that is designed to store a charge Q. When combined with a series resistor RATT and shunt resistor RDC, it charges and discharges exponentially as shown in Figure 6.1. The series resistor RATT controls the attack (attack time), while the shunt resistor RDC controls the decay (decay time).

Figure 6.2 shows this concept. In Figure 6.1(a) SW1 is closed, connecting a constant voltage to the capacitor via R1, which limits the current (charge flow) and therefore controls the charging time. SW2 is open when the capacitor has no duty. The capacitor charges quickly at first and then taps off. In Figure 6.2(b) SW1 is opened, disconnecting the source, and SW2 is closed. R2 limits the current to a discharge path and therefore controls discharging time. The capacitor voltage VCAP moves exponentially up or down.

When with some switching logic, the basic circuit of Figure 6.3 can generate multiple attack and/or decay phases, as different resistors may be switched in and out the control voltage turned on or off. If the decay generator is connected to a voltage source VSUS, the capacitor voltage will decay down to this level as shown in Figure 6.3.

The flattened top of the attack portion of the curve is not entirely desirable in the envelope generator for synthesis. The way is to prevent the capacitor from fully charging. In the Curtis CEM3310 envelope generator used in several other synth designs, the capacitor only charges to 77% of the asymptote value, giving the attack of a more linear contour. This is shown in Figure 6.4.

The CEM3310 is essentially a variation of this charge/discharge circuit, and produces the time domain shape shown in Figure 6.5, where a note on triggers switching logic that applies a series resistor to charge the capacitor over an attack time, RATT. Then, a discharge resistor is added after the discharge switch is closed, and the capacitor discharges to a fixed sustain level, VSUS. A note off triggers the logic to apply a different resistor across the capacitor, which then discharges the rest of the way. This creates four phases of operation: attack, decay, sustain, and release, abbreviated as ADSR.

The most important feature of Figure 6.5 is that the A, D and R segments are not linear but exponential, following the capacitor charge/discharge curves. When used to control an amplifier, they result in an amplitude contour that sounds to our ears which perceive amplitude logarithmically. When applied to pitch contour of an oscillator or filter cut-off of this EG will move the control value around exponentially; fortunately these are also natural-sounding pitch and frequency changes for our ears.
