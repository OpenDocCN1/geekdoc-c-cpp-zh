# # Delay Effects

A deep discussion of audio effect signal processing is beyond the scope of this book; you can find many algorithms and theoretical discussions in Designing Audio Effects Plug-Ins in C++. Audio effects, however, are now standard in most synthesizers. The more popular synth effects (FX) are delay, reverb, chorus/flanger, and vocoder. If you have played a professionally programmed patch just about any commercial synth, you have most likely heard at least one audio effect operating on the synthesized signal. There is something that audio effects bring in the patches that can't be done with oscillators, EGs and filters. Rather than try to give a brief rundown on several effects, on the most versatile and powerful family—delay effects.

Just about every of my favorite sounds has at least the delay effect on each. The addition of a simple delay effect makes a surprising difference in the final synth sound—fuller, deeper and wider. MiniSynth again with the addition of delay effects; simple one-note lines can turn into frenzy of patterns or long synths. You will be amazed at the addition of this chapter's delay-based effects if you use samples that already have effects added to them. In this chapter, we will add not one but four very interesting and often used types of delay effects: stereo delay, two multi-tap delays and a ping-pong delay.

Audio effects come in two fashions in music synthesizers: insert and master. The master effects are called "Aux Bus" or "Aux". An insert is inline with the voice block of the synth. It is often placed before or after the DCA and may be mono or stereo, depending on parameter and voice type. These effects typically include dynamics, processors and distortion effects. Master effects operate on the final (or master) stereo output of the synthesizer on all voices that are voicing. These effects include delay, reverb, chorus/flanger, and anything that needs to be mixed in the original dry sound with wet/dry ratio. This is a good rule of thumb—if you have a dry/mix control, it is probably a master effect. Our delay is a master effect, operating on the final output. Therefore, we need only one delay unit than for each voice. That said, many synths will allow you to use an effect in either way—a delay effect might be applied to a single voice as an insert or as a master effect. Notice that the delay effect sits outside the voices since it is a master effect.

# # 13.1 Circular Buffers

Delay effects employ a DSP programming concept called circular buffering. A buffer is really just an array of values. When you use an index to step through the array, you can move in the (positive) or reverse (negative) direction by increasing the index up or down. When you go to a boundary of the array at the top or bottom you increment or decrement the index, you move outside the array, ending with a crash.
