The ideas before BLEP is to start with a trivial waveform introducing discontinuities, then merge this BLEP edge into the trivial waveform to soften or smooth the discontinuities. This means that you must modify some number of samples on each side of the discontinuity. which may depend on resources (memory, CPU usage, etc.). In practice you only need to modify a few sample values to experience a drastic reduction in aliasing. The quality of BLEP is considered to be excellent—better than BLIT-SSW. Brandt also experimented with filtering the BLIT signal with a minimum lowpass filter before integrating it to create the BLEP. This MinBLEP eliminates most of the lookahead requirement but has its own problems. Airy explains that MinBLEP introduces a frequency dependent DC offset and requires precise knowledge of the phase and amplitude of the discontinuity prior to interpolation. In practice, we are only going to take a few samples after the discontinuity, and the lookahead issue really isn't much of a problem.

The BLEP is merged with the trivial waveform by subtracting a residual. The residual is the signal that remains after an ideal step has been subtracted from the BLEP. For a rising edge, the residual is added, while for a falling edge, it is subtracted. This may be implemented with an inverted residual as in Figures 5.8 (a) and (b).

BLEP is somewhat open-ended when it comes to generating these signals (as the final implementation details). For example, we choose an obvious and simple lowpass impulse response using the Airy/Bright used the ideal sin(x) function as the theoretical basis for the BLEP design. However, you do not necessarily need to use sin(x) function. You may use any lowpass impulse response you want. You may also decide to make trade-offs in the filter design to reduce aliasing in parts of the spectrum while allowing it in. A common goal is to eliminate aliasing that is close to the Nyquist frequency.
