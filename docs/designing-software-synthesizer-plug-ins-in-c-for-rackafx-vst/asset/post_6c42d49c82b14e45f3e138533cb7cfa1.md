## 7.6 Moog Ladder Filter

Together with series (forward) and parallel (ground) paths, Robert Moog invented a filter that became the ladder filter shaped of transistors and capacitors. From this point, the ladder filter became the most celebrated and extended design of any filters. Several Integrated Circuit (IC) have many variations on this circuit used in synthesizers from nearly every company in the 1970s and 1980s, including the CEM328, CEM3372 and SSM3024. A unique feature of this filter is that changes in resonance create overall changes in the gain; as Q increases, the filter gain drops as shown in Figure 7.25. The rolloff is 24db/octave as a fourth order filter.

![](img/b42d0dca53f4a889d8162d55f7c8c05d_403_3.png)

In the Sallen-Key filter, resonance is formed with a positive feedback loop that reinforces the resonant frequency of the filter. The Moog ladder filter also creates a positive feedback path for frequencies near the resonant frequency. It can also self-oscillate at the resonant frequency. The Moog ladder filter consists of a cascade of four synchronously tuned LPF stages in a global feedback loop as shown in Figure 7.26.

Figure 7.25: The Moog ladder filter at a variety of Q settings.

Figure 7.26: The Moog ladder filter topology and analog signal graph.

![](img/b42d0dca53f4a889d8162d55f7c8c05d_404_0.png)

Putting first order LPFs makes such a filter, but it is not resonant. The way this filter becomes resonant has to do with the phase response of each section. The phase shift cutoff is -45 degrees for a first order LPF stage. Each stage adds another ~45 degrees. After going through four of these, the filter shifts the cutoff by ~180 degrees. The output is out of phase with the input. The output is fed back into the input through a negative K, which flips the phase at the cutoff so it is in phase with the input. This amplifies the cutoff frequency and frequencies that are very close to it, resulting in a resonant peak. If K = 0 there is no feedback and resonance occurs. When K = 4 we achieve 100% feedback through the loop; K needs to be 4 (rather than 1) because the feedback energy is spread across the four filter stages. When K = 4 the filter self-oscillates.

## 7.7 Moog Ladder Filter Gain Compensation

The loss of gain as the Q increases is sometimes problematic, especially if the filter is used as a bass instrument. However, the reduction in gain also prevents the filter from overloading. Unlike the Oberheim SEM and Karg305, there is a built-in mechanism to alleviate clipping. Curtis Electronics Specialists show how to control the loss of gain. The filter in the ARP6200 is also a Moog ladder derivative that incorporates passband compensation. Zavalishin proposes simply boosting the input by a factor of 1 + K. By making the boost factor 1 + K, a variable version is implemented as shown in Figure 7.27.

## 7.8 Oberheim Xander Variations

In 1984, Tom Ober modified the ladder filter design for the Xander synth. He realized that by taking outputs from each of the four LPFs and combining them in various ratios, many new filters could be obtained. The following are some of the variations available (other than the normal LPF):

- second order LPF
- second order BPF
- fourth order BPF
- second order HPF
- fourth order HPF

Details of how this was accomplished in analog as well as further combinations are in (Oberheim, 1984). The Xander used a CEM3372 IC, which labels the LFs as A, B, C, D. We can label the gain coefficients in the same manner and produce the result in Figure 7.28.

The values of the A, B, C, D and E coefficients are listed in Table 7.1 (Välmäki and Huovilainen, 2006), and the frequency responses are reported in Figure 7.29. Experimental show these are quite useful and will either self-oscillate under the proper conditions.

## 7.9 Nonlinear Moog Ladder Filter Models

The analog filter is nonlinear in nature due to the transistor ladder diff pairs conducting current. The differential voltage and current relationship uses the tanh() function. Huovilainen (2006) proposed a nonlinear model that inserted a tanh() block in each first order LPF stage (which was based on a biquad structure) and another in the feedback path. Zavalishin (2012) proposed an "nonlinear model" by inserting two tanh() blocks in trapezoidal integration, at the input and another in the feedback path. Välmäki and Zavalishin both proposed "cheap" versions with only one tanh() block; for Välmäki it is at the input x(n), for Zavalishin it is inside the feedback loop as shown in Figure 7.30. We have also experimented with the saturating integrator used in the Oberheim SEM or one of the LPF stages. You are urged to experiment with these as well as your own nonlinear variations. Remember that for true self-oscillation, the loop must be 4.0 nonlinear saturation gain. This is how the waveforms feature a saturation control that brings their gain up, and beyond, unity.
