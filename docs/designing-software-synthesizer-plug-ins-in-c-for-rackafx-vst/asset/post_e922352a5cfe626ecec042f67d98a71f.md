
One way to use the joystick is to simply move it around in real-time while you hold a note or multiple notes. The output is a mix of the sources. As you move the joystick, the sound evolves as the mix ratios change, always totalling 1.0 so that no clipping occurs. As you move the joystick, the vector EG amplitude value for each source. It's a near perfect trick, but you use a playing-hand to move the joystick. A more interesting and useful feature of vector synths is the ability to program the joystick in a path. Each time you trigger a new note, the joystick moves along the path you've assigned. The path consists of a set of vertices and timing values between each as shown in Figure 11.9. Each pair of vertices forms a segment. In this case, the path starts at vertex 0 and makes its way to vertex 1 in 1 second, then on to vertex 2 in 0.75 seconds, and so on, until it stops at vertex 4. Therefore, segment 1 lasts 1 second and 2 is 0.75 seconds.

The author notes that a vector is defined as something that has both a magnitude and a direction. The vector path dictates the direction of the joystick, which controls the instantaneous mix. The ProphetVS and Wastestation allow you to program a path consisting of four segments that correspond to the traditional attack, decay, sustain and release components of an EG. RackFX's built-in path program allows up to 15 segments, and the optional vector path generator object (available for all APIs) gives you unlimited number of segments.

The programmed path in Figure 11.11(a) is only one type. In this simplest case, the mix vector stops at vertex 0 during the note on, travels through the path terminating at vertex 4, and stays until the note is released. If the note is released after the path is finished, it simply stops. This is one of the modes in VectorSynth. The ProphetVS and Wastestation do not feature this path; instead their pathing is to move the first three segments as then stop at vertex 3 and stay not off, when the path moves into the last segment, as shown in Figure 11.11(b) (adapted from the Wastestation Player's Guide).

Both VectorSynth and AnySynth feature this mode of operation, called sustain mode, and the second-last vertex in the joystick program is used as the sustain path. In addition to the normal sustain mode, the ProphetVS and Wastestation offering loops options. For example, moving through the path to vertex 3 and sitting on it during the sustain path, you could move to vertex 3 then loop back over some or all of the previous segments, as: 0->1->2->3->2->3->2..., where you are looping back and between vertex 2 and 3 the sustain path.

## 11.7 Orbits and Orbits

You can modulate the (x,y) coordinates of the joystick with LFOs, envelope generators, MIDI note numbers or other modulation sources. The ProphetVS and Wastestation allow you to modulate the x and y coordinates independently. For example, you modulate the x coordinate with an LFO and leave the y coordinate alone. This would shift the joystick from left to right as shown in Figure 11.12 (the joystick could be anywhere; it is located at (0,0) in this example). The LFO amplitude sets the modulation depth and the LFO frequency rates at which the joystick coordinates move. In Figure 11.12(b) the sound fluctuates between 100% A and 100% C at the extremes.

A particularly interesting way to modulate the mix vector is to apply LFO to the coordinates using the normal output to modulate one axis and the quad phase output to modulate the other axis. This produces a motion that varies from elliptical to perfectly circular, depending on the amplitude of the two components, as shown in Figure 11.13. You might remember using a phasor (a rotating point in space) to generate sine and cosine functions in your math or physics classes. This is the opposite idea—here, the sine and cosine functions are used to generate a rotating point. This kind of modulation creates a rotor—that Klaus Pohl coined in 1998 when he created the "Rotor Module" for the DSP Library. According to John Bowen, Pohl got the idea for rotor from the rotary engine. It creates a modulated sound with a cyclical nature. Moog calls this modulation an orbit. We will use the two terms interchangeably; projects have a rotor that allows you to adjust the orbit-X and orbit-Y depths. If you modify your LFO to allow an adjustable phase relationship between the two outputs, you can create any kind of ellipse, such as Lissajous patterns. If you modulated the output amplitudes of LFO with another LFO, you could create an orbit that varies from ellipse to circular, as shown in Figure 11.14.

## 11.5 The Moog Anisog Synthesizer

In 2011, Moog reintroduced vector synthesis to the world with its Anisog iOS app. The app has won multiple awards in both musical and technical categories. Like its predecessors, it excels at producing lush wavables such as searing, quickly modulated sounds. Moog names their brand of vector synthesis anisotropic. Merriam-Webster defines anisotropic as "exhibiting different values when measured in different directions", which the vector emphasizes direction and magnitude. The control surface consists of a rectangular grid of 128 wavables comprising eight rows and 16 columns, as shown in Figure 11.17. There is no joystick, as the grid and touch surface replace it.

The rows are calibrated timbres. Each row consists of 16 wavables that are musically or sonically related. Each wavetable is a sample of a sound with a slight variation in timbre, noise or some other attribute the timbres in the same row. For example, one row may feature a sound bass sweep. Moving across the row from left to right reveals that successive wavetable is slightly brighter than the previous, as it were sampled with the filter knob in a slightly different but increasing position. The wavables get more or less resonant, noisy, bass-y, mid-range, transient, et., as you move from left to right. Each row is a complete set of sounds. When you assign the rows, you may assign similar or different sounds. You might populate the upper rows with harsh metallic sounds and the lower rows with soft mellow ones, or interleave them. The timbres themselves are named in a way that is reminiscent of the ProphetVS, such as "Distorted Primes" and "Saw Octave Drift". In other words, they are not named "Slap Bass" or "Liquid Lead"—rather, they are named after the waveform clips they contain.

Anisog allows you to create a vector path connecting up to 14 segments using 15 vertices by tapping on the iOS device. This results in a familiar vector path as shown in Figure 11.18—the obvious difference is that the path is stretched across a rectangle of 128 sounds rather than a diamond. Likewise, you can hear the wavet smoothly crossing into another as you move along the path. You are not allowed to program the time between each segment, as in the ProphetVS and Wastenv; instead you control a single rate value that controls the overall time from the beginning vertex to the end vertex. Thus, longer segments will have a longer time from beginning to end, and shorter segments will have a shorter time. You can also program the path mode as once, loop, or back-and-forth.
