# Chapter 11

Dave Smith and his company Sequential Circuits, invented Vector Synthesis (VS) and introduced the ProphetVS in 1986. Not long after, Yamaha acquired Sequential Circuits and invented vector synthesis in the SY22/SY35/TG33. Dave Smith and the original VS team members then moved to Karg Research and Development to design the Wasteland of synthesizers, which were all vector synthesis. A fundamental goal in synthesis is creating sounds that exist both in time and frequency. Traditionally, you use envelope generators and filters to manipulate amplitude and timbre during note events. MiniSynth and DigiSynth use EG to manipulate the filter and DCA to create this kind of evolution, but it follows the same pattern for each note event and the pattern is relatively short-lived; EG has only a few strokes.

Vector synthesis takes a different approach that is actually ingeniously simple: you dynamically crossfade (mix) the outputs of multiple sound sources. This crossfading introduces shifts in amplitude and frequency based on the original amplitude and frequency content of the sound sources. The Wasteland can generate complex, lush, evolving sounds by holding only one key. In the ProphetVS, crossfolding could take place on complexes that were previously made with the same mixing engine so that stacks of mixed sounds could be re-mixed and again with each other. With the correct programming, the Wave introduced Wave Sequencing, which crossfades waveforms in a sequence creating rhythmic patterns, which you can do by using vector synthesis in this chapter's Vector.

Vector synthesis became popular in the 1990s but has reappeared in the award-winning Moog Analog iPad app, which is the basis for the second project in this era: AniSynth. Sorg re-introduced the WaveSR as a software plugin. Other forms of synthesis have appeared in current designs using Alchemy, which uses vector mixing on variables as well as pitched sounds, and the Bowen Synth Design. John was an original member of the VS teams at both Sequential and Sorg, so vector synthesis is an integral component in Solaris. Figure 11.1 shows a simplified block of the four oscillators labeled A, B, C, and D, so you can think of it as four EGs in one.

In the ProphetVS, the four wavelet oscillators could operate with any 96-on "waves", which mainly consisted of mathematically generated waveforms and short slices of audio samples. Although it shares many similarities with the ProphetVS, the Karg wavelet architecture is very different as shown in Figure 11.2. Each "oscillator" is actually a complete voice unto itself; there are separate filters, DCAs, EGs, two avoidable LFOs, and one avoidable EG for each. The filters are non-resonant, although the new WaveSR plug-in features resonant. The oscillator waves could be individual sample or wave sequences. The WaveSR featured 484-on waves consisting of single cycles, multiple cycles, transient blasts, transient+loops, mathematically generated waveforms, and drifts plus samples of acoustic instrument tracks. While the ProphetVS was a hybrid digital/analog design, the Wasteland was pure digital.

# 11.1 The Vector Joystick

The heart of the vector synthesis is a joystick that is used to mix the outputs of four sound sources labeled A, B, C, and D. The joystick operates over a "mixture plane" or "vector plane". The joystick is labeled A-C (x-axis) and B-D (y-axis), as shown in Figure 11.3. If you've used a joystick for surrounding mix or an arcade game, the Prophet and Wavestation joysticks also move in a circle, but only the central demand (a square turned on its side) the remaining area is a keep-out zone.

There are two properties of this joystick that are important:

- linear motion of the joystick produces mix envelopes that create proper sounding crossading for our ears
- the mix values for the four sources will always add up to 1.0; with the joystick in the center, the mix values are: A:0.25 B:0.25 C:0.25 D:0.25, as shown in Figure 11.3
- the range of joystick coordinates is x = [-1.+1] and y = [-1.+1] with (0,0) at the origin

Figure 11.4 shows the exponential value of the mix values; here the joystick moves along the x-axis towards the vertex. As it moves, you can see the value for C drops off quickly while the A component ramps up—since the distance from the joystick to B is the same, the mix values are the same. Note that when the joystick is at (x,y)=(0,0), the mix value is 100% for everything. The joystick moves around the perimeter of the diamond, and the mix crossfades one source into the next as shown in Figure 11.5.
