This is the link that lets use the FM theory to perform the PM. In both cases, the index of modulation is adjustable from zero (no modulation, sinusoidal output) to maximum value $I_{MAX}$. In both FM and PM, the modulation index is proportional to the deviation (phase or frequency). Figure 12.1 shows a simple oscillator patch as applied to synthesis. The frequency of the MIDI notes sets the carrier's frequency $f_c$. The modulator frequency $f_m$ is usually set as a ratio of the carrier; for the Yamaha DX series, the ratio is selected from a list of interesting values from 0.5 to 25.95. The ratio of the two frequencies, along with the index of modulation, determines the resulting spectrum and rules about the signal's characteristics and musical qualities. These two sinusoidal oscillators and the two controls (ratio and modulation index) can create a vast number of complex and harmonically interesting signals.

The spectrum of an FM signal consists of the carrier frequency plus other related sidebands. The harmonics appear both above and below the carrier. The carrier and harmonics are scaled depending on the index of modulation. In some cases, the carrier may disappear. Figure 12.2 shows the progression of harmonics for the following setup (note that the peaks are normalized to the highest peak; without normalization, the sidebands grow in amplitude as the index increases—the total energy is conserved):

- $f_c = 500$ Hz
- $f_m = 500$ Hz (ratio 1)
- modulation index: varies from 0 to 4

Let's make some observations before looking at math. You can see how increasing the index of modulation adds more sidebands and they form around the carrier. If you observe any frequency including the carrier, you can see that as the index of modulation increases or decreases, the amplitudes of the harmonic components bob up. The carrier and the harmonic components bob up. Unlike typical spectra, where the fundamental frequency is the lowest component (or the highest amplitude) and the harmonics taper off as they increase, FM produces a different kind of spectrum. In fact, a difficult aspect of FM synthesis is it is not intuitively obvious how the index changes the harmonic components that make up the spectrum. When you turn the cutoff frequency of a lowpass filter, it is obvious how the position affects the spectrum. In FM synthesis, the relationship between the index and spectrum is not simple. You will notice in Figure 12.2 that we stopped the experiment at $I = 4.0$, just before the sidebands hit 0 Hz. What happens if we lower the carrier or increase the index so that harmonics appear below 0 Hz? The answer is that these harmonics are reflected back into the positive frequency domain as shown in Figure 12.3. When the harmonics reflect around the 0 Hz line, they may not line up with harmonics in the positive half. Figure 12.3 shows a situation where they do not line up and instead fill the spaces between harmonics. This will have a certain effect on the spectrum. So, we observed that all the frequency components including the carrier move up and down as the index changes. We also observed that sidebands below 0 Hz are reflected as positive frequencies. Math confirms these observations.

## 12.2 FM Spectra

Taking the Fourier transform of Equation 12.1 reveals an interesting story and it explains our observations. The first term tells us that there will be an infinite number of sidebands, and their harmonic frequencies are going to occur at multiples of the carrier plus the harmonics of the modulator, that is:

```
$f_c \pm nf_m, n=1^{\infty}$ (12.8)
```

So if $f_c=1000$ Hz and $f_m=400$ Hz, FM would produce components at the carrier plus those listed in Table 12.1; the negative components would reflect back into the positive domain.

The next thing the transform tells us is that these harmonics have amplitudes that vary as Bessel functions of the first kind and depending on the index of modulation. The Bessel functions are part of the solution to the Fourier transform of the FM signal. This family of functions are all damped sinusoids; they undulate up and down. Since the sideband amplitudes are related to these functions, it explains why they move up and down as the index of modulation changes. The Bessel functions of the first kind are labeled $J$ and indexed from 0 to infinity, producing a set $\{J_0, J_1, J_2, ...\}$. Their argument is the index of modulation, producing a new set $\{J_0(l), J_1(l), J_2(l), ...\}$. The set of harmonic components making up the spectrum is called $\phi_{FM}(t)$, and the amplitudes are:

```
$$\phi_{FM}(t) = A \sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty} J_l(l) \cos(\alpha_c + l\omega_m t)$$ (12.9)
```

Expanding the summation:

```
$$\phi_{FM}(t) = A [J_0(l)\cos(\alpha_c) + J_1(l)\sin(\alpha_c+\omega_m t) + J_1(l)\sin(\alpha_c-\omega_m t) + J_2(l)\cos(\alpha_c+2\omega_m t) + J_2(l)\cos(\alpha_c-2\omega_m t) + J_3(l)\cos(\alpha_c+3\omega_m t) + J_3(l)\cos(\alpha_c-3\omega_m t) + J_4(l)\cos(\alpha_c+4\omega_m t) + J_4(l)\cos(\alpha_c-4\omega_m t) + ...]$$ (12.10)
```

The first term represents the carrier, and the rest are pairs of sidebands. Notice that the signs in front of the Bessel terms alternate for the frequencies that sit below the carrier frequency. This sign you the phase of the harmonic component. This is summed up in Table 12.2, which may be easier to interpret than Equation 12.10.
