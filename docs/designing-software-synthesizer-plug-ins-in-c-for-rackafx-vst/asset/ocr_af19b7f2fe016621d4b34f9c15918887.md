A difficult aspect of FM synthesis is in creating a patch. It is not intuitively obvious how the index changes the harmonic components that make up the spectrum. When you turn the cutoff frequency of a lowpass filter, it is obvious how the position of the knob affects the spectrum. In FM synthesis, the relationship between the index and spectrum is not simple. You will notice in Figure 12.2 that we stopped the experiment at I = 4.0, just before the lower sidebands hit the 0 Hz wave. What happens if we lower the carrier or increase the index so that harmonics appear below 0 Hz? The answer is that these harmonics are reflected back into the positive frequency domain as shown in Figure 12.3.

When the harmonics reflect around the 0 Hz line, they may or may not line up with harmonics in the positive half. Figure 12.3 shows a situation where they do not line up and instead fill the spaces between harmonics. This will have a certain audible effect on the spectrum. So, we observed that all the frequency components including the carrier move up and down in amplitude as the index changes. We also observed that sidebands below 0 Hz are reflected as positive frequencies. Math confirms these observations.

# 12.2 FM Spectra

Taking the Fourier transform of Equation 12 reveals an interesting story, and it explains our observations. The first term tells us that there will be an infinite number of sidebands, and their harmonic frequencies are going to occur at multiples of the carrier +/- the harmonics of the modulator, that is:

```
$f_c \pm nf_m, n=1^{\infty}$ (12.8)
```

So if $f_c=1000$ Hz and $f_m=400$ Hz, FM would produce produce components at the carrier plus those listed in Table 12.1; the negative components would reflect back into the positive frequency domain.
