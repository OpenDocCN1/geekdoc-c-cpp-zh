Unlike the Moog ladder, the diode ladder structure creates multiple feedback loops around each section (LPF2 feeds back into LPF1, LPF3 feeds back into LPF2, LPF4 feeds back into LPF3) as shown in Figure 7.40 (Zavalishin, 2012). A topology known as leap-frog-from (LFF). In addition to the feedback paths, there are also attenuators on the inputs to the last three LPFs.

With the Moog ladder filter, we can eliminate or all of the pass gain loss by boosting the input by a factor of $1+K$. A variable version can be implemented by using the boost factor $1+a$, where $a$ is on the input of $0.0$ to $1.0$ in the same manner as shown in Figure 7.27. Depending on your taste and the application, you may find some kind of compensation is needed.

The diode ladder filter is full of nonlinearities. Zavalishin's advanced models place satur(t) in the input and in local feedback paths around each integrator. If we can attempt similar solutions using the method of placing the nonlinear block at the input, just inside the loop after $u$ but before the first LPF or inside each LPF. Because of the massive gain reduction with resonance, placing the nonlinearity at the input serves to preserve the oscillatory nature of the filter while being superior to others; they are all approximations.

Due to the complexities of the multiple feedback paths, the filter analysis and synthesis of the block diagram is much more difficult than any of the filters in this.
