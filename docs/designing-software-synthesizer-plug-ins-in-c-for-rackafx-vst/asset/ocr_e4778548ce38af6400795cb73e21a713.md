The result is a filter that has a frequency response that is identical to that of a BZT-to-biquad bilinear, but with fewer coefficients, lower memory requirements, and the ability to go directly from an analog block to to digital filter without the BZT or any of the ensuing algebra. This integrator replacement technique is not new. Fetweis (1971), Bruno (1975), El-Masry (1979) and others experimented with analog to our modified filter using analog block diagrams and signal flow graphs (which are discussed in Section 4.15). In 1989, Lindquist repackaged Härm's technique, which he called the Analog Filter Design Method. In this method, passive analog filters are converted directly to digital form by extracting the analog block diagram (or signal flow graph) and replacing the analog integrators with digital integrators (Lindquist, 1989). In all of these cases, the resulting delay-free loops presented fundamental design problems, typically overcome with signal flow manipulation or modification of the digital replacement integrators. Resolving and implementing these delay-free loops is the second part of the VA technique.

Implementing delay-free loops in discrete algorithms is not a trivial problem and has been approached in several ways over the last few decades. In 1975, Szczupak and Mitra described "Detection, Location, and Removal of Delay-Free Loops in Digital Filter Configurations," in which they describe a method for detecting and removing any zero-delay loop by manipulating signal flow graphs. Härmä (1998) provides a technique for resolving delay-free loops in any generalized recursive (feedback) filter with "Implementation of Recursive Filters Having Delay Free Loops." Another technique is described in Borin, De Pol and Rocchesco (2000), including delay-free loops in non-linear waveguide (physical modeling algorithms). Vadim Zavalish described a delay-free resolution in a self-published paper in 2008, then again in the self-published book "The Art of VA Filter Design in 2012. This method uses an instantaneous response to remove the delay-free loop in an algebraic manner In this chapter we will introduce another design technique that we call the Modified Härmä method (Pirikle, 2014). This method is explained in detail, with examples, both here and in Chapter 7. Zavalish's and our Modified Härmä produce identical results, and we will do examples with each of them. Zavalish's work is centered on the preservation of the original analog signal flow through the filtering elements: coefficients multipliers, sums, and integrators. He names this method the Topology-Preserving Transform or TPT. He argues that preservation of the digital filter will respond under time-varying conditions in which the filter's cutoff frequency is modulated.
