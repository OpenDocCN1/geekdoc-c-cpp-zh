You might be saying “alright, but what about ambient synth sounds?” In this case, the goal is to design a sound that is
suited as background material; about the ominous sound of your favorite computer game. If you listen
to just the background part, you might be surprised to hear that the frequency goes through extreme extremes
time—the changes happen slowly, so you don't really notice during gameplay. In these patches, the sound's
requency flows slowly, often in a scary or deliberately unsettling manner. So, even though there are
transient edges or quickly changing frequency components, there is an evolution of frequency going on, albeit
very slowly. If the game composer used a simple sinusoid as the background music for your games, you would just
ignore it, and the creepy or disturbing effect would be lost. So, our ears are also interested in the frequency domain
envelope of the sound—or how the different frequency components change in time.

All of these lead to a generalization about what we'd like to have in a synthesizer patch, and that ultimately dictates
modules we need for a system.

In general, we need to create sounds that evolve, morph or change in time and frequency in order to create
sonically interesting events. The time domain envelope dictates the amplitudes of the signal during the
event in time, while the frequency domain envelope controls the way frequency components change over
