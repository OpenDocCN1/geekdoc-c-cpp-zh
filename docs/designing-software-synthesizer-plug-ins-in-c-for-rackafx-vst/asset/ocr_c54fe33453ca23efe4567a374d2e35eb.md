# Chapter 10

By the late 1980s, synthesizer companies have switched paradigms from analog circuits to audio samples. A sample is a recorded sound. It is an acoustic instrument, a sound of nature, or even a recording of another synthesizer. In the early days of sample-based synthesis, solid-state memory was expensive, so the samples were typically very short. With today's cheap memory prices, sampling still has issues where sample length is concerned. For example, how long would a sample of a concert piano's note (A1) last if the note was fortissimo (fff) in a concert hall? You would need to sample the note until it decayed the digital noise floor (–96 db for 18-bit audio). In addition, there are many instruments whose envelopes do not decay, as the pipe organ–these events last only as long as the keys are held. The early pioneers came up with an interesting way to get the most from their samples, and we use this method today. DigiSynth is a sample synthesizer that will incorporate the transient-loop method of handling samples in addition to playing loops or one-shot samples. A C++ object we created CWaveData that handles the task of opening a .wav file and extracting its entire audio contents into a 32-bit float buffer. DigiSynth can playback any single or arrays of samples as multi-multiples.

# 10.1 Audio Samples

Let's consider a sample of a typical musical event such as an acoustic instrument's output. Musical notes often consist of a noisy attack followed by a quasi-periodic steady state that may or not decay away; for instance, a plucked string decays quickly, while a bound string can be made to sustain for as long as the musician wishes. The idea is to truncate the audio sample so that it contains the transient attack and piece of the steady-state that is loop-able. By choosing the loops carefully, the quasi-periodic nature of this section can be sustained forever. This transient-loop technique is shown in Figure 10.3.

Applying an envelope to the steady state can mimic the natural decay and release of the original event. Sometimes, the ADHDSR (attack-decay-sustain-release) EG is employed with no attack or a very short followed by a hold period when the transients play. In some cases, there is no sustain section of the envelope, and it moves from decay (or hold) directly to release.

There are three ways to play an audio sample:

+   - one-shot–the sample plays from beginning to end and stops

+   - loop–the sample loops from beginning to end

+   - transient-loop–the sample plays the transient and then goes into the loop section with the envelope applied

A fourth option also exists called loop-and.
