θ(t) is the argument of the trig functions and is called the angle. It is broken into two parts: frequency and phase. The frequency of oscillation is ω, the angular velocity of a rotating phasor and the phase component is φ. Ordinarily the frequency is fixed and not time-varying. Likewise the phase component is constant; it is usually a offset, and we set it to 0.0 for convenience. It represents the phasor's initial starting point. If the frequency and phase are not fixed, but however vary in time, how do you quantify frequency and phase? The answer is with the instantaneous frequency and phase relationship. The instantaneous frequency ω, and phase θ_i are related as:

```
$$\omega_i(t) = \frac{d\theta}{dt}$$\n$$\$\theta(t) = \int \omega_i(t)dt$$ \qquad (12.2)
```

This makes sense—ω_i is the angular velocity and the rate of change of the phase angle in—the time derivative. In both FM and PM, you replace the oscillator with a new instantaneous frequency ω_i. In frequency modulation, you vary the instantaneous frequency and keep the phase constant. In Phase modulation, however the phase also changes the instantaneous frequency. In both cases the frequency of oscillation ω_c is called the carrier frequency.

In FM, the instantaneous frequency is the sum of the carrier frequency and some function f(t), scaled by the frequency deviation Δω. Typically, we set the phase offset Δφ to 0.0 and ignore it. In PM, the carrier frequency remains constant, and its phase is altered by some function f(t), scaled by the phase deviation Δω.

```
$$\f(t) = \sin(\omega_m t)$$\n$\$\Delta\phi = 0$$$\n$\$\omega_i = \omega_c + \Deltaωf(t)$$\n$\$\theta(t) = \int (\omega_c + \Deltaωf(t))dt$$$\n\n$\$\f(t) = \sin(\omega_m t)$$\n$\$\theta(t) = \omega_ct + \Deltaωf(t)$$$ \qquad (12.3)
```

The function that varies the frequency or phase is either sin(ω_m t) or cos(ω_m t), where ω_m is the modulation frequency. This sets up four combinations of sin/cos as carrier/modulator. Suppose we sin(ω_m t) as the carrier and cos(ω_m t) as the modulator. Then you can choose any combination of sin/cos carrier/modulator pairs—the spectra of the resulting signals are the same.

```
$$\FM(t) = \sin(\omega_m t + \Deltaω\cos(\omega_m t))$$\nn$\$\omega_i = \omega_c + \Deltaω\sin(\omega_m t)$$\n\n$$ \qquad (12.4)
```

The cos(t) function outputs values that fluctuate from -1 and +1, so if the carrier frequency is 500 Hz and the frequency deviation Δf is 250 Hz, the instantaneous frequency will vary between 250 Hz and 750 Hz, or fc ± Δf.

```
$$\PM(t) = \sin(\omega_m t + \Deltaω\cos(\omega_m t))$$$ \qquad (12.5)
```

Likewise in PM, if you set the phase deviation to 3.1415 (i.e., π), then the instantaneous phase will vary between ~π and +π radians. In both cases the strength or depth of modulation is controlled with the front term of the modulator. This term is called the modulation index, or index of modulation, and is found:

```
$Index of Modulation$\n$\nFM$\nI = \Deltaf/f$\nPM$\nI = \Deltaω/\omega_m$$ \qquad (12.6)
```
