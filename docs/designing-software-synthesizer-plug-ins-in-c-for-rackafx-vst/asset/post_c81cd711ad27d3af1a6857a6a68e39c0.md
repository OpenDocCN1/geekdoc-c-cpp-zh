
For the LPF
$$y_{LPF} = Gx + S$$
$$G = \frac{g}{1+g} \quad S = \frac{s}{1+g}$$

For the HPF
$$y_{HPF} = x-(Gx+S)$$
$$G = \frac{g}{1+g} \quad S = \frac{s}{1+g}$$

The variables x, y and s in the instantaneous response equations refer to the nodes x(n), y(n) and s(n) in the block diagrams, and v(n) is the intermediate node used to calculate.

## 7.2 Oberheim SEM State Variable Model

Oberheim's Synthesizer Expander Module (SEM) in 1974 featured a modified state variable design. It allowed for three standard outputs for LPF, HPF and BPF, plus a variable BSF that could produce asymmetric resonant bandstop responses in addition to a perfect notch. Not more importantly, it limited a mechanism in the inner feedback path which connects the output of the BP section to the input in a negative feedback loop. In the analog design, the feedback level is controlled with a potentiometer (variable resistor). Therefore, as this feedback signal increases, the overall resonance or Q will decrease. An issue with the SVF design is that when resonance increases, the gain also increases. With the resonance control at a very high setting, this can lead to clipping and distortion, especially at low frequencies. Only a small amount of feedback is applied, and the output of the cascade of sections is greatly amplified. The SEM filter has a switch that turns on when the output of the BP section becomes large enough and bypasses the resonance potentiometer. Figure 7.5(a) shows a simplified circuit version of the SEM. The Operational Amplifier (OTA) and capacitor in each of the boxes implements voltage controlled filtering the tuned integration sections. You can see the similar succession of HPF, BPF and LPF outputs. The BSF output is formed by summing the HPF and LPF outputs. Resistor $R_s$ provides the global negative feedback path from the LPF output back to the input, which is designed using an inverting summer circuit with an input impedance the same as $Rs$. We are particularly interested in the circuit that forms the inner feedback path from the BP output back to the input.

Figure 7.5(b) shows a block diagram with digital integrators replacing the OTA versions. The use of pair diodes D1 and D2, along fed back via an attenuator that is formed by variable R1/R2. When the output at $y_{sim}$ becomes large, the switch of the two diodes turns on and bypasses part of the signal around the feedback control. Resistors R3 and R4 control the mix of the bipolar and straight signals. The threshold is adjusted by the attenuator. This results in a nonlinearity where the diodes do not turn on and off a perfect switch. In addition, each diode is separately engaged for the negative portion (D1) and the positive portion (D2) of the feedback waveform. We can use any number of wave shapes to emulate the diodes and splitter to route portions of the signal around them, a larger problem arises in that the nonlinearity this creates will cause a problem with the design equations of the filter.

Experimentation with several schemes yielded filters with horrible tuning stability and often resonant frequency instability. Avalashin proposes a simple solution by placing the nonlinearity inside of the first integrator. This produces a filter that has good tuning stability but is much an approximation, the term “SEM Model” should be taken lightly. You are certainly encouraged to experiment with this design issue. The block diagram for our SEM emulation is shown in Figure 7.6. A value B is used to mix the HPF and LPF outputs to create the BSF variations.

## 7.3 Korg Sallen-Key Filter Model

Korg used several filter architectures in its early synthesizer designs, the Sallen-Key used in the MS-10 and early MS-20 synths stands out as an interesting and useful design. It has reappeared in several Korg products, including the more modern synths. The term CV actually refers to a sub-circuit where Control Voltage (CV) modifies the cutoff frequency of the main filtering circuit. Here is where we use to include the LPF or HPF circuit. The LPF and HPF are controlled versions of the Sallen-Key filter topology. This is one of the early filter designs that use the SVF; some analog filter books refer to it as "Class 2a (Lindquist)" and "PF1" (Wahhammer). Sallen-Key filters have a positive feedback path in a negative feedback path, and this allows the filter to self-oscillate. Korg took advantage of this design to produce a simple but highly resonant filter. Both LPF and HPF are second order types and will self-oscillate, but the Korg- HPF has an interesting quirk; its roll-off is 6 dB/octave instead of 12 dB/octave. This produces a HPF with more response.

## 7.4 Korg Sallen-Key LPF Model

Figure 7.9 shows the block diagram of the two-amplifier Sallen-Key LPF. Two LPFs are formed by the cascade of R1/C1 and R2/C2. K=K1=K2 is the strength of the amplifier blocks, and we can see the output is fed back into the filter via capacitor C1.

The reason for choosing the two-amplifier version is that the amps buffer the LPF stages to prevent loading. Our digital filters do not suffer from impedance loading, so we use this version. The original Korg analog version uses the single amplifier circuit. The difference in the two designs boils down to the self-oscillation gain value; you can find a complete derivation and implementation of the lossy single-amplifier design at http://willinkle.com/synthbook/. Its frequency response is identical to this but requires more complex design equations to evaluate the loss sections. Using signal flow, we can derive the block diagram and signal flow of the filter shown in Figure 7.11. In the signal graph, "LPF and HPF*" mean first order filters. In this design, we let K1 =1.0, neglect the effects of loading and use an ideal version of the original single amplifier design that Korg implemented.
