## 6. Numerical investigations

### 6.1. Simulation setting

In this section, we compare the caching allocation derived from the set of proposed schemes against some well known previously proposed techniques such as the Random Caching and the Coordinated Caching schemes, which have been introduced in [52] and [14] respectively. The proposed set of techniques include the ILP optimization model (which is viewed as offering the optimal solution in this paper by using the branch-and-bound method, hence in the figures to follow we use term Optimal to represent the results of the ILP model) and the GSAC technique. Random Caching can be deemed as a technique that can be easily adopted and used by operators where hosting nodes are chosen randomly for the requested content. Based on the caching capacity, the Coordinated Caching places content in local or nearby caching nodes, and, thus, balances the energy consumption between data transmission and storage.

The experiments are performed in a 20 caching nodes and 52 links network model, which captures a nominal tree-like configuration of a mobile edge network. Without loss of generality, we assume that all links have equal capacity and all caching nodes have identical memory space, but the actual values vary to investigate the trade-off between bandwidth and storage (caching memory capacity). Accordingly, we investigate the trade-off between caching memory capacity and 80/20 rule, i.e., approximate 80% of user requests concentrate on the top 20% content categories. This rule has been widely used in various fields including caching [38]. The caching allocation based on such prediction and the impact of predicted accuracy is also discussed.

The performance is evaluated mainly via two key metrics: the energy gain and cache-hit ratio. As for the energy gain, we use the so-called No-caching approach as the benchmark, which, in essence, means we do not host any content in caching nodes, and each flow request should be transferred to a core network content server. In that case, the gain is calculated by

\(Gain = \frac{E(\chi_{s_{no\_snap}})}{E(\chi_{s_{snap}})}\)
where \(E(\chi_{s_{no\_snap}})\) is the energy cost by applying No-caching strategy, and \(E(\chi_{s_{snap}})\) represents the energy consumption by using estimated algorithms, i.e. the Optimal, the GSAC, the Coordinated Caching and the Random Caching. As a result, a larger energy gain indicates a better energy-saving performance. Furthermore, the cache-hit ratio is defined as the percentage of content requests served by caching nodes.

All results presented hereafter are averaged over one hundred network scenarios (i.e., Monte Carlo simulations) and based on those we analyse the performance of different schemes. To put the computational times in context we note that all simulations performed using MATLAB R2021b running on top of a 64-bit Windows 10 environment, whilst the machine is equipped with an Intel Core i9-10900K CPU with 64 GB RAM. The simulation parameters assumed in the investigations are presented and summarized in Table 5. Unless stated otherwise, the values of \(\kappa_s\), \(\kappa_{rs}\), PL\(_{max}\) and \(\sum_m a_m\) are set with their default values.

### 6.2. Impact of the number of requests

We first study how the number of content requests affects the performance of proposed schemes, in which case we set \(\sum_m a_m\) to vary from 200 to 2000. As Fig. 3 shows, the evaluation is among energy gain, cache-hit ratio, time complexity of running each algorithm, and time for proactively pushing contents to caching nodes. Moreover, the confidence interval of each simulation case is added on the plot. In Fig. 3(a), both the Optimal and GSAC on (P1) share a climbing-remaining-dropping trend while the Random has a decreasing trend. With the increase in user requests, as expected, the energy consumption for caching service is increasing accordingly, and this trend is independent if we utilize the Optimal scheme, the GSAC, the Coordinated, and the No-caching scheme. However, the performance plays a significant role in the overall energy gains: (1) an increase (i.e., positive slope) represents the No-caching scheme grows at a faster rate than the evaluated algorithm; (2) a plateau (i.e., zero slope) shows that both the No-caching and the evaluated algorithm have similar increasing rates; and (3) a decrease (i.e., negative slope) indicates that the No-caching grows slower than the evaluated algorithm.

In Fig. 3(a), when the number of requests ranges from 200 to 500, the network has sufficient resources (i.e. caching space and link bandwidth) to serve end-users, and the Optimal together with GSAC can both significantly outperform the No-caching scheme in reducing energy consumption. The energy gain ranges from 302 to 317 for the Optimal and 2.93 to 2.99 for the GSAC scheme. Then passing that state, the energy gain starts to decrease gradually while the energy ranges between 500 and 1400, which reflects a plateau on the energy gain of the Optimal curve. For the GSAC method, the plateau ends at 1100 requests. After the turning points (i.e. 1400-request for the Optimal and 1100-requests for the GSAC), more users' flows are redirected to a core network server, hence the performance resembles more to the No-caching scheme. Therefore, the energy gain is reduced to 2.70 for the Optimal and 2.50 for GSAC. Regarding Coordinated Caching, the energy gain performs similarly as the Optimal when there are 200 requests, and then decreases continuously, especially after 800 requests. This is expected by the fact that the Coordinated Caching neglects the bandwidth limitation. Therefore, once the connected link is congested, the performance of Coordinated Caching degrades.

For Random Caching, observe the gradual decline on the energy gain from 1.92 to 1.29 in the simulation, which indicates this method cannot deal with the increasing requests in a flexible manner. Finally, it is worth noting that the energy gain in Fig. 3(a) is above 1, so all evaluated algorithms can save energy compared to the No-caching approach.

Fig. 3(b) reveals that there is a continuous decrease in the cache-hit ratio as the number of requests increases. Generally, the Optimal and GSAC guarantee that the majority of requests (over 90% in the simulation) can be satisfied on the caching nodes. Nearly one-fifth of the flows are redirected to a core network server by applying Coordinated Caching in the 2000-request case, while the ratio for Random Caching is almost half. Note that, while the ratio for Optimal and GSAC have marked decreased after 1400-request and 1100-request respectively, which match the turning points in Fig. 3(a).

A similar pattern still holds on (P2) as shown in Figs. 3.(c) and 3.(d). However, in (P2) the energy gain of the Optimal keeps rising beyond the 500-request point. The reason is that in (P2) each flow is served independently, even if the requested content is in essence the same. Thus, the No-caching algorithm consumes more energy than in (P1). Although the Optimal energy is also increasing, it is at a similar rate as the No-caching, which reflects a plateau when the number of requests ranges from 800 to 1700. Fig. 3.(c) reports the performance of GSAC and Coordinated Caching that decreases continuously. In contrast to the decreasing trend in Fig. 3.(a), the energy gain is localized around 1.00 of GSAC should be the last instead of network resource limitation. Because the Optimal solution keeps steady which indicates the better allocation exists but GSAC cannot find it. Similar to (P1), the gain of Random Caching keeps decreasing with increasing number of requests. Compared with Random Caching, the Optimal can save around 75% energy in the 1100-request case, which is also the maximum attained performance gain in the simulations. Interestingly, the actual influence of the different delivery modes on energy gain and cache-hit ratio vary: on the one hand, unicast mode (P2) generates more traffic flows which make the energy gain higher than (P1) comparing 3.(a) and 3.(c) vertically; on the other hand, more flows exhaust the network resource rapidly and, thus, the cache-hit ratio in 3.(d) is less than 3.(b).

The running time of (P1) and (P2) are evaluated in Figs. 3.(e) and 3.(f) respectively. The time for Optimal solution varies from around 10 minutes to 4 h, while Coordinated Caching and the proposed heuristic algorithm GSAC are less than 4.5 s and 4 s respectively. Random Caching is the fastest among the evaluated algorithms, which requires less than 100 milliseconds. The time for performing proactive caching, i.e. pushing content from the data server to the caching candidates according to the four estimated algorithms, are also presented in 3.(g) and (h) respectively. Both figures reveals that there is a gradual increase in the content pushing time with the growth of user requests. Compared to the other algorithms, the GSAC scheme has more caching items to allocate. Therefore, the pushing time of the red curve is higher than the other.

In Fig. 5 the Optimal outperforms the other methods in both (P1) and (P2) problems, albeit with a higher time complexity cost. The heuristic algorithm GSAC is able to provide a competitive result, followed by Coordinated and Random schemes. For instance, compared with the Optimal allocation, in (P1) GSAC saves 99.9% running time with the cost of approximate 6.0% performance loss when there are 1100 requests. Under the same scenario, the time-saving ratio for Coordinated Caching and Random Caching is similar to the GSAC scheme; but the performance reductions are 12.2% and 54.9% respectively.

### 6.3. Impact of the available network capacity (in terms of bandwidth and storage)

To further appreciate the underlying trade-off between the bandwidth and caching space, the values of the parameters $w_c$ and $c_l$ are varied from 0.25 to 1 with a sampling interval of 0.25. This setting aims to shed light on the following key question: when the memory space and link capacity are insufficient to support all content requests, to further improve the performance (i.e. energy gain and cache-hit ratio), should we increase the link capacity first or the caching space?

In Fig. 4 we test the performance of the different algorithms over (P1) and (P2). The contour lines with labels represent the energy gain or cache-hit ratio accordingly. Moreover, the blue arrows depict the gradient, which can be viewed as the directionality towards optimized parameterization. In Fig. 4.(a), when both the bandwidth and memory are less than 0.5, either increasing the caching space or expanding the link capacity results in an energy gain growth. However, once the link capacity exceeds 0.5 GB, the energy gain increases when link bandwidth is greater than the memory, as shown in Fig. 4.(a) that the arrow points towards the bandwidth increasing direction. To some extent, enlarging the caching memory implies more energy consumption for hosting content. A similar trend holds on Fig. 4.(b), 0.5 GB works as a watershed: before 0.5 GB both bandwidth and caching memory make contributions to the energy gain; after 0.5 GB the bandwidth dominates the performance improvement. When it comes to (P2), the tendencies of optimal 4.(i) and GSAC 4.(j) are similar with (P1). What is striking in Fig. 4.(c) is that all arrows points towards the bandwidth increasing direction, which indicates that expanding link capacity has a distinct effect on the energy gain improvement while the caching space has a negligible effect on the energy gain improvement. The reason for its insensitivity on caching memory is that Coordinated Caching neglects the link bandwidth limitation. In other words, Coordinated Caching provides an optimal caching allocation when the link capacity is infinite. Therefore, a larger bandwidth is closer to the Coordinated Caching assumption. This reason can also explain why the gradient arrows in Figs. 4.(g), 4.(k), and 4.(c) towards right. Similarly, as shown in Figs. 4.(d), 4.(h), and 4.(p), the performance of Random Caching is very sensitive to the bandwidth change but sluggish to the caching memory.
