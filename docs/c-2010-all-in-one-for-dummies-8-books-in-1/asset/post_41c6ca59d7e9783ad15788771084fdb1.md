
## 204 Grabbing Your Last Chance to Catch an Exception

Any exception not caught somewhere else percolates up to Main(). This is your last opportunity to grab the error before it ends up in Windows, where the error message is much harder to penetrate and frustrate — or scare the beggars out — the program's users.

All the serious code in FactorialException's Main() is inside a try block. The associated catch block catches any exception whatsoever and outputs a message to the console, and the application exits.

This catch block serves to prevent hard crashes by intercepting exceptions not handled elsewhere. And it's your chance to explain why the application is quitting.

Experiment. To see why you need this last-chance handler, deliberately throw an exception in a little program without handling it. You see the user would without your efforts to make the landing a bit softer.

![](img/36596fb11aa4233d5cab0d6ddd1d82e8_229_0.png)

During the development, you want to see exceptions that as you test the code, in their natural habitat — so you want all of the geekspeak. In the version release, convert the programmerish to normal English, display the message to the user, including, if possible, what he might do to run successfully next time, and exit stage. This plain-English version of the exception handler is one of the chores you complete before you release your program into the world.

Your last-chance handler should certainly log the exception information locally, for forensic analysis.

## Chapter 1: Object-Oriented Programming: What It All About?

### In This Chapter

- Reviewing the basics of object-oriented programming
- Getting a handle on abstraction and classification
- Understanding why object-oriented programming is important

This chapter answers the two-pronged question: “What are the concepts behind object-oriented programming, and how do they differ from the procedural concepts covered in Book I?”

### Object-Oriented Concept #1: Abstraction

Sometimes, when my son and I are watching football, I whip up a terribly batch of nachos. I dump chips on a plate, throw on some cheese and lots of jalapeños, and nuke the whole mess for a few minutes. To use the microwave, I open the door, throw in the food, and punch a few buttons on the front. After a few minutes, the nachos are done. (I try not to stand in front of the microwave while it's working, lest my eyes start glowing in the dark.)

Now think for a minute about all the things I do in order to use the microwave:
- Rewire or change anything to get it to work. The microwave has an interface — the front panel with all the buttons and time display — lets me do everything I need.
- Reprogram the software to drive the little processor inside even if I cooked a different dish last time.
- Look inside the microwave's case.

### Abstraction

Even if I were a microwave designer and knew all about the workings of a microwave, including its software, I still wouldn't think about all those concepts while using it to heat nachos.

![](img/36596fb11aa4233d5cab0d6ddd1d82e8_233_0.png)

These observations aren't profound: You can deal with so much stress in life. To reduce the number of issues you deal with, work at a certain level of detail. In object-oriented (OO) computerese, the level of detail at which you are working is the level of abstraction. To introduce any OO term, I abstract away the details of the microwave.

Happily, computer scientists — thousands decades invented object orientation and other concepts that reduce the complexity at which programmers have to work. Using powerful abstractions makes the job simpler and far less prone than it used to be. In a sense, that is what the last half century or so of computing has been about: managing more complex concepts and structures with errors.

When I am making nachos, I view the microwave oven as a box. While I am trying to knock out a snack, as long as I use the microwave only by its interface (the keypad), nothing I do should cause the microwave to enter an inconsistent state and crash or, worst, turn my house into a blackened mass.

Suppose that I ask my son to write an algorithm for how to make nachos. After he understands what I want, he can write, "Open a can of beans, grate some cheese, the jalapeños," and so on. When he reaches the part of microwaving, he might write something like: "Cook in the microwave for five minutes." In that world of procedural programming, you can't easily think in terms of abstraction. You have no objects and no abstractions which hide inherent complexity.

### Object-Oriented Concept #2: Classification

### Preparing object-oriented nachos

In an object-oriented approach to making nachos, you first identify the types of objects in the problem: chips, beans, cheese, jalapeños, and an oven. Then you begin the task of modeling those objects in software, without regard for the details of how they might be used in the final program. For example, you can model cheese as an object in isolation from the other objects and combine it with the beans, the cheese, the jalapeños, and the oven and make them interact. (And you might decide that some of these objects don't need to be objects in software: cheese, for instance.)

While you do, you're said to be working (and thinking) at the level of the basic objects. You need to think about a useful oven, but you don't have to think about the logical process of making nachos—you. After all, the microwave designers didn't think about the specific problem of making a snack. Rather, they set about solving the problem of designing and building a useful microwave.

After you successfully code and test the objects you need, you can ratchet up to the next level of abstraction and start thinking at the nachommaking level rather than at the microwave-making level.

(And, at this point, I can translate my son's instructions directly into C# code.)

### Classification

Critical to the concept of abstraction is that of classification. If I were to ask my son, "What's a microwave?" he might say, "It's an oven..." If I then ask, "What's an oven?" he would reply "It's a kitchen appliance..." If he would probably say "Why are you asking so stupid questions?"

The answers my son might give stem from his understanding of this particular oven as an example of the type item known as a microwave oven. In addition, he might see a microwave oven as just a special type of oven, which itself is just a special type of kitchen appliance.

In object-oriented computerese, the microwave is an instance of the class microwave. The class microwave is a subclass of the class oven, and the class oven is a subclass of the class kitchen appliance.

Humans classify. Everything about our world is ordered into taxonomies. We do this to reduce the number of items we have to remember.
