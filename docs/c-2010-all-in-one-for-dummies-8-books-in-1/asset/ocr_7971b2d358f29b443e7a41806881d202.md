In inheritance, a class "adopts" the members of another. Thus I can create a class SavingsAccount that inherits data members as account id and methods such as Deposit() from a base class BankAccount. That's useful, but this definition of inheritance isn't sufficient to mimic what's going out there in the business world.

See Chapter 6 of this minibook if you don't know (or remember) much about class inheritance.

A microwave is a type of oven, not because it looks like an oven but, rather, because it performs the same functions as an oven. A microwave may perform additional functions, but, at the least, the base functions — most importantly, heating when I say, "StartCooking." (I rely on my object class Refrigerator to cool the beer.) I don't particularly care what the oven must do internally to make that happen, more than I care what type of oven it is, who made it, or whether it was on sale when my wife bought it. — Wait, I do care about that one.

From our human vantage point, the relationship between a microwave and a conventional oven doesn't seem like such a deal, but consider the problem from the oven's point of view. The steps that a conventional oven performs are completely different from those that a microwave may take.

![](bbox=[0.133, 0.837, 0.193, 0.907])

The power of inheritance lies in the fact that a subclass doesn't have to inherit every single method from the base class just the way it's written. A subclass can inherit the essence of the base class method implementing the details differently.
