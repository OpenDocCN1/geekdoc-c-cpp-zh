# C# During Its Abstract Period

The duck is a type of bird, I think. So are the cardinal and the hummingbird, in fact, every bird out there is a subtype of bird. The flip side of argument is that there is no bird that isn't some subtype of Bird. That statement doesn't sound profound, but in a way, it is. The software equivalent of statement is that all bird objects are instances of subclass of Bird — there's never an instance of class Bird. What's a bird? It's like a robin or a grackle or another specific species.

Different types of birds share many properties (otherwise, they wouldn't be birds), yet no two types share every property. If they did, they wouldn't be different types. To pick a particularly large example, not all birds Fly() the same way. Ducks the cardinal's style is similar but not identical. (Don't get me started about emus and ostriches or the rubber duck in your tub.)

But if not birds fly the same way and there's no such thing as a Bird, what the heck is Bird.Fly()? The subject of the following sections, is what it is.

# UML Lite

The Unified Modeling Language (UML) is an expressive language that's capable of clearly defining a great deal about the relationships of objects within a program. One advantage of UML is that you can ignore the more specific language features without losing meaning entirely.

The most basic features of UML are described in this list:

- Classes are represented by a box divided vertically into three sections. The member of the class appears in the uppermost section.
- The members of the class appear in the middle section, and the methods of the class in the bottom. You can omit the middle or bottom section if the class has no data members or if you want a high-level-classes-only view.
- Members with a plus (+) in front are public; with a minus (-) are private. To protected and internal visibility, you must use the pound sign (#) or should say the "Sharp sign” and the title (,,) respectively.

A private member is accessible only from other members of the class. A public member is accessible to all classes. See Chapter 5.

- The label *abstract* next to a name indicates an abstract class or method.

UML uses a different symbol for an abstract method, but I keep it simple. (It's *UML.*) You can also show abstract items in italics.

- An arrow between two classes represents a relationship between the two classes. A number above the line expresses the cardinality—the cardinality is assumed to be 1 if you have each of end. The asterisk symbol (*) means any. If no number is present, you can see that a single university has any number of students—one-to-many relationship (refer to Figure 7-1).

- A line with a large, open arrowhead, or a triangular arrowhead, represents the IS. A relationship (inheritance). The arrow points up the hierarchy to the base class. Other types of relationships include the HAS_A relationship (line with a filled diamond at the ending end).

To explore UML in depth, check *UML 2 For Dummies*, by Michael Jesse Choles and James A. Schardt.

How can you avoid these problems? Not going to school is one way, but another is to fix the source of the problem: A university isn't a particular type of high school. A relationship exists between the two, but IS_A isn't the right one. (HAS_A doesn't work. A university HAS_A high school? A high school HAS_A university? Come on! Instead, high schools and universities are special types of schools. What do they have in common.

Figure 7-3 describes a relationship. The newly defined School contains the common properties of both types of schools, including the relationship they have with Student objects. School contains the common Enroll() method, although it's abstract because HighSchool and University usually don't implement Enroll() the same way.

The classes HighSchool and University now inherit from a common base class. Each contains its unique members: NameFavorite() in the case of HighSchool, and Get() for the University. In addition, both classes override the Enroll() method with a version that describes how that of school enroll students. In fact, I've extracted a superclass, or base class, from two similar classes, which now become subclasses.
