# 208 Object-Oriented Concept #1: Abstraction

Even if I were a microwave designer and knew all about the inner workings of a microwave, including its software, I still wouldn't think about all those concepts while using it to heat nachos.

![](bbox=[0.153, 0.198, 0.215, 0.263])

These observations aren't profound: You can deal with so much stress in your life. To reduce the number of issues you deal with, you work at a certain level of detail. In object-oriented (OO) computerese, the level of detail at which you're working is the level of abstraction. To introduce any OO term while I have the chance, I abstract away the details of the microwave's innards.

Happily, computer scientists — thousands of decades — invented object orientation and other concepts that reduce the level of complexity at which programmers have to work. Using powerful abstractions makes the job simpler and far less-prone than it used to be. In a sense, that's what the last half-century or so of computing progress has been about: managing ever more complex concepts and structures with ever errors.

When I'm on nachos, I view my microwave oven as a box. (While I'm trying to knock out a snack, I can't worry about the innards of the microwave-wave and still follow the Dallas Cowboys on) As long as I use the microwave only by way of its interface (the keypad), nothing can I do should cause the microwave to enter an inconsistent state and crash or, worst, turn my — or my house — into a blackened, flaming mass.



Suppose that I ask my to write an algorithm for how to make nachos. After he understands what I want, he can write, "Open a can of beans, grate some cheese, the jalapeños," and so on. When he reaches the part of microwaving the convection, he might write (on a good day) something like: "Cook in the microwave for five minutes."

In that world of procedural programming, you can't easily think in terms of levels of abstraction. You have no objects and no abstractions which hide inherent complexity.
