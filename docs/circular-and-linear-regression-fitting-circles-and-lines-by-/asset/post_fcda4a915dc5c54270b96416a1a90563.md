### Taylor expansions

The asymptotic properties of the estimates $\hat{A}$, $\hat{B}$, $\hat{C}$, to the leading order, can now be obtained easily from those of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ by using a Taylor approximation. We demonstrate this in the context of the Type A model.

First, $A = \sin \boldsymbol{\theta}$ and $B = -\cos \boldsymbol{\theta}$, hence using Taylor expansion up to the third order terms, we get

$A = \bar{A} - \bar{B}\, \delta\boldsymbol{\theta} - \frac{1}{2}\,\bar{A}\,(\delta\boldsymbol{\theta})^2 + \frac{1}{6}\bar{B}\,(\delta\boldsymbol{\theta})^3 + \mathcal{O}\bigl((\delta\boldsymbol{\theta})^4\bigr)$

$B = \bar{B} + \bar{A}\, \delta\boldsymbol{\theta} - \frac{1}{2}\,\bar{B}\,(\delta\boldsymbol{\theta})^2 - \frac{1}{6}\bar{A}\,(\delta\boldsymbol{\theta})^3 + \mathcal{O}\bigl((\delta\boldsymbol{\theta})^4\bigr)$.

### Means and variances of $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$

Taking mean values (recall that $\mathbb{E}(\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}(\delta\boldsymbol{\theta})^3 = 0$) gives

$\mathbb{E}(\hat{A}) = \hat{A}\biggl[1 - \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2}{2S}\biggr] + \mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^4)$,

$\mathbb{E}(\hat{B}) = \hat{B}\biggl[1 - \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2}{2S}\biggr] + \mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^4)$,

i.e., the estimates $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ are biased toward zero. Taking variances gives

$\mathrm{Var}\,\hat{A} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2\hat{B}^2}{S} + \mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^4)$,

$\mathrm{Var}\,\hat{B} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2\hat{A}^2}{S} + \mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^4)$,

$\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{A}, \hat{B}) = -\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2\hat{A}\hat{B}}{S} + \mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^4)$.

Note that the standard deviation of every estimate is $\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})$, while its bias is $\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2)$; hence the bias is of higher order of smallness. In other words, the bias is negligible when we, for instance, assess the mean squared error of an estimate, say

$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{A}) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(\hat{A} - \bar{A}\bigr)^2\bigr] = \mathrm{Var}(\hat{A}) + \bigl[\mathbb{E}(\hat{A}) - \bar{A}\bigr]^2 = \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2\hat{B}^2}{S} + \mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^4)$,

so the bias is simply absorbed by the $\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^4)$ term.

### Mean and variance of $\hat{C}$

Lastly, by using $\hat{C} = -\hat{A}\bar{x} - \hat{B}\bar{y}$ one obtains

$\mathbb{E}(\hat{C}) = \hat{C}\biggl[1 - \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2}{2S}\biggr] + \mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^4)$,

and

$\mathrm{Var}\,\hat{C} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}^2\biggl[\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bigl(\hat{A}\bar{y} - \hat{B}\bar{x}\bigr)^2}{S}\biggr] + \mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^4)$.

where
$$
\bar{\bar{x}} = \sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{x}_i \quad \text{and} \quad \bar{\bar{y}} = \sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{y}_i
$$
are the sample means of the true values. The extra term $\frac{1}{n}$ in (2.26) comes from the fact that $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ (the sample means of the observations) are random variables correlated with $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$.

### Covariances

For the sake of completeness, we include three more covariances
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{A}, \hat{C}) &= \frac{\sigma^2 \bar{B}(A\bar{\bar{y}} - \bar{B}\bar{\bar{x}})}{S} + \mathscr{O}(\sigma^4)\\
\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{B}, \hat{C}) &= -\frac{\sigma^2 \bar{A}(A\bar{\bar{y}} - \bar{B}\bar{\bar{x}})}{S} + \mathscr{O}(\sigma^4)\\
\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\theta}, \hat{C}) &= -\frac{\sigma^2(A\bar{\bar{y}} - \bar{B}\bar{\bar{x}})}{S} + \mathscr{O}(\sigma^4).
\end{aligned}
$$

### Practical remarks

In practice, the true values that appear in the above formulas are not available. Normally, one makes further approximation replacing the true points with the observed ones and the true parameter values with their estimates (this does not alter the above expressions, the resulting errors are absorbed by the $\mathscr{O}(\sigma^4)$ terms). In the same fashion, one estimates $S$ by
$$
\hat{S} = s_{xx} + s_{yy}
$$
and $\sigma^2$ by
$$
\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{A}x_i + \hat{B}y_i + \hat{C})^2. \tag{2.27}
$$
Gleser [73] and Patefield [143] show that all these estimates are strongly consistent, thus their use in the construction of confidence intervals is justified.

## 2.7 Approximate analysis

**Taylor approximation versus rigorous proofs.** In the previous section, we used Taylor expansion to derive formulas for the variances and covariances of all important estimates based on the second order approximation (2.25) to the variance $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})$, whose proof was left “behind the scene” (we simply referred to the exact results by Anderson [7] and Kunitomo [118]).
In this section we derive (2.25) itself by Taylor approximation without using the exact results. Thus we demonstrate that Taylor expansion, if properly used, is a powerful (albeit not rigorous) tool, which allows one to obtain correct approximations even including higher order terms. This will help us in the studies of nonlinear regression later, in particular circles and ellipses.

### Matrix approximations

We use notation of Section 2.3 and focus on perturbations $\delta\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A} - \tilde{\mathbf{A}}$, $\delta\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{S} - \tilde{\mathbf{S}}$, etc., where the tilde is used to denote the true values. The orthogonal fit minimizes

$\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A} = (\tilde{\mathbf{A}} + \delta\mathbf{A})^T(\tilde{\mathbf{X}} + \delta\mathbf{X})^T(\tilde{\mathbf{X}} + \delta\mathbf{X})(\tilde{\mathbf{A}} + \delta\mathbf{A})$.

Expanding, using the fact $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = 0$, which follows from (2.15), and keeping only statistically significant terms³ gives

$\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{A}) = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^T\delta\mathbf{X}^T\delta\mathbf{X}\tilde{\mathbf{A}} + \delta\mathbf{A}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\delta\mathbf{A} + 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^T\delta\mathbf{X}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\delta\mathbf{A} + 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^T\delta\mathbf{X}^T\delta\mathbf{X}\delta\mathbf{A}$.

Now the minimum of $\mathscr{F}$ is attained at $\partial \mathscr{F} / \partial \mathbf{A} = 0$, i.e., at

$\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\delta\mathbf{A} + (\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\delta\mathbf{X} + \delta\mathbf{X}^T\delta\mathbf{X})\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = 0$,

hence

$\delta\mathbf{A} = -(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}})^-(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\delta\mathbf{X} + \delta\mathbf{X}^T\delta\mathbf{X})\tilde{\mathbf{A}}, \quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad(2.28)$

where $(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}})^-$ denotes the Moore-Penrose generalized inverse of the matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}}$ (which is rank deficient because $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = 0$).

### A convenient assumption

Since the distribution of $\delta\boldsymbol{\theta} = \boldsymbol{\theta} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ does not depend on $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ (Section 2.4), we can simplify our calculation by assuming that the line is horizontal, i.e., $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = (0,1)^T$. Then $\delta\mathbf{A} = (\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}, 0)^T$, to the leading order, and

$\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \begin{bmatrix} S & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{hence} \quad (\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}})^- = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{S} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

The matrix $\delta\mathbf{X}$ is

$\begin{bmatrix} \delta_1 - \bar{\delta} & \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_1 - \bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ \delta_n - \bar{\delta} & \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_n - \bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \end{bmatrix}$,

where we continue using the sample mean notation, i.e., $\bar{\delta} = \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_i$, etc. Now (2.28) takes form

$\delta\boldsymbol{\theta} = -\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^n(\bar{x}_i - \bar{\bar{x}})(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}) - \frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^n(\delta_i - \bar{\delta})(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})$.

Using our assumptions on the errors $\delta_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_i$ we easily compute that $\mathbb{E}(\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}) = 0$ and

$\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^n(\bar{x}_i - \bar{\bar{x}})(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})\right]^2 = \sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n(\bar{x}_i - \bar{\bar{x}})^2 = \sigma^2 S$,
