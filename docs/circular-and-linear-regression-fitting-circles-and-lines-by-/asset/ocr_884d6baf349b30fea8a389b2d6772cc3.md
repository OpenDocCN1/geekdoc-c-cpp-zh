**SVD-based Hyper fit.** A numerically stable implementation of the Hyper fit is very similar to the SVD-based Pratt fit developed in Section 5.5. First, one computes the (short) SVD, $\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{T}$, of the matrix $\mathbf{X}$. If its smallest singular value, $\sigma_{4}$, is less than a predefined tolerance $\epsilon$ (say, $\epsilon=10^{-12}$), then the solution $\mathbf{A}$ is simply the corresponding right singular vector (i.e., the fourth column of the $\mathbf{V}$ matrix). In the regular case ($\sigma_{4} \geq \epsilon$), one forms $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{V}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^{T}$ and finds the eigenpairs of the symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Y H}^{*-1}\mathbf{Y}$. Selecting the eigenpair $\left(\eta,\mathbf{A}_{*}\right)$ with the smallest positive eigenvalue and computing $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Y}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{*}$ completes the solution. The corresponding MATLAB$^{\circled{R}}$ code is available from [84].

**An improved Hyper fit.** Rangarajan and Kanatani [152] note that one can eliminate the entire $\widehat{O}\left(\sigma^{2}\right)$ bias by adopting the constraint matrix $\mathbf{N} = \mathbf{H}^{*}$ given by (7.40); this fact is clear from (7.39). The matrix $\mathbf{H}^{*}$ is computationally more complex than our $\mathbf{H}$ (it requires finding the pseudo-inverse of $\mathbf{M}$), but numerical experiments [152] show that the accuracy of the resulting fit is higher than that of our Hyper fit for small $n$ (such as $n = 10$ or $n = 20$); for larger $n$'s there is apparently no difference in accuracy.

# 7.6 Algebraic circle fits in natural parameters

Our next goal is to express the covariance and the essential bias of algebraic circle fits in terms of the more natural geometric parameters $(a,b, R)$.

**Transition between parameter schemes.** The conversion formulas between the algebraic circle parameters $(A,B,C,D)$ and its geometric characteristics $(a,b, R)$, see (3.11), are

```
$a = -\frac{B}{2A}$, $b = -\frac{C}{2A}$, $R^2 = \frac{B^2 + C^2 - 4AD}{4A^2}$. (7.46)
```

Taking partial derivatives gives a $3 \times 4$ "Jacobian" matrix

```
$\mathbf{J} = \left[\begin{array}{cccc} \frac{B}{2A^2} & -\frac{1}{2A} & 0 & 0 \\ \frac{C}{2A^2} & 0 & -\frac{1}{2A} & 0 \\ -\frac{R}{A} - \frac{D}{2A^2R} & \frac{B}{4A^2R} & \frac{C}{4A^2R} & -\frac{1}{2AR} \end{array}\right]$.
```

Thus we have

```
$\Delta_{1}\Theta = \ddot{\mathbf{J}}\Delta\mathbf{A}$ and $\Delta_{2}\Theta = \ddot{\mathbf{J}}\Delta\mathbf{2A} + \widetilde{O}_{P}\left(\sigma^{2} / n\right)$, (7.47)
```

where $\ddot{\mathbf{J}}$ denotes the matrix $\mathbf{J}$ at the true parameters $\left(\bar{A}, \bar{B}, \bar{C}, \bar{D}\right)$. The remainder term $\widetilde{O}_{P}\left(\sigma^{2} / n\right)$ comes from the second order partial derivatives of $(a, b, R)$ with respect to $(A, B, C, D)$. For example

```
$\Delta_{2} a = (\nabla a)^{T}(\Delta_{2}\mathbf{A}) + \frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{1}\mathbf{A}\right)^{T}\left(\nabla^{2} a\right)\left(\Delta_{1}\mathbf{A}\right)$, (7.48)
```
