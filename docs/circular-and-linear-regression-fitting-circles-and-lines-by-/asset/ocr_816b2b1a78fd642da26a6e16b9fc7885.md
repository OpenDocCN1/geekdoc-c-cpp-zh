**Experiment.** For each $\alpha = 360^\circ, 350^\circ, \dots, 30^\circ$ we have generated $10^6$ random samples of $n = 20$ points located (equally spaced) on an arc of angle $\alpha$ of the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ and corrupted by Gaussian noise at level $\sigma = 0.05$. We started with a full circle ($\alpha = 360^\circ$) and went down to rather small arcs ($\alpha = 30^\circ$).

For each random sample we found the best fitting circle by three methods: Kåsa, Pratt, and a geometric fit (Levenberg-Marquardt). Each method gives $10^6$ estimates of the radius $R$, which we ordered and recorded the three quartiles: $q_1$, $q_2$ (the median), and $q_3$. These three characteristics roughly represent the overall performance of the method; in particular, the interval $(q_1, q_3)$ contains $50\%$ of all the radius estimates. The results are summarized in Table 5.4.

We see that all three methods perform nearly identically on large arcs, over the range $150^\circ \le \alpha \le 360^\circ$. A notable feature is a slight bias toward larger circles (the median always exceeds the true value $R = 1$ by a narrow margin), this phenomenon will be explained in Chapters 6 and 7.

On arcs below $150^\circ$, the Kåsa method starts sliding down, as it consistently underestimates the radius. This tendency becomes disatrous on arcs between $90^\circ$ and $45^\circ$, as all the three quartiles fall sharply, and for arcs under $45^\circ$ the Kåsa method returns absurdly small circles.
