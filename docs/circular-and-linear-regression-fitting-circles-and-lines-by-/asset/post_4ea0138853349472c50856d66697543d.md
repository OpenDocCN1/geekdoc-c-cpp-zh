
symmetric matrix, we have $\mathbf{H}_m = \mathbf{Q}_m^T \mathbf{D}_m \mathbf{Q}_m$, where $\mathbf{Q}_m$ is an orthogonal matrix and $\mathbf{D}_m = \text{diag}\{d_1, \ldots, d_{2n}\}$ is a diagonal matrix. The vector $\mathbf{E}_m = \mathbf{Q}_m \mathbf{E}$ has the same distribution as $\mathbf{E}$ does, i.e., the components of $\mathbf{E}_m$ are i.i.d. normal random variables with mean zero and variance $\sigma^2$. Thus

$$
\mathbf{E}^T \mathbf{H}_m \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}^T \mathbf{D}_m \mathbf{E}_m = \sigma^2 \sum d_i Z_i^2, \qquad (6.21)
$$

where the $Z_i$'s are i.i.d. standard normal random variables. One can also regard (6.21) as a generalization of the $\chi^2$ distribution; though it is not always positive, as we may have $d_i < 0$ for some $i$'s. In any case, the mean value of (6.21) is

$$
\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{E}^T \mathbf{H}_m \mathbf{E}\right) = \sigma^2 \text{tr}\mathbf{D}_m = \sigma^2 \text{tr}\mathbf{H}_m.
$$

Therefore, taking the mean value in (6.20) gives the bias of $\hat{\theta}_m$:

$$
\mathbb{E}(\Delta \hat{\theta}_m) = \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \text{tr}\mathbf{H}_m + \mathscr{O}(\sigma^4). \qquad (6.22)
$$

We use the fact that the expectations of all third order terms vanish, because the components of $\mathbf{E}$ are independent and their first and third moments are zero; thus the remainder term is of order $\sigma^4$. In fact the expectations of all *odd* order terms vanish, because the distribution of the components of $\mathbf{E}$ is symmetric about zero.

Next, squaring (6.20) and again using (6.21) give the mean squared error

$$
\mathbb{E}\left( (\Delta \hat{\theta}_m)^2 \right) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{G}_m^T \mathbf{G}_m + \frac{1}{4} \sigma^4 \left( [\text{tr}\mathbf{H}_m]^2 + 2\|\mathbf{H}_m\|_F^2 \right) + \mathscr{R}. \qquad (6.23)
$$

Here

$$
\|\mathbf{H}_m\|_F^2 = \text{tr}\mathbf{H}_m^2 = \|\mathbf{D}_m\|_F^2 = \text{tr}\mathbf{D}_m^2, \qquad (6.24)
$$

where $\|\cdot\|_F$ stands for the Frobenius norm (it is important to note that $\mathbf{H}_m$ is symmetric). The remainder $\mathscr{R}$ includes terms of order $\sigma^6$, as well as some terms of order $\sigma^4$ that contain third order partial derivatives, such as $\partial^3 \hat{\theta}_m / \partial x_i^3$ and $\partial^3 \hat{\theta}_m / \partial x_i^2 \partial x_j$. A similar expression can be derived for $\mathbb{E}\left( \Delta \hat{\theta}_m \Delta \hat{\theta}_{m'} \right)$ for $m \neq m'$, we omit it and only give the final formula below.

Comparing (6.22) and (6.23) we conclude that

$$
\text{Var}\left( \hat{\theta}_m \right) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{G}_m^T \mathbf{G}_m + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^4 \|\mathbf{H}_m\|_F^2 + \mathscr{R}. \qquad (6.25)
$$

Thus, the two fourth order terms in the MSE expansion (6.23) have different origins: the first one, $\frac{1}{4} \sigma^4 [\text{tr}\mathbf{H}_m]^2$, comes from the bias, and the second one, $\frac{1}{2} \sigma^4 \|\mathbf{H}_m\|_F^2$, comes from the variance.

**Covariances.** The above formulas can be easily extended to the cross product terms:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left( \left(\Delta \hat{\theta}_m \right)\left(\Delta \hat{\theta}_{m'} \right) \right) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{G}_m^T \mathbf{G}_{m'} + \frac{1}{4} \sigma^4 \left( [\text{tr}\mathbf{H}_m]\left(\text{tr}\mathbf{H}_{m'}\right) + 2\langle \mathbf{H}_m, \mathbf{H}_{m'} \rangle_F \right) + \mathscr{R}, \qquad (6.26)
$$

where $\langle\mathbf{H}_m, \mathbf{H}_{m'}\rangle_F = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}_m\mathbf{H}_{m'})$ stands for the Frobenius scalar product of matrices (again we note that $\mathbf{H}_{m'}$ is symmetric). Comparing (6.22) and (6.26) we conclude that

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\theta}_m, \hat{\theta}_{m'}) = \sigma^2\mathbf{G}_m^T\mathbf{G}_{m'} + \frac{1}{2}\,\sigma^4\langle\mathbf{H}_m, \mathbf{H}_{m'}\rangle_F + \mathscr{R}. \qquad (6.27)
$$

Just as before, the first $\mathcal{O}(\sigma^4)$ term in (6.26) comes from the biases, while the second one comes from the covariance.

## 6.7 A model with small noise and “moderate sample size”

Our approximate error analysis in Section 6.6 includes all the terms of order $\sigma^2$ and $\sigma^4$. In applications, one usually gets a large number of terms of order $\sigma^4$ given by complicated formulas; we will see examples in Chapter 7. Even the expression for the bias (6.22) alone may contain several terms of order $\sigma^2$. In this section we sort the higher order terms out to keep only the most significant ones. Our sorting method is motivated by Kanatani [104].

**Kanatani’s treatment of higher order terms.** Kanatani [104] recently derived formulas for the bias of certain ellipse fitting algorithms. He kept all the terms of order $\sigma^2$, but in the end he noticed that some terms were of order $\sigma^2$ (independent of $n$), while the others of order $\sigma^2/n$. The magnitude of the former was clearly larger than that of the latter, and when Kanatani made his final conclusions he ignored the terms of order $\sigma^2/n$. To justify this discretion, he simply noted that “in many vision applications $n$ is fairly large.”

**Formal classification of higher order terms.** We formalize Kanatani’s prescription for treating higher order terms in the approximate error analysis, and adopt the following rules:

- In the expression for the bias (6.22) we keep terms of order $\sigma^2$ (independent of $n$) and ignore terms of order $\sigma^2/n$.
- In the expression for the mean squared error (6.23) we keep terms of order $\sigma^4$ (independent of $n$) and ignore terms of order $\sigma^4/n$.

In the strict mathematical sense, to justify our rules we would need to assume that not only $\sigma \to 0$, but also $n \to \infty$, although $n$ may increase rather slowly. Precisely, $n$ need to grow slower than $1/\sigma^2$. For simplicity, however, we prefer to treat $\sigma$ as small variable and $n$ as a moderately large constant. We call this a *model with small noise and moderate sample size*.

**Magnitude of various terms.** The main term $\sigma^2\mathbf{G}_m^T\mathbf{G}_m$ in our expression for the mean squared error (6.23) is always of order $\sigma^2/n$; it will never be ignored. Out of the two higher order terms $\frac{1}{4}\,\sigma^4[\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{H}_m]^2$ and $\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma^4\|\mathbf{H}_m\|_F^2$, the latter is always of order $\sigma^4/n$ (under certain natural conditions), hence it will be discarded. The bias $\frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \mathrm{tr}\, \mathbf{H}_m$ in (6.22) is, generally, of order $\sigma^2$ (independent of $n$), thus its contribution to the mean squared error (6.23) is significant. However the full expression for the bias may contain terms of order $\sigma^2$ and of order $\sigma^2 / n$, of which the latter will be ignored; see below.

We only explain why our terms have the above order or magnitude, omitting a formal proof, which is quite complex.

**Analysis of the order of magnitude.** We make a natural assumption that the estimate $\hat{\Theta}$ is balanced in the sense that every observation $\mathbf{x}_i$ contributes to it equally. More precisely, the partial derivatives of $\hat{\Theta}(\mathbf{X})$ with respect to the components of $\mathbf{X}$ must have the same order of magnitude.

For example, all our circle fits are balanced, in this sense, as they are based on averaging observations. In particular, the geometric circle fit minimizes $a^2 + b^2 - r^2$, recall (4.25). The Kåsa fit solves the system (5.5) that involve averages, too.

Under this assumption it is easy to see that the partial derivatives of $\hat{\Theta}(\mathbf{X})$ with respect to the components of $\mathbf{X}$ are $\mathcal{O}(1/n)$. For example, if one perturbs every data point by $\epsilon \ll \sigma$, then the center and radius of the fitting circle should change by $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)$. On the other hand, $\Delta \hat{\Theta} = \langle \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \hat{\Theta}, \Delta \mathbf{X} \rangle + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)$. As one can always make the perturbation vector $\Delta \mathbf{X}$ parallel to the gradient $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \hat{\Theta}$, it follows that $\| \Delta \hat{\Theta} \| \geq n^{1/2} \epsilon \| \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \hat{\Theta} \|$, thus $\| \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \hat{\Theta} \| \leq n^{-1/2}$, i.e., each component of $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \hat{\Theta}$ must be $\mathcal{O}(1/n)$.

This implies that the components of the vector $\mathbf{G}_m$ and the diagonal elements of $\mathbf{D}_m$ in (6.23)–(6.24) are of order $1/n$. As a result,

$$
\mathbf{G}_m^T \mathbf{G}_m = \mathcal{O}(1/n)
$$

and also

$$
\mathrm{tr}\, \mathbf{H}_m = \mathrm{tr}\, \mathbf{D}_m = \mathcal{O}(1)
$$

and

$$
\| \mathbf{H}_m \|_F^2 = \| \mathbf{D}_m \|_F^2 = \mathcal{O}(1/n).
$$

Also recall that the remainders denoted by $\mathcal{R}$ in our expansions include some terms of order $\sigma^4$ that contain third order partial derivatives; those derivatives can be shown to have order of $1/n$.

Thus the terms in (6.23) have the following orders of magnitude:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta \hat{\theta}_m)^2\right] = \mathcal{O}(\sigma^2 / n) + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^4) + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^4 / n) + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^6), \qquad (6.28)
$$

where each big-O simply indicates the order of the corresponding term in (6.23).

We see that the fourth-order term $\frac{1}{4} \sigma^4 [\mathrm{tr}\, \mathbf{H}_m]^2$ coming from the bias is larger (hence more significant) than the other one, $\frac{1}{2} \sigma^4 \| \mathbf{H}_m \|_F^2$, that comes from the variance.
