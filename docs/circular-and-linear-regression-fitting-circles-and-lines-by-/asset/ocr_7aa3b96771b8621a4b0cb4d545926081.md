In the $xy$ coordinates, the map $\mathscr{F}$ is computed by

$x' = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}, \quad \quad y' = -\frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}.$

Since the negative sign before the second fraction is not essential for our purpose of fitting circles, many researchers just drop it and use the slightly simplified rules

$x' = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}, \quad \quad y' = \frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}. \quad \quad \quad (8.20)$

From now on we will call this map the inversion $\mathscr{F}$. It has all the properties of the actual inversion mentioned above.

The map $\mathscr{F}$ has many remarkable properties summarized next.

**Theorem 17** *The map $\mathscr{F}$ is conformal at every point $(x, y)$, except the origin $(0, 0)$. This means that its derivative $D\mathscr{F}$ (the linear part of $\mathscr{F}$) preserves angles between tangent vectors and it expands (or contracts) all tangent vectors uniformly. Precisely, if $(dx, dy)$ is a tangent vector at $(x, y) \neq (0, 0)$, then its image $(dx', dy') = D\mathscr{F}(dx, dy)$ has length*

$\|(dx', dy')\| = (x^2 + y^2)^{-1} \|(dx, dy)\|.$

*Note that the factor $(x^2 + y^2)^{-1}$ does not depend on the direction of the tangent vector $(dx, dy)$.*

We have seen conformal maps in Section 8.3. This theorem can be verified directly, by computing the matrix $D\mathscr{F}$ of partial derivatives of the formulas (8.20) with respect to $x$ and $y$, we leave this exercise to the reader.

**Theorem 18** *The map $\mathscr{F}$ transforms every line into a line or a circle. It transforms every circle into a line or a circle. In particular, every circle passing through the origin $(0, 0)$ is transformed into a line not passing through the origin $(0, 0)$.*

This theorem is illustrated in Fig. 8.2.

**Inversion-based circle fit.** The key fact in the last theorem is that $\mathscr{F}$ transforms *some* circles (precisely, those passing through the origin) to lines and back.

Now suppose one looks for a circle passing through the origin. Then the above theorem suggests the following fit:

+   **Step 1.** Map the data points $(x_i, y_i)$ into $(x'_i, y'_i)$ by formulas (8.20).

+   **Step 2.** Fit a line to the points $(x'_i, y'_i)$.

+   **Step 3.** Transform the line obtained at Step 2 into a circle passing through the origin by the map $\mathscr{F}$ (recall that $\mathscr{F}^{-1} = \mathscr{F}$).
