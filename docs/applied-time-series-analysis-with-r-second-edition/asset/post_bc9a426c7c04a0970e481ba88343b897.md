
System frequencies of the AR part of the ARMA(p,q) model will tend to introduce peaks in the spectral density similar to those for the corresponding AR(p) model. However, system frequencies of the MA part tend to produce troughs or dips in the spectral density at the corresponding system frequencies. The peaks in a spectral density for an ARMA(p,q) (or AR(p)) model are caused by small values in the denominator of Equation 3.92 which occur near the system frequencies of the AR part of the model. Correspondingly, small values in the numerator of Equation 3.92 near the system frequencies of the MA part will produce the tendency for dips or troughs to appear in the spectral density.

**Using R:** As mentioned previously, the tswge functions `gen.arma.wge`, `true.arma.aut.wge`, `true.arma.spec.wge`, and `plotts.true.wge` can be used to generate realizations, and create plots of realizations, true autocorrelations, and true spectral densities, for ARMA processes. Again remember to be careful about the signs of the coefficients.

## 3.3.3 Factor Tables and ARMA(p,q) Models

According to Theorem 3.4 the stationarity and invertibility of an ARMA(p,q) model can be tested by examining the factors of \(\phi(z)\) and of \(\theta(z)\), respectively. As in the AR(p) case, factor tables are a useful tool for understanding these factors, and this can be accomplished using the tswge R function `factor.wge`. The factor tables for the AR components of an ARMA(p,q) model are interpreted as in the AR(p) case. That is, system frequencies indicate natural frequencies in the model, and spectral densities will tend to have peaks near these system frequencies. The key to understanding factor tables for an ARMA(p,q) process is to realize that the factor tables for the MA components are interpreted in an opposite manner. Specifically, the MA system frequencies indicate frequencies whose impact has been weakened in the total model. Spectral densities will tend to have dips rather than peaks in the vicinity of the MA system frequencies. (See the discussion following Equation 3.92.) As an example, note that the spectral density in Figure 3.3a for the MA(1) model \(X_t = (1 - 0.99B)a_t\) has a dip or trough at \(f = 0\) whereas the spectral density for \((1 - 0.99B)X_t = a_t\) would have a sharp peak at \(f = 0\). Another example of this effect is the fact that in Equation 3.90 the AR factor \(1 - 0.95B\) and the MA factor \(1 - 0.8B\) are each associated with system frequency \(f = 0\). In Example 3.16 we illustrated the fact that the MA factor \(1 - 0.8B\) weakened the behavior of the AR factor \(1 - 0.95B\) in the ARMA(1,1) model in Equation 3.90. We use the term “system frequencies” to indicate the frequencies displayed in the factor table whether the factor is an AR or MA factor. Examples 3.17 and 3.18 further illustrate the use of factor tables to aid the understanding of ARMA(p,q) models.

## 3.3.4 Autocorrelations of an ARMA(p,q) Model

Multiplying each term in Equation 3.86 (without loss of generality using the zero mean form) by \(X_{t-k}(k \ge 0)\) and taking expectations yields

$$
\gamma_k = \phi_1\gamma_{k-1} + \cdots + \phi_p\gamma_{k-p} + \gamma_{Xa}(k) - \theta_1\gamma_{Xa}(k-1) - \cdots - \theta_q\gamma_{Xa}(k-q). \tag{3.96}
$$

where

$$
\gamma_{Xa}(m) = E[X_{t-m}a_t] \tag{3.97}
$$

From Equation 3.97, it follows that

$$
\gamma_{Xa}(m)=
\begin{cases}
0, & m>0,\\
\psi_{|m|}\sigma_a^2, & m\le 0.
\end{cases} \tag{3.98}
$$

Thus, for \(k \ge 0\)

$$
\gamma_k = \phi_1\gamma_{k-1} + \cdots + \phi_p\gamma_{k-p} - \sigma_a^2\bigl(\theta_k\psi_0 + \cdots + \theta_q\psi_{q-k}\bigr), \tag{3.99}
$$

where \(\theta_0 = -1\), and the parenthetical expression is taken to be zero if \(k > q\). Specifically, \(\gamma_q\) is given by

$$
\gamma_q = \phi_1\gamma_{q-1} + \cdots + \phi_p\gamma_{q-p} - \sigma_a^2\theta_q, \tag{3.100}
$$

and \(\gamma_k\) does not satisfy a homogeneous equation, since \(\sigma_a^2\theta_q \ne 0\). However, for \(k > q\),

$$
\gamma_k - \phi_1\gamma_{k-1} - \cdots - \phi_p\gamma_{k-p}=0, \tag{3.101}
$$

and

$$
\rho_k - \phi_1\rho_{k-1} - \cdots - \phi_p\rho_{k-p}=0, \tag{3.102}
$$

so that \(\gamma_k\) and \(\rho_k\) satisfy a pth-order linear homogeneous difference equation with constant coefficients for \(k > q\). Since we have assumed that \(\phi(z)\) and \(\theta(z)\) share no common factors, \(p\) is the lowest order of a homogeneous linear difference equation with constant coefficients satisfied by \(\rho_k\). The solution to Equation 3.102 can be made to satisfy \(p\) linear boundary conditions obtained from Equation 3.99 for \(k = q, q - 1, \ldots, q - p + 1\). Thus, for \(k > q - p\), it follows that

$$
\rho_k = \sum_{i=1}^{v} \sum_{j=0}^{m_i-1} C_{ij} k^{j} r_i^{-k}, \tag{3.103}
$$

or in the case of no repeated roots of the characteristic equation,

$$
\rho_k = C_1 r_1^{-k} + \cdots + C_p r_p^{-k}, \tag{3.104}
$$

where the \(C_{ij}\) and \(C_j\) are constants uniquely determined from Equation 3.99 with \(k = q, q - 1, \ldots, q - p + 1\).

The autocovariance function can also be found as the solution of a system of nonhomogeneous linear difference equations. Letting \(\nu = \max(p, q + 1)\) and for \(k = 0,1,\ldots,\nu\) in Equation 3.99 gives the \(\nu + 1\) linear equations in the \(\nu + 1\) unknowns \(\gamma_0, \gamma_1, \ldots, \gamma_\nu\):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_0 &= \phi_1\gamma_1 + \phi_2\gamma_2 + \cdots + \phi_p\gamma_p - \sigma_a^2(-1 + \theta_1\psi_1 + \cdots + \theta_q\psi_q), \\
\gamma_1 &= \phi_1\gamma_0 + \phi_2\gamma_1 + \cdots + \phi_p\gamma_{p-1} - \sigma_a^2(\theta_1 + \theta_2\psi_1 + \cdots + \theta_q\psi_{q-1}), \\
&\vdots \\
\gamma_\nu &= \phi_1\gamma_{\nu-1} + \phi_2\gamma_{\nu-2} + \cdots + \phi_p\gamma_{\nu-p} - \sigma_a^2(\theta_\nu + \theta_{\nu+1}\psi_1 + \cdots + \theta_q\psi_{q-\nu}),
\end{aligned} \tag{3.105}
$$

where \(\theta_j = 0\) whenever \(j > q\). The \(\psi\)-weights can be obtained from the \(\phi_j\) and \(\theta_j\) using a recursive algorithm to be given shortly. For \(k > \nu\), \(\gamma_k\) can be solved by simply using the recursion in Equation 3.101 using the starting values, \(\gamma_\nu, \gamma_{\nu-1}, \ldots, \gamma_{\nu-p+1}\).

Note that Equation 3.103 shows that \(\rho_k \to 0\) (and equivalently \(\gamma_k \to 0\)) as \(k \to \infty\) for an ARMA(p,q) process. From Theorem 1.1, it thus follows that, in the case of an ARMA(p,q) process, \(\Gamma_k\) and \(\rho_k\) defined in Equations 1.2 and 1.3, respectively, are positive definite for each integer \(k > 0\).
