## 12.3.3 Fundamental Wavelet Approximation Result
The fundamental wavelet-based approximation result analogous to the Fourier series approximation is given in Theorem 12.1 that follows.

### Theorem 12.1
Let {ψ_{j,k}} be an orthogonal wavelet basis, and let g ∈ L²(ℝ). Then,

g(x) = Σ_{j=-∞}^{∞} Σ_{k=-∞}^{∞} d_{j,k} ψ_{j,k}(x),  (12.15)

where the equality is in the mean-square sense and the wavelet transform coefficients, d_{j,k}, are given by

d_{j,k} = 1/||ψ||² ∫_{-∞}^{∞} g(t) ψ_{j,k}(t) dt,  (12.16)

where ||ψ||² denotes ∫_{-∞}^{∞} |ψ(x)|² dx.

Proof: See Daubechies (1992) and Ogden (1997). Appendix 12A gives a formal derivation to show that if Equation 12.15 holds, then d_{j,k} is given by Equation 12.16.

Equation 12.15 is analogous to the Fourier series representation in Equation 12A.3. Also, the coefficients, d_{j,k}, in Equation 12.16 correspond to the Fourier series coefficients, α_k in Equation 12A.4. In Fourier analysis large values of α_k² are associated with data (in our setting time series) that have periodic content in the neighborhood of the frequency f = k/n. Similarly, large values of | d_{j,k} | in Equation 12.15 are associated with detail behavior of duration about 2^j, that is, the “frequency” or “1/scale” is about 2^{-j}. Equation 12.17 is sometimes written as

$g(x) = S_J(x) + \sum_{j=-\infty}^{J} D_j(x),\tag{12.21}$

where $S_J(x) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} S_{J,k} \phi_{J,k}(x)$ and $D_j(x) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} d_{j,k} \psi_{j,k}(x)$. The first term in Equation 12.21 gives a “smooth” approximation to $g(x)$ to level $J$ (i.e., involves the higher values of $j$). The $s_{j,k}$ are called smooth coefficients. As we have previously discussed, the $d_{j,k}$ are called the detail coefficients, and smaller values of $j$ correspond to finer detail.

## 12.3.4 Discrete Wavelet Transform for Data Sets of Finite Length
For clarity of presentation, the discussion up to this point regarding wavelets has involved data on a continuum. However, for analyzing discrete data, $\boldsymbol{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$, of finite length (such as a realization from a discrete time series), we will use the discrete wavelet transform (DWT). Given an orthogonal wavelet basis set, the DWT maps the vector $\boldsymbol{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$ to a vector of $n$ wavelet coefficients. Letting $\boldsymbol{w}$ denote the vector of wavelet coefficients, then it can be shown that

$\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x},\tag{12.22}$

where $\boldsymbol{M}$ is an orthogonal matrix that can be written as

$\boldsymbol{M} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{M}_1 \\ \boldsymbol{M}_2 \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{M}_{J_0} \\ \boldsymbol{V}_{J_0} \end{pmatrix},\tag{12.23}$

where

$M_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \widetilde{\psi}_{1,1}(1) & \widetilde{\psi}_{1,1}(2) & \cdots & \widetilde{\psi}_{1,1}(n) \\ \widetilde{\psi}_{1,2}(1) & \widetilde{\psi}_{1,2}(2) & \cdots & \widetilde{\psi}_{1,2}(n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ \widetilde{\psi}_{1,n/2}(1) & \widetilde{\psi}_{1,n/2}(2) & \cdots & \widetilde{\psi}_{1,n/2}(n) \end{pmatrix},$ 
$M_2 = \begin{pmatrix} \widetilde{\psi}_{2,1}(1) & \widetilde{\psi}_{2,1}(2) & \cdots & \widetilde{\psi}_{2,1}(n) \\ \widetilde{\psi}_{2,2}(1) & \widetilde{\psi}_{2,2}(2) & \cdots & \widetilde{\psi}_{2,2}(n) \\ \vdots & & & \\ \widetilde{\psi}_{2,n/2^2}(1) & \widetilde{\psi}_{2,n/2^2}(2) & \cdots & \widetilde{\psi}_{2,n/2^2}(n) \end{pmatrix},$ 
$M_{J_0} = (\widetilde{\psi}_{J_0,1}(1)\ \widetilde{\psi}_{J_0,1}(2)\ \cdots\ \widetilde{\psi}_{J_0,1}(n)),$ 
$V_{J_0} = (\widetilde{\phi}_{J_0,1}(1)\ \widetilde{\phi}_{J_0,1}(2)\ \cdots\ \widetilde{\phi}_{J_0,1}(n)).$

Since M is orthogonal then it also follows that

$$x = M' w,$$ 
(12.24)

where M' is the transpose of M. That is, knowing M allows us to transform from the data to the wavelet coefficients (the DWT), and M' allows us to perform an inverse discrete wavelet transform (IDWT), that is to transform from the wavelet coefficients to the data.

NOTE: While the DWT can be applied to data sets whose length is not a power of 2, in the illustrative material here, we will assume that $n = 2^{J_0}$ for some $J_0$.

For a discussion of discrete wavelets and scaling functions, see Percival and Walden (2000), Strang and Nguyen (1997), and Bruce and Gao (1996). It should be noted that the discrete version of a wavelet takes the general form of the continuous wavelet, but is not simply a discretized version of it. Most authors refer to the elements of M as wavelet and scaling filter coefficients. Since the wavelet and scaling filter coefficients are close approximations to the corresponding discretized versions of the continuous dilated and translated wavelets, we use the notation $\widetilde{\psi}_{j,k}$ and $\widetilde{\phi}_{j,k}$ (see Percival and Walden (2000), Equations 469 and 477a).

Using the partitioning in Equation 12.23 we can write Equation 12.22 as

$$\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{w}_1\\\mathbf{w}_2\\\vdots\\\mathbf{w}_{J_0}\\\mathbf{v}_{J_0}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{M}_1\\\mathbf{M}_2\\\vdots\\\mathbf{M}_{J_0}\\\mathbf{V}_{J_0}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x},\tag{12.25}$$

where

$$\mathbf{w}_1 = (d_{1,1}\quad d_{1,2}\quad d_{1,3}\quad d_{1,n/2})',$$ $$\mathbf{w}_2 = (d_{2,1}\quad d_{2,2}\quad \cdots\quad d_{2,n/2^2})',$$ $$\mathbf{w}_{J_0}=d_{J_0,1}\quad \mathbf{v}_{J_0}=s_{J_0,1}.$$

See Percival and Walden (2000) for a discussion of the issues involved in constructing the matrix $\mathbf{M}$.
