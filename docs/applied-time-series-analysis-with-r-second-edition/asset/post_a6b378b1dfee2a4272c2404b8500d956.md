NOTE: As might be deduced from this example, there is a fundamental difference between the behavior of ρ_k^* for a nonstationary process and of ρ_k for a stationary process. That is, in a stationary process, ρ_k is influenced to some degree by all of the factors in the model, both AR and MA. However, in a nonstationary process, ρ_k^* is affected only by the roots on the unit circle, and in fact, only by those of highest multiplicity. In this regard, we have the following theorem that was first suggested by Gray et al. (1978) and proven in general by Findley
(1978), Quinn (1980), and Gray and Woodward (1981).

## Theorem 5.1 (Findley–Quinn Theorem)

Suppose that the following are true:

- 1. $X_t$ is an ARMA$(p + d, q)$ with $d$ roots “close” to the unit circle
- 2. Of the $d$ roots in (a), $j$ have highest multiplicity $\ell$

Then, $\rho_k^*$ (the limiting value of $\rho_k$ as the $d$ roots approach the unit circle uniformly) exists, and for each $k = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots$, $\rho_k^*$ satisfies the linear homogeneous difference equation of order $j$ for $j > 0$.

$\left(1 - \zeta_1 B - \cdots - \zeta_j B^j\right)\rho_k^* = 0,\qquad (5.14)$

where $j$ is specified in (b). The roots of $\zeta(z)=0$ are all on the unit circle (i.e. have modulus 1), and $\zeta(B)$ is the operator formed from the product of the nonstationary factors of highest multiplicity in $\lambda(B)$.

## 5.2.2 Cyclical Models

In Example 5.7, it is seen that for an ARUMA(p,d,q) process, the limiting autocorrelations, ρ*_k, do not decay to zero. In some cases they stay fixed at 1, and in others they are periodic. In particular, if all of the d roots of λ(z) = 0 in an ARUMA(p,d,q) process are equal to 1, the process is noncyclical. On the other hand, if at least one of the d roots of highest multiplicity is −1 or not real, then we will refer to the process as a cyclical ARUMA(p,d,q) process. The simplest cyclical ARUMA process is (1 + B)X_t = a_t. In this case, ρ*_k = (−1)^k so that the autocorrelation is periodic with system frequency f0 = 0.5.
