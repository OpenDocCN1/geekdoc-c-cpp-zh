It should be noted that wavelets (mother and father) usually have no analytic form. They are typically calculated using a dilation equation. See Daubechies (1992), Bruce and Gao (1996), and Strang and Nguyen (1997).

Equation 12.17 is sometimes written as

$g(x) = S_J(x) + \sum_{j=-\infty}^{J} D_j(x),\tag{12.21}$

where $S_J(x) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} S_{J,k} \phi_{J,k}(x)$ and $D_j(x) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} d_{j,k} \psi_{j,k}(x)$. The first term in Equation 12.21 gives a “smooth” approximation to $g(x)$ to level $J$ (i.e., involves the higher values of $j$). The $s_{j,k}$ are called smooth coefficients. As we have previously discussed, the $d_{j,k}$ are called the detail coefficients, and smaller values of $j$ correspond to finer detail.

# 12.3.4 Discrete Wavelet Transform for Data Sets of Finite Length

For clarity of presentation, the discussion up to this point regarding wavelets has involved data on a continuum. However, for analyzing discrete data, $\boldsymbol{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$, of finite length (such as a realization from a discrete time series), we will use the discrete wavelet transform (DWT). Given an orthogonal wavelet basis set, the DWT maps the vector $\boldsymbol{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$ to a vector of $n$ wavelet coefficients. Letting $\boldsymbol{w}$ denote the vector of wavelet coefficients, then it can be shown that

$\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x},\tag{12.22}$

where $\boldsymbol{M}$ is an orthogonal matrix that can be written as

$\boldsymbol{M} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{M}_1 \\ \boldsymbol{M}_2 \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{M}_{J_0} \\ \boldsymbol{V}_{J_0} \end{pmatrix},\tag{12.23}$
