
Whenever j = 0,1,..., ℓ - 1, the forecast errors may be correlated.

- 8. e_{t0}(ℓ1) and e_{t0}(ℓ2) are correlated, that is, forecast errors from the same origin are correlated.
- 9. The “best mean squared error (MSE) forecast” for ∑_{k=1}^m α_k X_{t0+k} from origin t0 is given by ∑_{k=1}^m α_k \hat X_{t0}(k). As an example, if X_t represents monthly sales, then (i) states that from the forecast origin t0, the “best” forecast for next quarter’s sales, that is, for X_{t0+1} + X_{t0+2} + X_{t0+3} is \hat X_{t0}(1) + \hat X_{t0}(2) + \hat X_{t0}(3)

## 6.4 π-Weight Form of the Forecast Function

A stationary and invertible process can be written in the π-weight form

∑_{j=0}^{∞} π_j (X_{t-j} - μ) = a_t  (6.15)

which was previously given in Equation 3.83. Therefore, since π0 = 1, Equation 6.15 can be written as

X_t = μ - ∑_{j=1}^{∞} π_j (X_{t-j} - μ) + a_t

which leads to the forecasting formula

\hat X_{t0}(ℓ) = μ - ∑_{j=1}^{∞} π_j (\hat X_{t0}(ℓ - j) - μ)  (6.16)

where, as before, \hat X_{t0}(ℓ) = X_{t0+ℓ}, for ℓ ≤ 0. The form in Equation 6.16 is similar to the ψ-weight form in Equation 6.9 in that it involves an infinite summation and is based on the unrealistic assumption that X_{t0}, X_{t0-1}, ..., X_1, X_0, X_{-1}, ... are known. Thus, the following truncated version of the forecast function is used in practice.

### Truncated Version of the Forecast Equation

$$\hat{X}_{t_0}^{(T)}(\ell)=\bar{X}-\sum_{j=1}^{t_0-1+\ell}\pi_j\left(\hat{X}_{t_0}^{(T)}(\ell-j)-\bar{X}\right). \tag{6.17}$$

For example, if $t_0=n$, then using [Equation 6.17](#), it follows that

$$\hat{X}_n^{(T)}(1)=\bar{X}-\pi_1(X(n)-\bar{X})-\pi_2(X(n-1)-\bar{X})-\cdots-\pi_n(X(1)-\bar{X}),$$
$$\hat{X}_n^{(T)}(2)=\bar{X}-\pi_1(\hat{X}_n^{(T)}(1)-\bar{X})-\pi_2(X(n)-\bar{X})-\cdots-\pi_{n+1}(X(1)-\bar{X}),$$
$$\vdots$$

**NOTES:** The $\pi$-weight version of the forecast function is appropriately used when the $\pi$-weights damp sufficiently quickly so that the $\pi_j$’s are essentially zero for $j>t_0-1+\ell$, which should generally hold for stationary and invertible ARMA processes when $t_0$ is sufficiently large. This form of the forecast function is useful for forecasting based on long memory models (see [Section 11.6](#)).

## 6.5 Forecasting Based on the Difference Equation

If $X_t$ is a stationary, invertible ARMA$(p,q)$ process, then [Equation 6.9](#) is an expression for the best predictor of $X_{t_0+\ell}$ given $X_{t_0}, X_{t_0-1}, \ldots$, in terms of the general linear process or “infinite moving average” representation of the process. The forecast, $\hat{X}_{t_0}^{(T)}(\ell)$, can also be written in terms of the difference equation satisfied by the data, a form that is more useful in practice. Suppose $X_t$ satisfies $\phi(B)X_t=\Theta(B)a_t$, and hence that $\phi(B)X_{t_0+\ell}=\Theta(B)a_{t_0+\ell}$. Thus,

$$X_{t_0+\ell}-\phi_1X_{t_0+\ell-1}-\cdots-\phi_pX_{t_0+\ell-p}=a_{t_0+\ell}-\theta_1a_{t_0+\ell-1}-\cdots-\theta_qa_{t_0+\ell-q}.$$

Taking conditional expectations, we obtain

$$E_{t_0}\left[X_{t_0+\ell}-\phi_1X_{t_0+\ell-1}-\cdots-\phi_pX_{t_0+\ell-p}\right]=E_{t_0}\left[a_{t_0+\ell}-\theta_1a_{t_0+\ell-1}-\cdots-\theta_qa_{t_0+\ell-q}\right]. \tag{6.18}$$

- 1. $X_1$, $X_2$, ..., $X_{t_0}$ have been observed
- 2. Model parameters have been estimated
- 3. The $a_t$ are unknown

Consequently, Equation 6.21 cannot be immediately applied, and in practice, we use Equation 6.21 with the following modifications:

- a. The estimated model is assumed to be the true model (and $\mu$ is estimated by $\overline{X}$).
- b. The $a_t$ are estimated from the data.

The comment in (a) relates to the fact mentioned at the beginning of this chapter, that our forecasting formulas are based on a known model.

The estimation of $a_t$ proceeds by noting that for an ARMA$(p,q)$ model,

$$X_t = \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_j X_{t-j} + a_t - \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j a_{t-j} + \left(1 - \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_j\right)\mu,$$

which can be rewritten as

$$a_t = X_t - \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_j X_{t-j} + \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j a_{t-j} - \left(1 - \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_j\right)\mu.$$

So, for $t \le t_0$, $E_{t_0}[a_t](= a_t)$ is estimated by

$$\hat{a}_t = \begin{cases} 0, & t \le p \\ X_t - \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_j X_{t-j} + \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j \hat{a}_{t-j} - \left(1 - \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_j\right)\overline{X}, & t = \max(p+1, q+1), \dots, t_0. \end{cases} \tag{6.23}$$

Moreover, from Equation 6.20, since $E_{t_0}[a_t] = 0$ for $t > t_0$, we define

$$\hat{a}_t = 0, \quad t > t_0.$$

This leads to the following.

### 6.5.2 Basic Difference Equation Form for Calculating Forecasts from an ARMA$(p,q)$ Model

$$\hat{X}_{t_0}(\ell)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\hat{X}_{t_0}(\ell-j)-\sum_{j=\ell}^{q}\theta_j\hat{a}_{t_0+\ell-j}+\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\right)\bar{X}.\tag{6.24}$$

Notice that the $\hat{a}_t$'s enter into the calculation of the forecasts only when there are moving average terms in the model. The use of Equation 6.24 for calculating forecasts is illustrated in the following two examples. It should be noted that an improved method of calculating the $\hat{a}_t$'s, based on backcasting, is discussed in Section 7.4.

We observe $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_{t_0}$, and our goal is to estimate or forecast $X_{t_0+\ell}$. From Equation 6.24, we obtain

In this section we focus on techniques for assessing the performance of the forecasts. We will consider two techniques: (a) using probability limits and (b) forecasting the last k data values.

### 6.7.1 Probability Limits for Forecasts

In the preceding sections, techniques were described for obtaining optimal forecasts of $X_{t_0+\ell}$ given information up to time $t_0$. In addition to finding the forecast $\hat{X}_{t_0}(\ell)$, it is also important to assess the uncertainty associated with the predictions.

Recall from Equation 6.11 that the forecast error associated with the prediction of $X_{t_0+\ell}$, given data to time $t_0$, is given by

$e_{t_0}(\ell) = X_{t_0+\ell} - \hat{X}_{t_0}(\ell)$
$= \sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1} \psi_k a_{t_0+\ell-k},$

where $\psi_0 = 1$. If the white noise is normal with zero mean and variance $\sigma_a^2$, then $e_{t_0}(\ell)$ is also normal, and from Equations 6.12 and 6.14, it follows that $e_{t_0}(\ell)$ has zero mean and variance:

$\text{Var}\left[e_{t_0}(\ell)\right] = \sigma_a^2 \sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1} \psi_k^2.$
(6.31)

Thus

$\frac{e_{t_0}(\ell)}{\sigma_a\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1} \psi_k^2\right\}^{1/2}} \sim N(0, 1),$

from which it follows that

$\text{Prob}\left\{-z_{1-\alpha/2} \le \frac{X_{t_0+\ell} - \hat{X}_{t_0}(\ell)}{\sigma_a\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1} \psi_k^2\right\}^{1/2}} \le z_{1-\alpha/2}\right\} = 1 - \alpha,$
(6.32)
