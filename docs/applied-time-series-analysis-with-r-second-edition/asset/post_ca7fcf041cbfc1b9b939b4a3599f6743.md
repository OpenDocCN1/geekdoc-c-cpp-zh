
where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are real, and $\rho_k$ in [Equations 3.55 and 3.60] can be seen to be a mixture of damped exponentials or damped oscillating exponentials that decay to zero as $k$ increases, since $|\alpha_1|<1$ and $|\alpha_2|<1$.

- 2. **Complex roots**, that is, $\phi_1^2+4\phi_2<0$: In this case, the second-order characteristic polynomial is irreducible (i.e., $r_1$ and $r_2$ are not real). The roots $r_1$ and $r_2$ are complex conjugates and thus so are $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$. We use the notation $\alpha_1=a+bi$ and $\alpha_2=a-bi$. Now, $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ are given by

$$a\pm bi=\frac{\phi_1}{2}\pm\frac{\sqrt{-(\phi_1^2+4\phi_2)}}{2}i.$$

It will be convenient to express $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ in polar form as $\alpha_1=R(\cos 2\pi f_0+i\sin 2\pi f_0)=Re^{2\pi f_0i}$ and $\alpha_2=Re^{-2\pi f_0i}$, where $\tan(2\pi f_0)=b/a$ and $R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. In our case,

$$\begin{aligned} R&=|\alpha_1| \\ &=\sqrt{-\phi_2}. \end{aligned}\tag{3.61}$$

and $\cos 2\pi f_0=a/\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=\phi_1/(2\sqrt{-\phi_2})$. Substituting into [Equation 3.60], we get

$$\rho_k=(\sqrt{-\phi_2})^k\left\{\frac{\sin((k+1)2\pi f_0)+\phi_2\sin((k-1)2\pi f_0)}{(1-\phi_2)\sin 2\pi f_0}\right\}.$$

Letting $\psi$ be such that $\tan\psi=\frac{1-\phi_2}{1+\phi_2}\tan 2\pi f_0$, we can rewrite $\rho_k$ as

$$\rho_k=(\sqrt{-\phi_2})^k\left\{\frac{\sin(2\pi kf_0+\psi)}{\sin\psi}\right\}.\tag{3.62}$$

where $\cos 2\pi f_0=\phi_1/(2\sqrt{-\phi_2})$. That is, the second factor of [Equation 3.62] is periodic with frequency

$$f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\phi_1}{2\sqrt{-\phi_2}}\right). \tag{3.63}$$

So, $\rho_k$ is a damped sinusoidal function, and we call $f_0$ the *natural frequency* or *system frequency*.

*Case 2—Roots are equal:* In order for the roots of the characteristic equation to be equal they must be real, and the characteristic polynomial has the factored form $\phi(z)=(1-r_1^{-1}z)^2$. From [Equation 3.47], $\rho_k$ is given by

$$\rho_k=C_{10}r_1^{-k}+C_{11}kr_1^{-k},$$

and graphically $\rho_k$ has an appearance similar to that for an AR(1).

## 3.2.7.2 Spectral Density of an AR(2)

The spectral density of an AR(2) model is given by [Equation 3.54] to be

$$S_X(f)=\frac{\sigma_a^2}{\sigma_X^2\left|1-\phi_1e^{-2\pi if}-\phi_2e^{-4\pi if}\right|^2}$$

$$=\frac{\sigma_a^2}{\sigma_X^2\left\{1+\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2-2\phi_1(1-\phi_2)\cos 2\pi f-2\phi_2\cos 4\pi f\right\}}. \tag{3.64}$$

In the case of complex roots sufficiently close to the unit circle, the peak in $S_X(f)$ occurs near, but not exactly at the system frequency $f_0$ (see Problem 3.20). Also, if the characteristic equation has at least one real root sufficiently close to the unit circle, then $S(f)$ will have a “peak” at $f=0$ if the root is positive, while $S(f)$ will have a peak at $f=0.5$ if the root is negative.

## 3.2.7.3 Stationary/Causal Region of an AR(2)

In order for the AR(2) process to be causal and stationary, it must be the case that $|r_1|>1$ and $|r_2|>1$. Clearly, this imposes the constraint that $\phi(z)\ne 0$ for $z=1$, that is, it follows that $1-\phi_1-\phi_2\ne 0$. However, this condition is not sufficient to guarantee causality and stationarity. Schur’s lemma (see [Appendix 3A]) gives necessary and sufficient conditions for the roots of a polynomial equation to all lie outside the unit circle. The matrix $C$ described in that lemma

in the case of a second-order model can be shown to be

$$C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - \phi_2^2 & -\phi_1(1 + \phi_2) \\ -\phi_1(1 + \phi_2) & 1 - \phi_2^2 \end{bmatrix}, \tag{3.65}$$

(see Problem 3.22(a)). By definition, $C$ is positive definite if and only if $1 - \phi_2^2 > 0$ and $(1 + \phi_2)^2\{(1 - \phi_2)^2 - \phi_1^2\} > 0$. Thus, it can be shown (Problem 3.22(b)) that the AR(2) process is stationary if and only if $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ satisfy

- $|\phi_2| < 1$
- $\phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1$
- $\phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1$

## 3.2.7.4 ψ-Weights of an AR(2) Model

Factoring the AR(2) model gives

$$X_t - \phi_1 X_{t-1} - \phi_2 X_{t-2} = (1 - \alpha_1 B)(1 - \alpha_2 B)X_t = a_t, \tag{3.66}$$

where the $\alpha_j$ may be real or complex numbers with $|\alpha_j| < 1$, $j = 1, 2$. Then, from Equations 3.26 and 3.27

$$X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \psi_k(2) a_{t-k},$$

where

$$\psi_k(2) = \frac{\alpha_2^{k+1} - \alpha_1^{k+1}}{\alpha_2 - \alpha_1}.$$

Now suppose that $r_1$ and $r_2$ are complex conjugate roots of the characteristic equation associated with the AR(2) model in Equation 3.66. Then, $\alpha_1 = r_1^{-1} = (a + bi)^{-1}$ and $\alpha_2 = r_2^{-1} = (a - bi)^{-1}$, so it follows that

$$\alpha_1 = \frac{a - bi}{a^2 + b^2}$$ 
$$\alpha_2 = \frac{a + bi}{a^2 + b^2},$$

where $b \neq 0$. Now letting $R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, then $\alpha_1 = R^{-1} e^{-i\theta}$ and $\alpha_2 = R^{-1} e^{i\theta}$, where $\theta = \text{arc tan}(b / a)$. Then,

$$\alpha_2 - \alpha_1 = 2R^{-1}\left[\frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2}\right] = 2iR^{-1} \sin \theta,$$

and

$$\psi_k(2) = R^{-k-1}\left[\frac{e^{i\theta(k+1)} - e^{-i\theta(k+1)}}{2iR^{-1} \sin \theta}\right] = R^{-k}\frac{\sin \theta(k + 1)}{\sin \theta}.$$

Therefore,

$$X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} R^{-k} \frac{\sin \theta(k + 1)}{\sin \theta} a_{t-k}. \tag{3.67}$$

were given. In considering the AR(2) process, it is interesting to examine the effect of introducing an additional first-order factor to the model in Equation 3.68. Figure 3.9 shows autocorrelations for the models

(1 − 0.95B)(1 − λB)X_t = a_t,    (3.69)

for λ = 0, 0.7, 0.9, and 0.95, while Figure 3.10 shows realizations of length n = 100 with σ_a^2 = 1 from each model. When λ = 0 the model in Equation 3.69 is an AR(1) model, and Figures 3.9a and 3.10a were previously shown in Figures 3.4a and 3.5a, respectively, although Figure 3.10a is plotted on a different scale than Figure 3.5a. The realizations in Figure 3.10 were each generated with σ_a^2 = 1 and using the same seed for the random number generator, so that differences among the realizations are due strictly to the differing autocorrelation functions and process variances. It should be noticed that the existence of the second real root strengthens the behavior seen in Figures 3.4a and 3.5a for the AR(1) model. For example, as λ increases, the autocorrelation functions damp more slowly, and therefore the realizations have more pronounced runs on the vertical axis and a resulting higher variance. The process standard deviations are σ_X = 3.2, 10.0, 26.3, and 45.3, respectively, and if we consider X_t as “signal” and a_t as “noise,” then the signal-to-noise ratio, σ_X^2 / σ_a^2 increases as λ increases. Also note that when λ = 0.95, the model has repeated roots. (The data set in Figure 3.10d can be found in `fig3.10d` in `tswge`.) The examples given here show behavior of the realizations and autocorrelations as λ → 0.95. Clearly, the behavior of the autocorrelations and realizations for the case of equal roots is similar to the behavior when the roots are unequal yet close together. In this book, we typically consider cases in which the roots are not repeated (except for certain nonstationary and seasonal models).

The variance of the process and the “signal-to-noise ratio” increase as λ → 1. In all these cases, the spectral densities have a peak at f = 0.5.

3. Characteristic equation has a positive and negative real root: In Figure 3.11, a realization of length n = 100, the autocorrelations, and the spectral density are shown for the AR(2) process (1 − 0.95B)(1 + 0.7B)X_t = a_t.
