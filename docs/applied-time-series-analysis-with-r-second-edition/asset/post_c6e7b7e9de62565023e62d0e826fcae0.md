## 11.4.1 Calculating Autocovariances

The autocorrelation function of a Gegenbauer process with u = 1 (i.e., a fractional model) is given in Theorem 11.4(5)(a), and the autocorrelation for a Gegenbauer process with u = −1 is given in Theorem 11.4(5)(b). No simple closed form expression for the autocovariance or autocorrelation exists for a Gegenbauer process with |u| < 1, but from Equation 2.7 it follows that

(11.24)

γ_k = σ_a^2 ∑_{j=0}^{∞} ψ_j ψ_{j+k}
= σ_a^2 ∑_{j=0}^{∞} C_j^{(λ)}(u) C_{j+k}^{(λ)}(u).

One technique for calculating the autocovariances is to truncate the series in Equation 11.24 after a sufficient number of terms. Table 11.1 (given by Ford, 1991) illustrates that, depending on the parameters, the terms in this series may not damp quickly enough to make truncation feasible.

The table considers the problem of using Equation 11.24 to calculate γ₀ for the case u = 1. In this case using Theorem 11.1(4) and the fact that d = 2λ, the sum of the infinite series in Equation 11.24 is known in closed form to be γ₀ = Γ(−4λ + 1)/[Γ(−2λ + 1)]². Table 11.1 compares values of the infinite sum in Equation 11.24 to the results of truncating the sum after 200,000 terms and after 10 million terms. In the table it is seen that for λ = 0.05 and λ = 0.125 the two truncated sums approximate the infinite sum quite well. However, in the near-nonstationary case λ = 0.24, the convergence is extremely slow and a sum to 10 million terms is a very poor approximation! Chung (1996a) expressed γₖ for a (1-factor) Gegenbauer process as a recursion involving Legendre functions. Using Equations 1.28 and 11.19, an expression for the autocovariance of a Gegenbauer process is given as the inverse Fourier transform of the spectrum,

γ_k = ∫_{-0.5}^{0.5} e^{2π i f k} P(f) df. (11.25)

In the 1-factor Gegenbauer setting, Ford (1991) examined the use of Levin
and Sidi’s (1981) d-transform for increasing the rate of convergence of the sum in Equation 11.24 and compared the resulting approximations with those obtained by numerically integrating in Equation 11.25. As noted, $P_X(f)$ is unbounded at $f_G = (\cos^{-1} u_j)/(2\pi)$ Ford (1991) concluded that careful numerical integration using Equation 11.25 is the preferred method. Based on these findings, we approximate the autocovariances of $k$-factor Gegenbauer and $k$-factor GARMA models by numerically integrating the integral in Equation 11.25. It should be noted that both methods produce approximate autocorrelations, and for some parameter configurations these can be relatively poor.

**Using R:** The tswge functions `true.farma.aut.wge` and `true.garma.aut.wge` calculate the true autocovariances and autocorrelations for FARMA and GARMA processes, respectively. See Appendix 11A for more details.

## 11.4.2 Generating Realizations

Gray et al. (1989, 1994) generate realizations from Gegenbauer and GARMA models using the strategy of truncating the general linear process given in Equation 11.11 to obtain

$X_t \approx \mu + \sum_{h=0}^{M} C_h^{(\lambda)}(u) a_{t-h}$

(11.26)

using $M = 290,000$. Woodward et al. (1998) suggest a more efficient algorithm due to Ramsey (1974) based on conditional expectations and variances of $X_t$ given $X_0, X_1, ..., X_{t-1}$. However, the more efficient method involves the true autocovariances, and our experience is that due to the computational difficulties described earlier, some of the conditional quantities become corrupted. Consequently, the realizations shown here will be based on the truncated general linear process.

If $X_t$ satisfies the model in Equation 11.20 with $\mu = 0$, then $Y_t = \prod_{j=1}^k (1 - 2u_j B + B^2)^{\lambda_j/2} X_t$ satisfies the ARMA$(p,q)$ model $\varphi(B)Y_t = \theta(B)a_t$. Woodward et al. (1998) recommend the use of a grid-search procedure for finding ML estimates of the parameters of the $k$-factor GARMA model. We assume that $k$ is known. In practice, $k$ will be estimated by identifying peaks in a nonparametric spectral estimator, or by examining factor tables. Then, based on positions and magnitudes of the peaks in the preliminary spectral estimate, a grid of possible values for $\lambda_1$, $u_1$, ..., $\lambda_k$, $u_k$ is selected to be used in the search. For each set of values $\lambda_1$, $u_1$, ..., $\lambda_k$, $u_k$ in the grid search, the data are transformed to obtain

$$Y_t = \prod_{j=1}^k (1 - 2u_j B + B^2)^{\lambda_j/2} (X_t - \overline{X}) \tag{11.27}$$
$$\approx \prod_{j=1}^k \left\{ \sum_{h=0}^{t+K-1} C_h^{(-\lambda_j/2)} (u_j)^h (X_{t-h} - \overline{X}) \right\}.$$

In order to make the transformation in Equation 11.27, $K$ values $X_{-1}, X_{-2}, \dots, X_{-K+1}$ are obtained by backcasting as discussed in Section 7.4 using a high-order AR model. The resulting process, $Y_t$, is modeled as an ARMA$(p,q)$, an associated likelihood function is obtained, and the combination of $\lambda_1$, $u_1$, ..., $\lambda_k$, $u_k$ associated with the largest likelihood value is the approximate ML estimator (for the current values of $p$ and $q$). To identify $p$ and $q$ this procedure is performed for each $p = 1, \dots, P$ and $q = 1, \dots, Q$, and the ARMA-based AIC associated with the ML estimator is obtained in each case. The model (i.e., $p$, $q$, $\lambda_1$, $u_1$, ..., $\lambda_k$, $u_k$) associated with the minimum AIC value is selected. See Hosking (1984), Gray et al. (1989, 1994), and Woodward et al. (1998).
