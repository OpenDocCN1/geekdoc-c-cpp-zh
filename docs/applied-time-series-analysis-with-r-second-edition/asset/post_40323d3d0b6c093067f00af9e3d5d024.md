The *cross-correlation*, $\rho_{ij}(k)$, is defined by

$$\rho_{ij}(k)=\frac{\gamma_{ij}(k)}{\sqrt{\gamma_{ii}(0)\gamma_{jj}(0)}}.$$

and, correspondingly, the correlation matrix $\boldsymbol{\rho}(k)$, $k=0,1,\ldots$, is given by

$$\boldsymbol{\rho}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{11}(k)&\cdots&\rho_{1m}(k)\\ \vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ \rho_{m1}(k)&\cdots&\rho_{mm}(k)\end{pmatrix}. \tag{10.3}$$

## NOTES:

- 1. The autocorrelations for variable $X_{tj}$ are the $j$th diagonal elements of the matrices $\boldsymbol{\rho}(k)$, $k=0,\ \pm1,\ldots$.
- 2. The values $\rho_{ij}(0)$ of $\boldsymbol{\rho}(0)$ measure what is referred to as the concurrent or contemporaneous linear relationship between $X_{ti}$ and $X_{tj}$. Note that $\boldsymbol{\rho}(0)$ has 1's along the diagonal.
- 3. A useful expression for $\boldsymbol{\rho}(k)$ is

$$\boldsymbol{\rho}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{11}^{-1/2}&0&\cdots&0\\0&\gamma_{22}^{-1/2}&0&\vdots\\\vdots&0&\ddots&0\\0&\cdots&0&\gamma_{mm}^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}\Gamma(k)\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{11}^{-1/2}&0&\cdots&0\\0&\gamma_{22}^{-1/2}&0&\vdots\\\vdots&0&\ddots&0\\0&\cdots&0&\gamma_{mm}^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}. \tag{10.4}$$
- 4. Note that for autocorrelations, the definitions $\gamma(k)=E[(X_t-\mu)(X_{t+k}-\mu)]$ and $\gamma(k)=E[(X_{t+k}-\mu)(X_t-\mu)]$ are equivalent, and also $\gamma(k)=\gamma(-k)$. These properties are not shared with the cross-correlation. We mentioned above that $\gamma_{ij}(k)=\gamma_{ji}(-k)$ (notice the change in the order of the subscripts).

*Caution:* We emphasize here that two definitions are commonly used for

the cross covariance

- a. $\gamma_{ij}(k)=E[(X_{t,i}-\mu_i)(X_{t+k,j}-\mu_j)]$ (the definition given in [Equation 10.2](#), which is used here, in Box, et al. (2008), and others)
- b. $\gamma_{ij}^{(a)}(k)=E[(X_{t+k,i}-\mu_i)(X_{t,j}-\mu_j)]$ used by Shumway and Stoffer (2010), Tsay (2002), Brockwell and Davis (1991, 2002), among others.

Of course, the two definitions of cross-covariance lead to two corresponding definitions of cross-correlation. It is critical to note that these two definitions are *not equivalent*, and we caution readers to always check for the definition of the cross-covariance being used before interpreting cross-covariance or cross-correlation results!

**Using R:** Base R function `ccf(xi,xj,type = 'covariance')` calculates $\gamma_{ij}^{(a)}(k)$ (where xi and xj denote the ith and jth time series, respectively). In order to compute $\gamma_{ij}(k)$ as defined in [Equation 10.2](#), then you can use the call statement `ccf(xj,xi)`. In either case, using `type = 'correlation'` computes the associated cross-correlations.

Definition 10.3 extends the concept of white noise to the multivariate setting.

## Definition 10.3

The multivariate time series $\{a_t; t\in T\}$ is said to be multivariate white noise if

- 1. $E(a_t)=0$ where $0$ is an $m$-component vector of 0’s.
- 2. $\rho_a(k)=0,\ k\ne 0$, where in this case $0$ denotes an $m\times m$ matrix of 0’s.

It should be noted that Definition 10.3 imposes no restrictions on $\rho_a(0)$. Definition 10.4 extends the concept of a general linear process defined in Definition 2.1 to the multivariate case, and Definition 10.5 defines the spectrum of a multivariate time series.

## Definition 10.4

A multivariate time series { X_t ; t = 0, ± 1, ± 2, ... } is said to be a causal

and stationary multivariate general linear process if it is given by

$$X_t - \boldsymbol{\mu} = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_j \mathbf{a}_{t-j},$$ (10.6)

where $\mathbf{a}_t$ is multivariate white noise and the $\Psi'_j$s are $m \times m$ matrices, such that $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_0 = \mathbf{I}$ (the $m \times m$ identity matrix) and whose components are absolutely summable. That is, letting $\psi_j(k,l)$ denote the element in the $k$th row and $l$th column of $\Psi_j$, then $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} |\psi_j(k,l)| < \infty$ for $1 \le k, l \le m.$
