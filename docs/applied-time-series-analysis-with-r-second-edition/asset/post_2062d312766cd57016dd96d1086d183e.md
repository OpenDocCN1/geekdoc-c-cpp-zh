## 10.4 Nonstationary VARMA Processes

The ARUMA($p,d,q$) processes discussed in [Section 5.2](#) can be generalized to the multivariate case. Specifically, we consider the model

$$\Phi(B)\lambda(B)(X_t-\mu)=\Theta(B)a_t,$$

where

- 1.

$$\lambda(B)=\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda_1(B) & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
0 & \lambda_2(B) & \ddots & \vdots \\
\vdots & \ddots & \ddots & 0 \\
0 & \cdots & 0 & \lambda_m(B)
\end{pmatrix}.$$

and $\lambda_j(z)=0$, $j = 1, ..., m$, has all its roots on the unit circle.

- 2. The roots of the determinantal equations $|\Phi(z)| = 0$ and $|\Theta(z)| = 0$ lie outside the unit circle.

For example, it might be that $\lambda_1(B) = (1 - B)$, $\lambda_2(B) = 1 - \sqrt{3} B + B^2$, $\lambda_3(B) = (1 - B)^2(1 + B^2)$, etc. The model in Equation 10.43 is called a VARUMA model and Equation 10.43 implies that the series $Y_{t j} = \lambda_j(B)(X_t - \mu_j)$ is a univariate stationary process and additionally that the process $Y_t = (Y_{t1}, ..., Y_{tm})'$ satisfies the stationary VARMA(p,q) model $\Phi(B)(Y - \mu_Y) = \Theta(B)a_t$, where $\mu_Y = (\mu_{Y t1}, ..., \mu_{Y tm})'$.

The VARUMA model that has received the most attention is the particular case in which

$\lambda(B) = \begin{pmatrix} (1 - B)^{d1} & 0 & ... & 0 \\ 0 & (1 - B)^{d2} & \vdots \\ \vdots & \ddots & 0 \\ 0 & ... & 0 & (1 - B)^{dm} \end{pmatrix}$, (10.44)

which is the multivariate counterpart to the ARIMA(p,d,q) model. Notice that if $X_{ij}$ is a stationary process, then $d_j = 0$, that is, no differencing is needed to stationarize $X_{ij}$.

### 10.5.1 Testing for Independence of Two Stationary Time Series

We consider the setting in which $X_{t1}$ and $X_{t2}$ are stationary univariate time series that each have a general linear process representation for which the $\psi$-weights are absolutely convergent. Let $\hat{\rho}_{12}(k)$ be the estimated cross-correlation at lag $k$ as defined in [Equation 10.12](#). It can be shown that $\hat{\rho}_{12}(k)$ is asymptotically normal with mean 0 and variance $(AV_{12})$ given by

$$AV_{12}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\rho_{11}(j)\rho_{22}(j).$$

See Brockwell and Davis (1991).

If it is desired to test $H_0:\rho_{12}(k)=0$ versus $H_a:\rho_{12}(k)\ne0$, it can be seen that, the asymptotic variance in [Equation 10.45](#) will not in general be known. Note however that if either or both of $X_{t1}$ and $X_{t2}$ is white noise, then

asymptotic variance becomes $AV_{12}=1/n$. Haugh (1976) recommended a procedure for testing $H_0 : \rho_{12}(k) = 0$ that involves the following steps:

- 1. Fit an ARMA(p,q) model to each of $X_{t1}$ and $X_{t2}$ (these two models may be of different orders $p_1$, $q_1$, $p_2$, and $q_2$)
- 2. Calculate the residual series $\hat{a}_{t1}$ and $\hat{a}_{t2}$, $t = 1, ..., n$ based on each model fit
- 3. Calculate the estimated cross-correlations, $\hat{\rho}_{12}^{(\hat{a})}(k)$, $k = 1, ..., K$ between the two residual series
- 4. For each $k$, conclude that $\hat{\rho}_{12}^{(\hat{a})}(k)$ is significantly different from zero at the $\alpha = 0.05$ level if $|\hat{\rho}_{12}^{(\hat{a})}(k)| > 2(1/\sqrt{n})$.

This procedure is very similar to the one described in Section 8.1 for testing $H_0 : \rho(k) = 0$ versus $H_a : \rho(k) \neq 0$. As in that case, it should be noted that the significance level $\alpha = 0.05$ applies separately for each $k$, and it would not be unusual for about 5% of the $\hat{\rho}_{12}^{(\hat{a})}(k)$'s to exceed $\pm2(1/\sqrt{n})$ when the null hypothesis is true.
