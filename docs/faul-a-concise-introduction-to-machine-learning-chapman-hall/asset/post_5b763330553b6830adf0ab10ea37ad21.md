
- 3. E-step: Evaluate the responsibilities \(p(z_{n,k} = 1 | \mathbf{v}_n)\) for \(n = 1, \dots, N\) and \(k = 1, \dots, K\).

- 4. M-step: Maximize

```
\(\sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{n=1}^N p(z_{n,k} = 1 | \mathbf{v}_n) \log \left( p(z_{n,k} = 1) p(\mathbf{v}_n | z_{n,k} = 1) \right)\)
```

with respect to the parameters of \(p_k(\mathbf{v}) = p(\mathbf{v} | z_{n,k} = 1)\) and mixing coefficients \(\pi_k = p(z_k = 1)\).

- 5. Terminate, if all changes are below the convergence threshold. Otherwise return to step 3

This method is known as the Expectation-Maximization algorithm or EM algorithm for short. Each step increases the logarithm of the data likelihood. Listing 6.3 uses the internal implementation of the EM algorithm in MATLAB within the call to fit a Gaussian Mixture. We revisit the Expectation-Maximization technique in the context of regression where we consider continuous latent variables. The EM algorithm is widely used and has many extensions. For an overview of those see [29].

## 6.5 Bayesian Mixture Models

The previous sections have treated the mixing coefficients \(\pi_k\) and the parameters of the probability distribution \(p_k\) of the \(k^{th}\) process for \(k = 1, \dots, K\) as constant unknown parameters, whose values need to be determined by maximizing the likelihood. We now take a Bayesian approach.

The prior assumption is that the vector \(\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K)\) follows a Dirichlet distribution with parameter \(\alpha = (\alpha/K, \dots, \alpha/K)^T\). For example, if \(\alpha = K\), then \(\alpha = (1, \dots, 1)^T\) and the probability distribution is the uniform distribution over the simplex in which \(\pi\) lies. So all possibilities for \(\pi\) are equally likely. As \(\alpha\) increases, the resulting Dirichlet probability density functions get more and more peaked at \(\pi = (1/K, \dots, 1/K)^T\) with any other vectors becoming less likely. The effect is illustrated in Figure 6.5. When \(\alpha = K\) in Figures 6.5a and 6.5b, the cluster sizes can be very different. As \(\alpha\) increases (Figures 6.5c and 6.5d), the clusters become similar in size. Their sizes approach \(N/K\).

Similarly, the probability distributions \(p_k\) are drawn themselves from a probability distribution. It is a distribution over distributions, known as base distribution \(G_0\). For example, if each process is a normal distribution with its own mean \(\mu_k\) and covariance matrix \(\Sigma_k\), these can be drawn from the normal inverse Wishart distribution, which has four parameters:

- the location vector \(\mathbf{m}\) lying in the feature space,
- the mean fraction \(\lambda\),
- the inverse scale matrix \(\Psi\), which has to be symmetric and positive definite,
- and \(\nu\), which has to be at least the number of dimensions \(d\) of the feature space and regulates the degrees of freedom.

The notation is \((\mu_k, \Sigma_k) \sim \text{NIW}(\mathbf{m}, \lambda, \Psi, \nu)\).

The covariance matrices $\Sigma_k$ follow the inverse Wishart distribution given by $\Psi$ and $\nu$, $\Sigma_k \sim \mathcal{W}^{-1}(\Psi, \nu)$. It has the probability density function

$$\mathcal{W}^{-1}(\Sigma_k | \Psi, \nu) = \frac{|\Psi|^{\nu/2}}{2^{\nu d/2} \Gamma_d(\frac{\nu}{2})} |\Sigma_k|^{-(\nu+d+1)/2} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \text{tr}(\Psi \Sigma_k^{-1}) \right), \quad (6.10)$$

where $|\cdot|$ denotes the determinant and $\text{tr}(\cdot)$ denotes the trace of a matrix. $\Gamma_d$ is the multivariate gamma function.

The expectation of the inverse Wishart distribution is given by

$$\mathbb{E}[\Sigma_k] = \frac{\Psi}{\nu - d - 1}.$$

The variance of each element $\Sigma_{ij}$ of $\Sigma$ is calculated as

$$\text{var}[\Sigma_{ij}] = \frac{(\nu - d + 1)\Psi_{ij}^2 + (\nu - d + 1)\Psi_{ii}\Psi_{jj}}{(\nu - d)(\nu - d - 1)^2(\nu - d - 3)} = \text{var}[\Sigma_{ji}],$$

since $\Psi$ is symmetric. On the diagonal, that is $i = j$, this simplifies to

$$\text{var}[\Sigma_{ii}] = \frac{2\Psi_{ii}^2}{(\nu - d - 1)^2(\nu - d - 3)}.$$

Since $\nu$ appears several times in the denominator and it controls the variability in the generated covariances and in turn in the different data sets which are generated. The variability decreases as $\nu$ increases. Figures 6.6 and 6.7 show

The inverse Wishart distribution \(W^{-1}(\Sigma|\Psi, \nu)\) is the conjugate prior for the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) of a multivariate normal distribution with a known mean. We can assume that the mean of this distribution is zero, since we can re-centre the data at zero by subtracting the mean from all samples.

![](img/d3c00252180686426c9e63ab889b535b_190_0.png)

Figure 6.7: Data generated for \(\nu = 10\).

Let \(\mathcal{D} = \{ \mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_N \}\) be draws from \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)\). The likelihood of this data is given by

```
\(\prod_{n=1}^N \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^d |\Sigma|}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{v}_n^T \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{v}_n \right) = (2\pi)^{-dN/2} |\Sigma|^{-N/2} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=1}^N \mathbf{v}_n^T \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{v}_n \right).\)
```

The posterior distribution of \(\Sigma\) is proportional to the product of the likelihood and the prior probability density function as given in (6.10) by Bayes' rule. Because of the proportionality, we drop all multiplicative constants in both. These are the factors \((2\pi)^{-dN/2}\), \(|\Psi|^{\nu/2}\), \(2^{-\nu d/2}\) and \(1/\Gamma_d(\nu/2)\). Hence, the posterior is proportional to

```
\(|\Sigma|^{-N/2} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=1}^N \mathbf{v}_n^T \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{v}_n \right) |\Sigma|^{-(\nu+d+1)/2} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \text{tr}(\Psi \Sigma^{-1}) \right).\)
```

![](img/d3c00252180686426c9e63ab889b535b_191_0.png)

Figure 6.8: Data generated for various values of \(\lambda\).

To simplify this expression, we use properties of determinants and traces of matrices. The product of determinants of two matrices is the determinant of the product of matrices. Therefore, \(|\Sigma|^{-N/2}|\Sigma|^{-(\nu+d+1)/2} = |\Sigma|^{-(N+\nu+d+1)/2}\). The expression \(\mathbf{v}_n^T \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{v}_n\) is a scalar and the trace of a scalar is the scalar itself. Further, the trace is invariant to a cyclic permutation of the order of multiplication. Hence, \(\mathbf{v}_n^T \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{v}_n = \text{tr}(\mathbf{v}_n^T \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{v}_n) = \text{tr}(\mathbf{v}_n \mathbf{v}_n^T \Sigma^{-1})\). Using these identities, the posterior is proportional to

```
\(\begin{aligned} |\Sigma|^{-(N+\nu+d+1)/2} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \left[ \sum_{n=1}^N \text{tr}(\mathbf{v}_n \mathbf{v}_n^T \Sigma^{-1}) + \text{tr}(\Psi \Sigma^{-1}) \right] \right) = \\ |\Sigma|^{-(N+\nu+d+1)/2} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \text{tr} \left( \left( \Psi + \sum_{n=1}^N \mathbf{v}_n \mathbf{v}_n^T \right) \Sigma^{-1} \right) \right), \end{aligned}\)
```

since the sum of traces of matrices is the trace of the sum of matrices. Apart from the normalizing constant, this is exactly the probability density function of \(\mathcal{W}^{-1}(\Sigma | \Psi + \sum_{n=1}^N \mathbf{v}_n \mathbf{v}_n^T, \nu + N)\). To summarize, after seeing the data, the inverse scale matrix is updated to \(\Psi + \sum_{n=1}^N \mathbf{v}_n \mathbf{v}_n^T\) and the degrees of freedom become \(\nu + N\). Since this increases, the estimator for the distribution describing \(\Sigma\) becomes tighter, the more data samples are seen. The least informative choice for \(\nu\) in the prior is \(\nu = d\).

Having drawn \(\Sigma_k\), \(\mu_k\) is drawn from a normal distribution with mean \(\mathbf{m}\) and covariance matrix \(\frac{1}{\kappa} \Sigma_k\). The parameter \(\lambda\) controls the spacing of the generated means \(\mu_k\). Figure 6.8 illustrates the effect of \(\lambda = 1, 1/3, 1/5\) for \(\alpha = 10K, \mathbf{m} = (0, 0)^T\). The previous \(\Psi\) and \(\nu = 6\). As \(\lambda\) decreases, the clusters separate. As \(\lambda\) increases, they overlap more and more. Figures 6.5 to 6.8 were generated by using the function in Listing 6.4.

To summarize, the data is generated following the distributions:

```
\(\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_n | \mathbf{z}_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \Sigma_k) \\ p(z_n = k) = \pi_k, \\ \pi \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha), \\ \mu_k, \Sigma_k \sim G_0 = \text{NIW}(\mathbf{m}, \lambda, \Psi, \nu). \end{aligned}\)
```
