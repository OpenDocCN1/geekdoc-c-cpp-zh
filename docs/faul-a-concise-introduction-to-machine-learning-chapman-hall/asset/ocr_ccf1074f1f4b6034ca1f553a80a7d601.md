where all constant factors were dropped due to the proportional sign. The posterior has again the functional form of a Dirichlet distribution. Incorporating the factor such that the posterior integrates to one leads to

```
f(\mu|\mathbf{m}, \alpha) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0 + N)}{\Gamma(\alpha_1 + m_1) \cdots \Gamma(\alpha_K + m_K)} \prod_{k=1}^K \mu_k^{m_k + \alpha_k - 1} = \text{Dir}(\mu|\mathbf{m} + \alpha),
```

where \(\alpha_0 = \alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_K\).

As before with the beta distribution, the knowledge gained from the experiments is given by \(\mathbf{m}\) and modifies the location and the mean and the shape of the distribution. The parameters \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) in the prior beta distribution and \(\alpha\) in the prior Dirichlet distribution can be viewed as initial guesses of the true distribution or fictional trial runs informed by expert knowledge and experience.

The probability of a particular outcome \(x_k = 1\) is

```
p(x_k = 1|\mathcal{D}) = \int p(x_k = 1|\mu) f(\mu|\mathbf{m}, \alpha) d\mu,
```

where the integral is over the simplex, where \(\mu\) must lie. Since \(p(x_k = 1|\mu) = \mu_k\), this becomes

```
p(x_k = 1|\mathcal{D}) = \mathbb{E}[\mu_k|\mathbf{m}, \alpha] = \frac{m_k + \alpha_k}{N + \alpha_0}. \quad (2.23)
```

As \(N\) grows, \(p(x_k = 1|\mathcal{D})\) approaches the maximum likelihood estimator. Again, the above result no longer depends on \(\mu\) and therefore \(\mu\) is a latent variable.

We have seen two examples of conjugate probability distributions and will see more. For example, if the mean of a normal distribution is the unknown parameter, the conjugate prior is also a normal distribution. For a more thorough treatment of conjugacy see [16].
