Jesper Juul, who, as we have said, sees the Wii and its mimetic interface as being at the center of the casual revolution, has conducted interesting Internet experiments along these lines. He shows that if you search Flickr, for example, for “Gears of War 2,” you get pictures of in-game battle, among other things. Whereas if you search “Wii Tennis,” you get a much higher percentage of pictures of people playing Wii Sports, moving around in their living rooms and waving their arms around.³⁰ The focus is on the player rather than on the screen.

We’ll come back to Juul’s Flickr-mining experiment later, but for now we want to look a little more closely at the idea, shared by Juul and other critics, that the Wii by design shifts the center of attention to the player’s body in physical space, the living room in front of the television. Recall that Will Wright has said essentially the same, and that the Wii is all about nonimmersive gaming, in which most of “the entertainment is not happening on the screen,” it’s happening out in the room with your friends. And Wright compares this to other mimetic interface games (as Juul calls them), like Guitar Hero or Rock Band, in which “most of the entertainment is off the screen, it’s in the social group around you.”³¹ Similarly, what Bart Simon has referred to as the “material imaginary” when playing the Wii foregrounds the importance of the “physical context of play,” even to the point of “gestural excess,” where—“especially in social settings”—players act out more than is necessary to accumulate the controls.³² The fact that mimetic interface games like those played on the Wii “allow players to play from the perspective of their physical presence in the real world,” as Juul says, marks a shift from 3D game space to “concrete player space.”³³ We would add that the related shifts in game mechanics, the setting of gameplay, and the ascendancy of certain genres and styles of play over others (namely, the rise of casual gaming) can all be seen at work in Nintendo’s strategy in building as well as marketing the Wii as a platform.
