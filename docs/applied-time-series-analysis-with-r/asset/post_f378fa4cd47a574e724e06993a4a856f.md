## 6.10 Forecasts Based on Signal-Plus-Noise Models

We next consider the signal-plus-noise model

$X_t = s_t + Z_t,$ (6.56)

discussed in Section 5.1 where $s_t$ is a deterministic signal, such as a linear or sinusoidal component, typically neither, and $Z_t$ is a stationary process that follows an $AR(p)$ model. Forecasts from these models can be obtained with slight modifications from the techniques considered previously. Consider, for example, the case in which forecasts are desired based on a realization from the model in Equation 6.56, $s_t = b_0 + b_it$, and $b_0$ and $b_i$ are considered to be known. Since $Z_t = X_t - b_0 - b_it$, it is a stationary process, forecasts $\hat{Z}_t(\ell)$ can be computed on the $AR(p)$ model satisfied by $Z_t$, and converted to forecasts for $X_{t+\ell}$ using the formula $\hat{X}_t(\ell) = b_0 + b_it + \hat{Z}_t(\ell)$. Of course, in practice, the model satisfied by $Z$ are known. As has been the practice throughout this chapter, forecasts and forecast errors are calculated based on the assumption that the model parameters are known, although these parameters will be in all likelihood to be estimated from the data. Parameter estimation will be discussed in Chapter 7.

The current setting in which the line (estimated or true) is assumed to be the true signal, then forecast limits for $\hat{X}_t(\ell)$ are $b_0 + b_it + \hat{Z}_t(\ell) \pm 1.96\sigma_Z(\sum_{k=0}^\ell \psi_k^2)^{V/2}$. The $\psi$-weights and $\hat{\sigma}_Z^*$ are based on the $AR(p)$ model fit to $Z_t$. It follows that the same strategy will produce forecasts and forecast errors for any deterministic signal $s_t$. ```

and

```
$X_t = b_0+A_1\cos(2\pi f_tt+A_2)+Z_t,\quad (6.61)$
```

where $1-\lambda_1z+z^2=0$ has a pair of complex roots on the unit circle with the associated system frequency $f_0$ and where $Z_t$ is zero-mean white noise. We consider the case in which $f_0=1/12$ for both models, and case realizations from these models will be cyclical with a period of about 12. As in the case where $s_i$ is a line, long-term forecasts from Equation 6.60 and 6.61 should be interpreted with caution. Forecasts from each model have a fixed phase shift and amplitude even though these values are determined very differently as illustrated in the preceding discussion. For, example, the sunspot data shown in Figure 1.23a. Using either of these models to produce long-term forecasts of sunspot activity is problematic for at least two reasons. First, the sunspot oscillations are asymmetric and fail to account for this behavior and may, for example, (1990) will not be able to account for this behavior and makes no sense. Sinusoidal forecasts use a nonlinear time series model to the asymmetry. However, a more subtle feature of the data is that there are small changes in the period length through time. As a consequence, long-term forecasts based on a fixed phase, no matter how, eventually begin to predict a trough where a peak occurs, etc. For this reason, forecasts that eventually damp the mean (based on a stationary model fit) may be more reflective of the information available concerning sunspot values in the more distant future.
