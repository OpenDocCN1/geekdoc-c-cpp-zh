
# Nonstationary Time Series Models

NOTE: As might be deduced from this example, there is a fundamental difference between the behavior of $\rho_s$ for a nonstationary process and for a stationary process, $\rho_s$ is influenced to some degree by all the factors in the model, both AR and MA. However, in a nonstationary process, $\rho_s$ is affected only by the roots on the unit circle, and in fact, only by those of highest multiplicity. In this regard, we have the theorem that was first suggested by Gray et al. (1978) and proven in general by Finley (1978), Quinn (1980), and Gray and Woodward (1981).

## Theorem 5.1 (Findley–Quinn Theorem)

Suppose that the following are true:

- 1. $X_t$ is an ARMA$(p+d, q)$ with $d$ roots “close” to the unit circle
- 2. Of the $d$ roots in (a), $j$ have highest multiplicity $\ell$

Then, $\rho_s^*$ (the limiting value of $\rho_s$ as the $d$ roots approach the unit circle uniformly) exists, and for each $k=0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots$, $\rho_s^*$ satisfies the linear difference equation of order $j$ for $j > 0$.

$$(1-\zeta_1B-\dots-\zeta_dB')\rho_s^*=0, \quad (5.14)$$

where $j$ is specified in (b). The roots of $\zeta(z)=0$ are all on the unit circle (i.e. have modulus 1), and $\zeta(B)$ is the operator formed from the product of the nonstationary factors of highest multiplicity in $\ell$.

# Nonstationary Time Series Models

### 5.2.2 Cyclical Models

In Example 5.7, it is seen that for the ARUMA($p,d,q$) process, the limiting autocorrelations, $\rho_k$, do not decay to zero. In some cases they stay fixed at 1, and in others they are periodic. In particular, if all of the $d$ roots of $\lambda(z)=0$ in an ARUMA($p,d,q$) process are equal to 1, the process is noncyclic. On the other hand, if at least one of the $d$ roots of highest multiplicity is –1 or real, then we will refer to the process as a cyclical ARUMA($p,d,q$) process. The simplest ARUMA process is $(1+B)X_t=a_t$. In this case, $\rho_k=(-1)^k$ so that the autocorrelation is periodic with system frequency $f_0=0.5$.

## 5.3 Multiplicative Seasonal ARUMA $(p,d,q) \times (P_s, D_s, Q_s)_s$ Process

As we have extended the ARMA(p,q) model to allow roots on the unit circle, we also extend the multiplicative seasonal model to include roots on the unit

circle. A zero-mean process, $X_t$, is said to follow a multiplicative seasonal ARUMA$(p,d,q) \times (P_s, D_s, Q_s)$ model if it can be written in the form

$$\phi(B)\Phi(B^s)\lambda(B)\lambda(B^s)X_t = \theta(B)\Theta(B^s)a_t, \quad (1.16)$$

the roots of $\phi(z)=0, \Phi(z^s)=0, \theta(z)=0,$ and $\Theta(z^s)=0$ all lie outside the unit circle, the roots of $\lambda(z)=0$ and $\lambda(z^s)=0$ on the unit circle are

Using Theorem 5.1, it follows that for the multiplicative seasonal model $\phi(B)\Phi(B)(1-B^s)X_t = \theta(B)\Theta(B^s)a_t$, for which the only roots on the unit circle are the roots of $1-z^s=0$, the limiting autocorrelations are given by

$$\rho_k'=1, \quad k=0, \pm s, \pm2s,\dots$$
$$=0, \quad \text{elsewhere.}$$

In this example $\lambda(B^s)=1-B^s$ and $D_s=1$.
A example of a nonstationary multiplicative seasonal model is given by

$$\phi(B)(1-B^4)(X_t-\mu) = \theta(B)a_t, \quad (1.17)$$

for which the limiting autocorrelations are given by

$$\rho_k'=1, \quad k=0, \pm4, \pm8,\dots$$
$$=0, \quad \text{elsewhere.}$$

In model in Equation 5.17 $d=0$ and $D_s=1$.

## EXAMPLE 5.9: THE AIRLINE MODEL

The monthly data for the number of international airline passengers (log scale) have been well analyzed as a seasonal data set with a trending behavior. A variety of models have been proposed for this data set, and as a group, these are referred to as “airline models.” We will use here for this purpose an AR($($p, d, q$)($p_{ij}, Q_{ij}$) model of the form an

$$\phi(B)\Phi(B^s)(1-B)(1-B^s)(X_t - \mu) = \theta(B)\Theta(B^s)a_t.$$

Note that this model has 13 roots on the unit circle: a seasonal factor $1 - B$ and 12 months with the characteristic factor equation $(1-z)(1-z^{12}) = 0$ would be the same as the one in Table 5.5, except that it would include two factors of $1 - B$. Thus, in Theorem 5.1 we will find that $d=13, j=1, \ell=2$. Notice that the autocorrelations, $\rho_t$, of such a process are given

$$\rho_t^2 = 1.$$

since the root of 1 is repeated twice. These will be discussed in detail in Section 8.3.5.3 as we analyze seasonal data.
