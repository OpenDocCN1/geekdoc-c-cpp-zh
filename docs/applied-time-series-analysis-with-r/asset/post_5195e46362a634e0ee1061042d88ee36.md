To begin this discussion, we recall the Finley–Quinn theorem satisfy (Theorem 5.1) that if $p > d$, the extended autocorrelations, $\rho_t$, satisfy a lower order difference equation than $p$. It was also seen in Example 3.12 that the stationary components are often “hidden” by nonstationary components, and in Example 7c, we noted that roots near the unit circle can cause parameter estimation problems.

As a consequence of the comments in the previous paragraph, it follows that model identification in the presence of nonstationarity or near nonstationarity must be done carefully. We make the following general comments in this regard:

General comments:

- 1. ML (and Burg/TT) estimates are better than YW (or extended YW) in the nonstationary or near stationary case. (See the estimation results in Tables 7.6 and 7.7)
- 2. Model ID techniques (such as the Box–Jenkins procedure) that are based on YW-type estimation can be misleading in the presence of nonstationary or near-nonstationary models. AIC using ML (or Burg) estimates for strictly AR models will not be as susceptible to nonstationarity or near nonstationarity.
- 3. The decision whether to fix a stationary model or a stationary model that has roots to the unit circle will often depend on the application, that is, the physical problem being considered.
- 4. YW and Burg estimates of the parameters of an AR model will always produce stationary models.
- 5. Indications of nonstationarity or near nonstationarity include
  a. Slowly damping autocorrelations.
  b. Realizations exhibiting wandering or strong cyclic behavior.

Because of items (1) and (2), we will use ML (and sometimes YW in the AR case) in our examples. We will further discuss these ideas in the following material.

### 8.3.1 Including a Nonstationary Factor in the Model

In the following we discuss methods that aid in the decision concerning whether to use a stationary component in the model. We first discuss the general procedure for analysis if the decision is made to include a nonstationary component, as $1 - B$, in the model for $X_t$. In this case, the model is $(1 - B)\phi(X_t - \mu_X) = \theta(B)$. The next step is to transform the data to stationarity. In this case, we calculate $Y_t = (1 - B)X_t = X_t - X_{t-1}$. If $1 - B$ is the only nonstationary component in the model, then $Y_t$ satisfies the ARMA(p,q) model $\phi(B)(Y_t - \mu_Y) = \theta(B)$. The final step is to identify the orders $p$ and $q$ and estimate the parameters of the stationary model for $Y_t$, using techniques discussed in Section 8.2. To summarize, we outline the steps in the following:

- a. Identify the nonstationary component(s) of the model.
- b. Transform the data using the nonstationary factor(s) identified in (a).
- c. Estimate the parameters of the "stationaryized" data."

It is important to realize that the decision concerning whether to include a factor $1 - B$ in a model cannot be answered simply by examining the parameters, since a factor $1 - B$ will generally not appear exactly in the factored form of the estimated model. That is, if there is a $1 - B$ in the true model, one would expect the model to contain a factor that approximates $1 - B$, but not exactly equal to it. As mentioned in General Comment 4, the YW and Burg will always produce a stationary AR model. Studies have shown that ML estimates of $\phi_1$ in the AR(1) model $(1 - \phi_1)X_t = a_t$, where $\phi_1 = 1$ to have a negative bias (Kang, 1992). Thus, the fact that $|\phi_1| < 1$ is not sufficient evidence of a stationary AR(1) model, nor does the fact that all roots of $1 - \phi_1z - \dots - \phi_pz^p = 0$ fall outside the unit circle implying that the model is stationary.

### 8.3.2 Identifying Nonstationary Component(s) in a Model

General comment 5 has been demonstrated in Examples 3.14, 5.5, and 5.6 we noticed that time series models with unit roots reveal realizations whose characteristics are dominated by this nonstationary (or nearnonstationary) behavior. For example, in Example 5.5, we considered the model $(1 - \alpha_1)(1 - 1.2B + 0.8B^2)X_t = \alpha_t$, where $\alpha_1 = 0.8, 0.95$, and $0.995$. We noticed as $\alpha_1 \to 1$ (the associated root of the characteristic equation approached +), the realizations were characterized by wandering behavior with no attraction to a mean level and theoretical autocorrelations that tend to damp slowly. We note that sample autocorrelations will tend to have a similar behavior but often damp more quickly than theoretical autocorrelations. Similarly, in Example 5.6, we examined an AR(3) model (1 – 0.8B) (1 – 1.34B + 0.995B^2)X_t = a, with a pair of complex roots close to the unit circle and noted that the realization and autocorrelations damped slowly. The behavior of the autocorrelations is consistent with the results of the Finley-Quinn theorem (Theorem 5.1).

It is common to make a decision concerning including a factor of 1 – B based on realizations and sample autocorrelations. Realizations with wandering or precipiece trending behavior along with slowly damping sample autocorrelations suggest a unit root of +1, that is, a nonstationary factor of 1 – B, should be included in the model (e.g., see Box et al., 2008, p. 197). The classic Box-Jenkins procedure is then to standardize it by differencing. If the differed data indicate evidence of a unit root of +1, then the Box-Jenkins procedure is to difference again, etc., stationarity is achieved. We present an easy method for inspecting a model for unit roots or near roots anywhere on the unit circle. We refer to this as *overfitting*, and it is based on the following result given by Tiao and Tsay (1983).

### Theorem 8.1 (Tiao–Tsay)

If X_t is ARUMA(p,d,q), that is, φ(B)λ(B)(X_t – μ) = θ(B)a_t, then the model obtained using ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates of an AR(d + j), fit the data to be filtered as ϕ_{d+j}(B) = ϕ(B)ϕ(B), where ϕ(B) is the estimator of λ(B).

The implication of the Tiao–Tsay (Theorem 8.1) is that if a “high-order” AR model is fit to data an ARUMA(p,d,q) model, the features of the model associated with the roots on the unit circle will be well estimated in the “overfit” model. Gray and Woodward (1986, 1987) demonstrated the application of Theorem 8.1 for identifying nonstationarity using Burg instead of OLS estimates. The procedure recommended here is to successively overfit data with AR(k) models. The factored forms of the resulting models are explained for non-stationary factors that show in each of the overfit AR models. This procedure is illustrated in Example 8.4.
