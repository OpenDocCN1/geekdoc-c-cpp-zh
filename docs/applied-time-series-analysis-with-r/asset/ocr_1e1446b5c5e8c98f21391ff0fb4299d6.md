As in the case where $s_i$ is a line, long-term forecasts from Equation 6.60 and 6.61 should be interpreted with caution. Forecasts from each model have a fixed phase shift and amplitude even though these values are determined very differently as illustrated in the preceding discussion. For, example, the sunspot data shown in Figure 1.23a. Using either of these models to produce long-term forecasts of sunspot activity is problematic for at least two reasons. First, the sunspot oscillations are asymmetric and fail to account for this behavior and may, for example, (1990) will not be able to account for this behavior and makes no sense. Sinusoidal forecasts use a nonlinear time series model to the asymmetry. However, a more subtle feature of the data is that there are small changes in the period length through time. As a consequence, long-term forecasts based on a fixed phase, no matter how, eventually begin to predict a trough where a peak occurs, etc. For this reason, forecasts that eventually damp the mean (based on a stationary model fit) may be more reflective of the information available concerning sunspot values in the more distant future.
