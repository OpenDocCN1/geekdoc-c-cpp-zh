The log-likelihood function is given by

$$l(\phi_1,\sigma_0^2)=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma_0^2)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-\phi_1^2)-\frac{S_u}{2\sigma_0^2}, (7.36)$

where

$$S_u=(1-\phi_1^2)x_1^2+\sum_{t=2}^n(x_t-\phi_1_{t-1})^2.$$

The exact ML estimates do not exist in closed form for the AR(1) case of Example 7.3, and, in general, the derivatives of In $|\mathbf{M}_n|$ are complicated. Box et. al. (2008) suggest two simplifications:

+   1. For large $n$, they claim that In $|\mathbf{M}_n|$ is negligible compared to $S_u$. This suggests the simplified log-likelihood function

$$l(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_n,\theta_1,\dots,\theta_n,\sigma_0^2)=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma_0^2)-\frac{S_u}{2\sigma_0^2}. (7.37)$

Approximations to the unconditional ML estimates can be obtained by maximizing $|\phi_1,\dots,\phi_n,\theta_1,\dots,\theta_n,\sigma_0^2|$ in Equation 7.37. This maximization can be accomplished by minimizing the unconditional sum of squares $S_u$ for finite, nonzero $\sigma_0^2$, and consequently, these estimates are sometimes referred to as unconditional least squares estimates.

Box et al. (2008) suggest a practical method for approximating the unconditional sum of squares, $S_u$ based on a back-forecast (or backtracking) technique. They express the unconditional sum of squares as

$$S_u=\sum_{t=1}^nE[a_t|X]^2. (7.38)$

$E[a_t|X]$ denotes the conditional expectation of $a_t$ given $X_1,\dots,X_n$. The unconditional sum of squares in Equation 3.38 can be approximated by the sum

$$\hat{S}_u=\sum_{t=1}^n\hat{a}_t^2. (7.39)$
