Step 2—Analyze the Transformed (Dual) Realization Using Methods for Stationary Time Series: In the case of fitting a G(p; $\lambda$) model to a set of TVF data, the dual model obtained in Step 1 is then modeled as an AR(p). +   Forecasting: If forecasts are desired for the original TVF data, the next step is to obtain the corresponding forecasts using the dual model. That is, techniques such as those given in Chapter 6 for obtaining forecasts for a stationary AR(p) model can be employed. The forecasts are then to be mapped back to the original timescale as discussed in Step 3.

+   Spectral analysis: In Figure 13a, we showed a windowed spectral estimator for a dual realization and noted the interesting and potentially useful fact that the time transformation changed the wide range of TVF behavior (i.e., the spread spectrum) in the original series to a single dominant frequency in the transformed time. Characterization of the dual space may provide a useful way of summarizing information in the original series. See Example 13.11 for an example involving seismic data.

**Using R:** The dual spectral density estimate discussed in (2) above can be obtained by simply estimating the spectral density of the dual data. This can be accomplished by using function parzen.wge. Alternatively, an AR spectral density estimate can be obtained by fitting an AR model to the dual data and finding the associated AR spectral estimate using true.arma.

# 13.3.3.1 G(p; $\lambda$) Spectral Density

In general, we will refer to the AR spectral density of the dual data as the G(p; $\lambda$) spectral density. Spectral density in Figure 13a is an example of an AR spectral estimate based on the dual, that is, a G(p; $\lambda$) spectral density. This spectral estimate can be obtained using a variety of methods, but we recommend using the G(p; $\lambda$) spectral density, which is the AR(p) spectral density associated with the AR(p) model fit to the dual. The special case of a G(p; $\lambda$) spectral density in the case of $\lambda = 0$ (i.e., the M-stationary case) is sometimes called an M-spectral density estimator. See Gray et al. (2005).
