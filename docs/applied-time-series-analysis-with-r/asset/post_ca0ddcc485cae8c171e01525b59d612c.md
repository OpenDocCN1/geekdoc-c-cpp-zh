
## 7.3 ML Estimation of ARMA(p,q) Parameters

The use of ML estimation necessarily involves making distributional assumptions. For purposes of calculating the ML estimates of the parameters, we assume that the $a_i$ are normally distributed and hence that $X=(X_1, ..., X_n)'$ has a multivariate normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma_n$ where

$$\mu = (\mu, \mu, \dots \mu)'$$

and

$$\Sigma_n = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_0 & \gamma_1 & \dots & \gamma_{n-1} \\ \gamma_1 & \gamma_0 & \dots & \gamma_{n-2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \dots & \vdots \\ \gamma_{n-1} & \gamma_{n-2} & \dots & \gamma_0 \end{pmatrix}$$

As with previous estimation procedures, we analyze $X_t - X$ and assume $\mu = 0$.

### 7.3.1 Conditional and Unconditional ML Estimation

Let $\sigma_a^2 M_n^1 = \Sigma_n$, the joint probability density function of $X=(X_1, ..., X_n)'$ is given by

$$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_a^2)^{n/2}} |M_n|^{1/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{x^T M_n x}{2\sigma_a^2}\right\}$$

The joint density function in Equation 7.29 can be written

$$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = f(x_{p+1}, \dots, x_n | x_1, \dots, x_p)f(x_1, \dots, x_p),$$

where $f(\cdot)$ is the conditional probability density function. Since the $a_i$ are uncorrelated $N(0, \sigma_a^2)$ random variables, it follows that

$$f(a_{p+1}, \dots, a_n) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_a^2)^{(n-p)/2}} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_a^2} \sum_{t=p+1}^n a_t^2}\right\}$$

Conditional on $X_1 = x_{t_1}, \dots, X_p = x_{t_p}$ and starting values $a_1 = a_1^*, \dots, a_p = a_p^*$, it follows from Equation 7.31 (assuming for convenience of notation that $q \leq p$) for a choice of the $\phi_s$ and $\theta_s$, the random variables $a_{p+1}, \dots, a_n$ and $X_{p+1}, \dots, X_n$ are related by the system of equations

$a_{p+1} = X_{p+1} - \phi_1 X_p - \dots - \phi_p X_1 + \theta_1 a_p + \dots + \theta_q a_{p-1}$  
$a_{p+2} = X_{p+2} - \phi_1 X_{p+1} - \dots - \phi_p X_2 + \theta_1 a_{p+1} + \dots + \theta_q a_{p-2}$

:  
$a_n = X_n - \phi_1 X_{n-1} - \dots - \phi_p X_{n-p} + \theta_1 a_{n-1} + \dots + \theta_q a_{n-q}.$

The values for $a_{t_1}, \dots, a_{t_p}$ are earlier taken to be zero, their unconditional mean. Since the absolute value of the Jacobian of this transformation is one, it follows that

$f(x_{p+1}, \dots, x_n | x_{t_1}, \dots, x_{t_p}) =$  
$= \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_a^2)^{(n-p)/2}} \exp \left\{ -\frac{1}{2\sigma_a^2} \sum_{t=p+1}^n (x_t - \phi_1 x_{t-1} - \dots - \phi_p x_{t-p} + \theta_1 a_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q a_{t-q})^2 \right\} \quad (7.32)$

where $S_c$, is given by

$S_c = \sum_{t=p+1}^n (x_t - \phi_1 x_{t-1} - \dots - \phi_p x_{t-p} + \theta_1 a_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q a_{t-q})^2.$

Calculating $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ using Equation 7.30 involves $f(x_1, \dots, x_p)$, which is given by

$f(x_1, \dots, x_p) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_a^2)^{p/2}} |M_p|^{1/2} \exp \left\{ -\frac{x_p' M_p x_p}{2\sigma_a^2} \right\}. \quad (7.33)$

where $x_p = (x_1, \dots, x_p)'$. Multiplying $f(x_{p+1}, \dots, x_n | x_{t_1}, \dots, x_{t_p})$ in Equation 7.32 by $f(x_1, \dots, x_p)$ in Equation 7.33 yields

$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = f(x_{p+1}, \dots, x_n | x_1, \dots, x_p)f(x_1, \dots, x_p)$

$= \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_a^2)^{(n-p)/2}} \exp\left\{-\frac{S_c}{2\sigma_a^2}\right\} \times \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_a^2)^{p/2}} |\mathbf{M}_p|^{1/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{\mathbf{x}_p^T\mathbf{M}_p\mathbf{x}_p}{2\sigma_a^2}\right\}$

$= \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_a^2)^{n/2}} |\mathbf{M}_p|^{1/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{S_u}{2\sigma_a^2}\right\}$

where

$S_u = S_c + \mathbf{x}_p^T\mathbf{M}_p\mathbf{x}_p. \quad (7.34)$

It is common terminology to refer to $S_u$ as the unconditional sum of squares and to $S_c$ as the conditional sum of squares. The log-likelihood function is given by

$l(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p, \theta_1, \dots, \theta_q, \sigma_a^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma_a^2) + \frac{1}{2}\ln|\mathbf{M}_p| - \frac{S_u}{2\sigma_a^2}. \quad (7.35)$

The ML estimators are found by maximizing Equation 7.35 with respect to the parameters $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p, \theta_1, \dots, \theta_q$ and $\sigma_a^2$. The estimators obtained are the unconditional or exact ML estimates.

### EXAMPLE 7.3: ML ESTIMATION IN THE AR(1) CASE

Consider an AR(1) model in which case

$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = f(x_2, \dots, x_n | x_1)f(x_1).$

In this case, $f(x_1)$ is given by

$f(x_1) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_a^2)^{1/2}} \exp\left\{-\frac{x_1^2}{2\sigma_a^2}\right\}$

$= \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_a^2)^{1/2}} (1-\phi_1^2)^{1/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{x_1^2(1-\phi_1^2)}{2\sigma_a^2}\right},$

since $\sigma_a^2 = \sigma_x^2/(1-\phi_1^2)$. Thus, in the notation of Equation 7.33, $|\mathbf{M}_p| = 1-\phi_1^2$ and

$$f(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \left(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_0^2}\right)^{n/2}(1-\phi_1^2)^{1/2}$$
$\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_0^2}\left((1-\phi_1^2)x_1^2+\sum_{t=2}^n(x_t-\phi_1 x_{t-1})^2\right)\right).$

The log-likelihood function is given by

$$l(\phi_1,\sigma_0^2)=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma_0^2)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-\phi_1^2)-\frac{S_u}{2\sigma_0^2}, \quad (7.36)$$

where

$$S_u=(1-\phi_1^2)x_1^2+\sum_{t=2}^n(x_t-\phi_1 x_{t-1})^2.$$

The exact ML estimates do not exist in closed form for the AR(1) case of Example 7.3, and, in general, the derivatives of $\ln |\mathbf{M}_n|$ are complicated. Box et. al. (2008) suggest two simplifications:

- 1. For large $n$, they claim that $\ln |\mathbf{M}_n|$ is negligible compared to $S_u$. This suggests the simplified log-likelihood function

$$l(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_n,\theta_1,\dots,\theta_n,\sigma_0^2)=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma_0^2)-\frac{S_u}{2\sigma_0^2}. \quad (7.37)$$

Approximations to the unconditional ML estimates can be obtained by maximizing $l(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_n,\theta_1,\dots,\theta_n,\sigma_0^2)$ in Equation 7.37. This maximization can be accomplished by minimizing the unconditional sum of squares $S_u$ for finite, nonzero $\sigma_0^2$, and consequently, these estimates are sometimes referred to as unconditional least squares estimates.

Box et al. (2008) suggest a practical method for approximating the unconditional sum of squares, $S_u$ based on a back-forecast (or backtracking) technique. They express the unconditional sum of squares as

$$S_u=\sum_{t=1}^nE[a_t|X]^2. \quad (7.38)$$

$E[a_t|X]$ denotes the conditional expectation of $a_t$ given $X_1,\dots,X_n$. The unconditional sum of squares in Equation 7.38 can be approximated by the sum

$$\hat{S}_u=\sum_{t=1}^n\hat{a}_t^2. \quad (7.39)$$

Estimation of $\sigma_a^2$: The ML estimate of $\sigma_a^2$ (based on maximizing either Equation 7.35 or Equation 7.37 can be obtained by differentiating $l(\phi, \theta, \sigma_a^2)$) with respect to $\sigma_a^2$, which yields (in case)

$\frac{\partial l}{\partial \sigma_a^2} = -\frac{n}{2\sigma_a^2} + \frac{S}{2\sigma_a^4} = 0$

where $S = S_u$ or $S_c$. Setting this partial derivative to zero and solving for $\sigma_a^2$ gives

$$\hat{\sigma}_a^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^n \hat{a}_t^2 \quad (7.41)$$

the $\hat{a}_t$'s in Equation 7.41 denote the estimates obtained via backcasting. $$\hat{\sigma}_a^2 = \frac{\hat{S}_c}{(n-p)} \quad (7.42)$$
