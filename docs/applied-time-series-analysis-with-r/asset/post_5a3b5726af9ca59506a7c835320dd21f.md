### 1.6 Estimating the Power Spectrum and Spectral Density for Discrete Time Series

Estimation of the power spectrum and spectral density from data can be a challenging problem, and there are a variety of estimation techniques in the literature. We will briefly introduce the topic here but come back to it in later chapters. Given the spectrum in Equation 1.29 for a discrete parameter time series, it seems that a natural estimator for the spectrum could be obtained by simply substituting the sample autocovances discussed in Section 1.4 into Equation 1.29. The resulting estimator is called the sample spectrum and is defined in Definition 1.14.

### Definition 1.14

$X$, be a discrete stationary time series defined on the integers. The sample spectrum based on a realization of length $n$ from this time series is given by

```
$$\hat{S}_X(f)=\hat{\sigma}_X^2+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\hat{\rho}_k\cos(2\pi fk),$$
```

for $|f|\leq 0.5$. The sample spectral density is given by

```
$$\hat{S}_X(f)=1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\hat{\rho}_k\cos(2\pi fk).$$
```

The limit, $n-1$, on the sum is reasonable, since sample autocovariances and autocorrelations for $k \geq n$ are defined to be zero.
A related estimator is the periodogram that is simply the sample spectrum evaluated at the harmonics of the fundamental frequency $f_1 = 1/n$. Specifically, the harmonics, $f_j$, are given by

```
$f_j = \frac{j}{n}, j=1,...$
$f_j = \frac{n}{2} (if \ n\ is even)$
$f_j = \frac{j}{n}, j=1,...,m (if \ n\ is odd and $n=2m+1).$
```

### Definition 1.5

Let $X_i$, a discrete time series defined on the integers. The periodogram based on a realization of length $n$ from this time series is given by

```
$I(f_j) = \hat{\sigma}_X^2 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \hat{\gamma}_k \cos(2\pi f_jk),$ (1.38)
```

where the $f_j$ are the harmonics defined earlier.

The periodogram can alternatively be defined in terms of the finite Fourier representation of the signal and as the modulus squared of the finite Fourier transform of the data. See Appendix 1A. When the sample spectrum and periodogram are obvious estimators of the spectrum, they are not well-behaved as estimators. Theorem 1.4 that follows outlines the properties of the sample spectrum.

The basic problem with the sample spectrum and the periodogram is that the estimates for $\gamma$ when $j$ is close to $n$ are extremely variable regardless of the magnitude of $n$. Much research has gone into the smoothing of the sample spectrum in order to obtain better spectral estimators. A common type of smoothed spectral estimator takes the general form

```
$$\hat{P}_S(f) = \lambda_0\hat{\gamma}_0 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{M} \lambda_k\hat{\gamma}_k\cos(2\pi f/k), \quad (1.39)$$
```

where the truncation point $M$ is chosen to be substantially smaller than $n$, and $\hat{\lambda}_k, k=0, 1, \dots, M$ is a window function that damps as $k$ increases. The weights for three classic window functions are:

- 1. Bartlett (triangular) window
$$\$\lambda_k = 1-\left(\frac{k}{M}\right), \quad k=0,1,\dots,M$$
$n=0, \quad k>M$

- 2. Tukey window
$$\$\lambda_k = \frac{1}{2}\left[1+\cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{M}\right)^2\right], \quad k=0,1,\dots,M$$
$n=0, \quad k>M$

- 3. Parzen window
$$\$\lambda_k = 1-6\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)^2+6\left(\frac{|k|}{M}\right)^3, \quad 0<k\le M/2$$
$n=2\left[1-\left(\frac{|k|}{M}\right)^3\right], \quad M/2<k\le M$$
$n=0, \quad k>M.$$
