One technique for calculating the autocovariances is to truncate the series in Equation 11.24 after a sufficient number of terms. Table 11.1 (given by Ford, 1991) illustrates that, depending on the parameters, the terms in this series may not damp quickly enough to make truncation feasible.

The table considers the problem of using Equation 11.24 to calculate $\gamma_u$ for the case $u=1$. In this case Theorem 11.1(4) and the fact that $d=2\lambda$, the infinite series in Equation 11.24 is known in closed form to be $\gamma_0^*=\Gamma(-4\lambda+1)/\Gamma(-2\lambda+1)^2$. Table 11.1 compares values of the infinite sum in Equation 11.24 to the results of truncating the sum after 200 million terms and 10 million terms. In the table it sees that for $\lambda=0.05$ and $\lambda=0.125$ the two truncated approximate the infinite sum quite well, in the near-stationary case $\lambda=0.24$, the convergence is extremely slow and a sum to 10 million is a very poor approximation! Chung (1996a) expressed $\gamma_u$ for a (1-factor) Gegenbauer process as a recursion using Legendre functions. Using Equations 11.28 and 11.19, an expression for the autocovariance of a Gegenbauer process is given by the inverse Fourier transform of the spectrum,

$$\gamma_k = \int_{-0.5}^{0.5} e^{2\pi ikf} P(f)df. \quad (11.25)$$

In the Gegenbauer setting, Ford (1991) examined the use of Levin and Sidi’s (1981) $d$-transform for increasing the rate of convergence of the sum in Equation 11.24 and compared the resulting approximations with those obtained by numerically integrating Equation 11.25. As noted, $P_x(f)$ is unbounded for $f_c = (\cos^{-1}(u))/2\lambda$. Ford (1991) concluded that careful numerical integration using Equation 11.25 is the preferred method. Based on these findings, we approximate the autocovariances of $k$-factor Gegenbauer and $k$-factor GARMA models by numerically integrating the integral in Equation 11.25. It should be noted that both methods produce approximate autocorrelations, and for some parameter configurations these can be relatively poor.

## 11.4.2 Generating Realizations

Gray et al. (1989, 1994) generate realizations of Gegenbauer and GARMA models using the strategy of truncating the general linear process given in Equation 11.1 to obtain

```
$X_t = \mu + \sum_{h=0}^{M} C_h^{(k)}(u)a_{t-h} \quad (11.26)$
```

with $M=290,000$. Woodward et al. (1998) suggest a more efficient algorithm due to Ramsey (1974) based on conditional expectations and variances of $X$, given $X_0, X_t, X_{t-j}$. However, the more efficient method involves the true autocavariances, and our experience is due to the computational difficulties described earlier, of the conditional quantities became corrupted. Consequently, the realizations shown here will be based on the truncated linear process.

In order to generate realizations from a $k$-factor GARMA, we let $Z_t$ be the corresponding $k$-factor Gegenbauer process, which we express as

```
$Z_t = \prod_{j=1}^{k} (1-2u_jB+B^2)^{-\lambda_j}a_t,$
```

and we write Equation 11.20 as

```
$\phi(B)X_t = \mu(1-\phi_1-\cdots-\phi_p)+\theta(B)Z_t.$
```

A realization from the $k$-factor Gegenbauer process, $Z_t$, is generated using the truncation method described earlier, and the $k$-factor GARMA process is then simulated using the recursion

```
$X_t = \mu(1-\phi_1-\cdots-\phi_p)+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j X_{t-j}+Z_t-\sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j Z_{t-j}.$
```

See Haskell (1984), Gray et al. (1989, 1994), and Woodward et al. (1998).

**Using R:** The tswge root gen.garma.wge generates realizations from a given 1-factor GARMA model. We recommend the function fracdiffsim in CRAN package for simulating realizations from fractional or FARMA models. For more information see Appendix 11A.

## 11.5 Parameter Estimation and Model Identification

We consider the problem of estimating the parameters of fractional and FARMA models. Geweke and Porter-Hudak (1993) used a semi-parametric periodogram-based regression method to estimate $d$, Hosking (1984) used a likelihood grid-search approach, and Whittle's (1951) frequency-domain approach to ML estimation was implemented by Fox and Taqqu (1986) to the problem of estimating the parameters of a FARMA model. In this section we adapt the grid-search approach by Hosking (1984) to the problem of estimating the parameters of a GARMA model which, as we have seen, includes the fractional and FARMA models as special cases. Other techniques for GARMA models will be briefly referenced. As in Chapter 7, we estimate $\mu$ by $\bar{X}$ and the mean corrected data (denoted by $X$) as the realization by. It should be noted that the properties of $X$ as an estimator of $\mu_k$ depend on $|u|$. For example, in the case of a (1-factor) GARMA model, the sample converges at the $(1/\sqrt{n})$ rate whenever $|u| < 1$, while it has a slower convergence rate. See Chung (1996b).

If $X_t$ satisfies the model in Equation 11.20 with $\mu=0$, then $Y_t = \sum_{j=1}^t(1-2u_B+B^2)^k X_j$ satisfies an $ARMA(p,q)$ $\phi(B)Y=\Theta(B)X$. Woodward et al. (1998) recommend the use of a grid-search for the parameters of a $k$ model. In practice, $k$ is estimated by identifying the peaks in the periodogram. Based on these findings, we approximate the autocovariances of $k$-factor Gegenbauer and $k$-factor GARMA models by numerically integrating the integral in Equation 11.25. It should be noted that both methods produce approximate autocorrelations, and for some parameter configurations these can be relatively poor.
