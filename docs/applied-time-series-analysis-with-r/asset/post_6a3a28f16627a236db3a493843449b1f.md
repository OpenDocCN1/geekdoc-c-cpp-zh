## 13.3.3 Analyzing TVF Data Using the G($p$; $\lambda$) Model

At the end of Section 13.1, we outlined the techniques for analyzing TVF data using G-stationary methods. In this section, we briefly discuss the items in the outline and provide a few examples with a focus on the G($p$; $\lambda$) model. These are discussed in much greater detail in Gray et al. (2005), Jiang et al. (2006), and Choi et al. (2006). The tswge R package contains functions that perform the calculations in the analysis steps described below. See Appendix 13B for more details. M-stationary processes are analyzed using the G($p$; $\lambda$)-based functions by specifying $\lambda=0$.

*Step 1—Transform the Time Axis to Obtain a Stationary Dual Realization*
A search routine is used to search for the values of $\lambda$ and offset to produce the "most stationary" dual. For each set of $\lambda$, $h$, and offset values in the search, the $X(t_k)$, $k=1, 2, \dots$, are approximated at $t_k$ in Equation 13.18, with interpolation. By indexing on $k$, the dual realization associated with the given $\lambda$, $h$, and offset is obtained. For each of these dual realizations, tswge function est.glamba.wge employs techniques for measuring the degree to which the correlation behavior seems to remain constant

across the realization. This measure is based on the fact that the correlation structure under stationary stays constant across the realization for TVF data, such as in Figure 13.6a, the correlation behavior is very different at the beginning of the realization than it is to the end. Based on such a measure of correlation change, the final values of $\lambda, h_i$ and offset are taken to be those that produced the "most stationary behaving" realization. To illustrate these remarks, Figure 13.7 shows the sample autocorrelations of the first third of the data in Figure 13.4a with a solid line, the sample autocorrelations for the middle third of the data with a dotted line, and the sample autocorrelations for the last third of the data with a dashed line. It can be seen that there is dramatic difference in autocorrelation patterns among the three. For the initial third of the data, there is higher frequency behavior that is changing so rapidly that the sample autocorrelations for the first eight lags or are very small. The sample autocorrelations for the other two-thirds of the data show a cyclic behavior but with a higher frequency behavior for the middle third (dotted line) than the final third (dashed line). The point is that if the data set were to be expected to each other. This is illustrated in Figure 13.7b, where we show the sample autocorrelations for the first, second, and last of the stationary data in Figure 13.4b. See Jiang et al. (2006). See Bao (2014) for an extension of the method of Jiang et al. (2006) that allows the comparison of sample autocorrelations based on division of the time interval into more than three segments.

Step 2—Analyze the Transformed (Dual) Realization Using Methods for Stationary Time Series: In the case of fitting a G(p; $\lambda$) model to a set of TVF data, the dual model obtained in Step 1 is then modeled as an AR(p). ### 13.3.3.1 G(p; $\lambda$) Spectral Density

In general, we will refer to the AR spectral density of the dual data as the G(p; $\lambda$) spectral density. Spectral density in Figure 13a is an example of an AR spectral estimate based on the dual, that is, a G(p; $\lambda$) spectral density. This spectral estimate can be obtained using a variety of methods, but we recommend using the G(p; $\lambda$) spectral density, which is the AR(p) spectral density associated with the AR(p) model fit to the dual. The special case of a G(p; $\lambda$) spectral density in the case of $\lambda = 0$ (i.e., the M-stationary case) is sometimes called an M-spectral density estimator. See Gray et al. (2005).

Step 3—Transform Back to the Original Timescale: It will be the case that the forecasts or spectral estimators obtained on the dual space are not the final results that are desired. We consider forecasting and spectral estimation separately.

- 1. Forecasting: Assume that $X(t)$ is observed at equally spaced points and that forecasts are for $X(k+1), X(k+2), \dots, X(k+h)$ for some origin $k$. The forecasts for $Y_{kv}, Y_{kv2}, \dots,$ which are obtained in Step 2, are converted into forecasts for the desired time points using interpolation.

- 2. Spectral analysis: It has been mentioned that the $G(p; \lambda)$ spectral density can be a very useful tool for summarizing frequency information based on a stationary transformation. However, the frequency scale in the dual space is difficult to relate back to frequencies in the original timescale. For this reason, it is often desirable to transform the frequency information in the $G(p; \lambda)$ spectral density back into the original time and frequency domains using Appendix 13A provides details concerning the definition of IF and the instantaneous spectrum. By referring back to Figure 13.3, it is seen that the peak in the dual spectrum in Figure 13.4d is associated with an IF of about

$f=0.2$ at $t=19$, of about $0.09$ at $t=80$, and about $t=152$. The instantaneous spectrum is a time-frequency plot (similar to the Wigner-Ville plot, Gabor transform, and wavelet spectrogram) that transforms the information in the $G(p; \lambda)$ spectral density back into the original dimensions of time and frequency to display the manner in frequency changes with time.

**Using R:** (i) Forecasts using a $G(p; \lambda)$ model using the outline discussed above can be obtained using the tswge fore.glambda.wge. (ii) The Parzen or AR spectral density estimates can be transformed into instantaneous spectrum using the tswge functions is.parzen.wge and is.ar.wge. See Appendix 13B for more details.
