# Long-Memory Processes

In the previous chapters, we have considered ARMA, ARIMA, seasonal, signal-plus-noise, and ARCH/GARCH processes. For the current discussion, we restrict ourselves to nonseasonal models. Stationary ARMA(p,q) processes have autocorrelations that decay (go to zero) exponentially as k large. That is, $ho_k \simeq \alpha^{|k|}$ as $k 	o \infty$ where $|\alpha| < 1$ and $k$ is the lag. We use notation $\hat{f}_t \simeq g_t$ as $t 	o \infty$ to indicate that $\lim_{t\to\infty}(f_t/g_t) = c$, where $c$ is a finite nonzero constant. For example, for an AR(1) model with $\phi_1 = 0.9$, $ho_k = 0.9^{|k|}$. In Example 3.10, it was shown that the autocorrelation function for the AR(3) model $(1 - 0.95B)(1 - 1.6B + 0.9B^2)X_t = a_t$ is given

```
$ho_k = 0.66(0.95)^k + 0.3518(0.95)^k [2\pi(0.09)k+1.31]$
```

so that in both cases $ho_k$ decays. In Chapter 5 we viewed ARMA(p,d,q) processes as limits of ARMA processes as one or more roots of the characteristic equation approach the unit circle. Since the autocorrelation of an AR process decays exponentially, such processes are referred to as “short-memory”. It was noted in Section 5.2 that the function decays much more slowly as one or more roots of the characteristic equation approach the unit circle, and in fact the extended autocorrelations of the limiting ARMA(p,d,q) process with integer $d > 0$ do not decay as k goes to infinity. Signal-plus-noise models also have autocorrelations that do not decay, but these models involve functional restrictions that often prove to be too limiting in practice. In Chapter 6, we showed that the correlation-driven (ARMA and ARIMA) models are more adaptive than the signal-plus-noise models in the ARMA/ARIMA models “react” strongly to the most current data and little to the distance of the distant point.

In many physical processes, in such areas as hydrology, seismic monitoring, atmospheric analysis, brain imaging, and others, the correlations for data that otherwise appear to be stationary in nature are seen to decay much more slowly than those of short-memory (e.g., ARMA) models. See, for example, Beran (1994), Mandelbrot (1983), Kessler (1992), Taqqqu (1986), and van der Ziel (1983). Hurst (1951, 1955) observed long-memory behavior (which became known as the “Hurst effect”) in hydrology data. This discovery prompted researchers to develop models that would adequately account for this long-memory behavior.
