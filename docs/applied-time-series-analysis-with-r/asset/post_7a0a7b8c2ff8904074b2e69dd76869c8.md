
## 7 Parameter Estimation

### 7.1 Introduction

Suppose a realization $x_1, x_2, ..., x_n$ is observed. The problem of fitting an ARMA$(p,q)$ model to the data is the problem of estimating

$p,q$ 
$\phi_1, \phi_2, ..., \phi_p$ 
$\theta_1, \theta_2, ..., \theta_q$ 
$\mu, \sigma_a^2. (7.1)$

The estimation of $p$ and $q$ is called order identification, and this topic will be covered in detail in Chapter 8. In the current chapter, we assume that $p$ and $q$ are known, and techniques will be discussed for estimating the additional parameters in (7.1). An analysis of a time series using an ARMA model typically first order identification followed by the estimation procedures to be discussed in this chapter. However, the dichotomy between the order identification and parameter estimation steps is not clear-cut since the means of selecting the appropriate model orders. Now, we discuss parameter estimation assuming known $p$ and $q$.

Much work has been done recently by statisticians on the problem of obtaining maximum likelihood (ML) estimates of the parameters due to their well-known optimality properties. The estimation of ARIMA parameters involves iterative procedures that require starting values. In this chapter, we first deal with the issue of finding computationally preliminary estimates of the parameters that can be used as starting values for the ML routines. It should be noted that these computationally efficient estimates have received considerable attention in the engineering and geophysical literature partially due to their need to estimate parameters in real time, to obtain estimates for a large collection of time series, or for time series of several thousand points. In Section 7.2, we discuss techniques for obtaining preliminary estimates of the model parameters, and then, in Section 7.3, we discuss ML estimation. Section 7.4 discusses the topic of estimating $\sigma_a^2$ and describes backcasting, Section 7.5 gives asymptotic properties, Section 7.6 provides some example applications and comparisons of estimation methods, and Section 7.7 discusses ARMA spectral estimation.

### 7.2 Preliminary Estimates

In this section, we discuss techniques for obtaining preliminary estimates of the parameters of $AR(p), MA(q)$, and $ARMA(p,q)$ models.

### 7.2.1 Preliminary Estimates for AR(p) Models

We consider the problem of estimating the parameters of the $AR(p)$ models

$X_t - \mu - \phi_1(X_{t-1} - \mu) - \dots - \phi_p(X_{t-p} - \mu) = a_t,$ (7.2)

when $p$ is assumed to be known. We focus here on Yule-Walker (YW), least squares, and Burg estimates.

#### 7.2.1.1 Yule–Walker Estimates

As the name suggests, the YW estimates are based on the YW equations in Equation 3.41. In particular, recall that the autocorrelation, $\rho_u$, of stationary $AR(p)$ process satisfies the difference equation

$\rho_k = \phi_1\rho_{k-1} + \dots + \phi_p\rho_{k-p},$ (7.3)

for $k > 0$. Letting $k=1, 2, \dots, p$ in Equation 7.3 yields the YW estimates

$\rho_1 = \phi_1 + \phi_2\rho_1 + \dots + \phi_p\rho_{p-1}$
$\rho_2 = \phi_1\rho_1 + \phi_2 + \dots + \phi_p\rho_{p-2}$
$\dots$
$\rho_p = \phi_1\rho_{p-1} + \phi_2\rho_{p-2} + \dots + \phi_p.$ (7.4)

In Chapter 3, these equations were seen to be useful for finding the $\rho_k$ when $\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots, \phi_p$ were known. If the $\rho_k$ are known, then the YW equations are $p$ equations in the unknowns $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p$, that can be solved for the $\phi_u$. YW estimates are found from a realization by replacing $\rho_k$, for $k=1, \dots, p$ in Equation 7.4 with the sample autocorrelations, $\hat{\rho}_k$, discussed in Chapter 1. That is, $\hat{\phi}_k = \hat{\rho}_k/\hat{\rho}_0$ where $\hat{\rho}_k$ is the biased estimator of $\gamma_k$ in Equation 1.19. Specifically,

$$\hat{\gamma}_k = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n-k} (X_t - \bar{X})(X_{t+|k|} - \bar{X}), k=0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots, \pm(n-1)$$
$=0, k \geq n.$

The $p \times p$ system of equations

$$\hat{\rho}_1 = \hat{\phi}_1 + \hat{\phi}_2\hat{\rho}_1 + \dots + \hat{\phi}_p\hat{\rho}_{p-1}$$
$$\hat{\rho}_2 = \hat{\phi}_1\hat{\rho}_1 + \hat{\phi}_2 + \dots + \hat{\phi}_p\hat{\rho}_{p-2}$$
$:$ 
$$\hat{\rho}_p = \hat{\phi}_1\hat{\rho}_{p-1} + \hat{\phi}_2\hat{\rho}_{p-2} + \dots + \hat{\phi}_p.$$

is solved for $\hat{\phi}_1, \dots, \hat{\phi}_p$, the resulting estimates are referred to as the Yule-Walker estimates and will be denoted here as $\hat{\phi}_{YW} =(\hat{\phi}_1, \dots, \hat{\phi}_p)$.

It is convenient to rewrite Equation 7.6 as

$$\hat{\mathbf{P}} \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}.$$
(7.7)

where

$$\hat{\mathbf{P}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \hat{\rho}_1 & \dots & \hat{\rho}_{p-1} \\ \hat{\rho}_1 & 1 & \dots & \hat{\rho}_{p-2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \hat{\rho}_{p-1} & \hat{\rho}_{p-2} & \dots & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
(7.8)

$\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=(\hat{\phi}_1, \hat{\phi}_2, \dots, \hat{\phi}_p)$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=(\hat{\rho}_1, \hat{\rho}_2, \dots, \hat{\rho}_p)$ so that the YW estimates can be written as $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{YW}=\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}.$

YW Estimation Using the Durbin-Levinson Algorithm: The matrix $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ in Equation 7.8 is an example of a symmetric Toeplitz matrix (Graybill, 1983). Levinson (1947) and Durbin (1960) have shown that solutions of Equation 7.7 can be estimated recursively without performing any matrix inversions. The recursion is as follows where $\hat{\phi}_{kj}$ the estimate of the $j$th AR coefficient for an AR($p$) model:

$$\hat{\phi}_{11} = \hat{\rho}_1.$$

Then, for $k=2, \dots, p$,

$$\hat{\phi}_{kk} = \frac{\hat{\rho}_k - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \hat{\phi}_{k-i}\hat{\rho}_i}{1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \hat{\phi}_{k-i}\hat{\phi}_{i}}.$$
(7.9)

j = 1, 2, ..., k - 1. Of course, the analogous recursion for Equation 7.4 provides an efficient technique for calculating the true parameters $\phi_1, ..., \phi_p$ when the autocovariances are known.

An important property of the YW estimates in the AR(p) setting is that the resulting model is stationary, the roots of $1-\hat{\phi}_{1}z-\dots-\hat{\phi}_{p}z^{p}=0$ lie outside the unit circle (see Brockwell and Davis, 1991). It should be noted that unlike the case with $\hat{\gamma}_k$, if the estimates $\hat{\gamma}_l$ given in Equation 1.18 are used in the calculation of the autocovariances, the "YW" estimates do not necessarily produce a stationary model. This is primarily due to the fact discussed in Section 1.4 that $\hat{\gamma}_k$ is positive semidefinite while $\hat{\gamma}_l$ is not.

Before finishing this section, we note that the YW estimates are method-of-moment (MM) estimates of $\phi_1, ..., \phi_p$, since they are obtained by equating sample autocorrelations to theoretical autocovariances and solving the resulting equations. MM estimates of $\mu$ and $\sigma_a^2$ are given by $\hat{\mu}=\bar{X}$ and $\hat{\sigma}_X^2=\hat{\gamma}_0$. For AR(p) models, Equation 3.45 shows

$$\hat{\sigma}_X^2=\sigma_a^2(1-\phi_1\rho_1-\dots-\phi_p\rho_p)$$

from which it follows that $\hat{\sigma}_a^2$ is

$$\hat{\sigma}_a^2=\hat{\sigma}_X^2(1-\hat{\phi}_1\hat{\rho}_1-\dots-\hat{\phi}_p\hat{\rho}_p).$$

In Section 7.4, we further discuss the estimation of $\sigma_a^2$.

#### 7.2.1.2 Least Squares Estimation

Ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates are obtained by rewriting the AR(p) model of Equation 7.2

$X_t-\mu=\phi_1(X_{t-1}-\mu)+\dots+\phi_p(X_{t-p}-\mu)+a_t$,

and treating Equation 7.11 as a regression model to estimate $\phi_1, ..., \phi_p$ and $\mu$. Specifically, taking $t=p+1, p+2, ..., n$ in Equation 7.11 gives the equations

$X_{p+1}-\mu=\phi_1(X_p-\mu)+\dots+\phi_p(X_1-\mu)+a_{p+1}$
$X_{p+2}-\mu=\phi_1(X_{p+1}-\mu)+\dots+\phi_p(X_2-\mu)+a_{p+2}$
$\vdots$
$X_n-\mu=\phi_1(X_{n-1}-\mu)+\dots+\phi_p(X_{n-p}-\mu)+a_n$.

OLS estimates are the values of $\hat{\mu},\hat{\phi}_1,..., \hat{\phi}_p$, that minimize the conditional sum of squares
