
# 11 Long-Memory Processes

In the previous chapters, we have considered ARMA, ARIMA, seasonal, signal-plus-noise, and ARCH/GARCH processes. For the current discussion, we restrict ourselves to nonseasonal models. Stationary ARMA(p,q) processes have autocorrelations that decay (go to zero) exponentially as $k$ large. That is, $\rho_k \simeq \alpha^{|k|}$ as $k \to \infty$ where $|\alpha| < 1$ and $k$ is the lag. We use notation $\hat{f}_t \simeq g_t$ as $t \to \infty$ to indicate that $\lim_{t\to\infty}(f_t/g_t) = c$, where $c$ is a finite nonzero constant. For example, for an AR(1) model with $\phi_1 = 0.9$, $\rho_k = 0.9^{|k|}$. In Example 3.10, it was shown that the autocorrelation function for the AR(3) model $(1 - 0.95B)(1 - 1.6B + 0.9B^2)X_t = a_t$ is given

$$\rho_k = 0.66(0.95)^k + 0.3518(0.95)^k [2\pi(0.09)k+1.31]$$

so that in both cases $\rho_k$ decays. In Chapter 5 we viewed ARMA(p,d,q) processes as limits of ARMA processes as one or more roots of the characteristic equation approach the unit circle. Since the autocorrelation of an AR process decays exponentially, such processes are referred to as “short-memory”. It was noted in Section 5.2 that the function decays much more slowly as one or more roots of the characteristic equation approach the unit circle, and in fact the extended autocorrelations of the limiting ARMA(p,d,q) process with integer $d > 0$ do not decay as $k$ goes to infinity. Signal-plus-noise models also have autocorrelations that do not decay, but these models involve functional restrictions that often prove to be too limiting in practice. In Chapter 6, we showed that the correlation-driven (ARMA and ARIMA) models are more adaptive than the signal-plus-noise models in the ARMA/ARIMA models “react” strongly to the most current data and little to the distance of the distant point.

In many physical processes, in such areas as hydrology, seismic monitoring, atmospheric analysis, brain imaging, and others, the correlations for data that otherwise appear to be stationary in nature are seen to decay much more slowly than those of short-memory (e.g., ARMA) models. See, for example, Beran (1994), Mandelbrot (1983), Kessler (1992), Taqqqu (1986), and van der Ziel (1983). Hurst (1951, 1955) observed long-memory behavior (which became known as the “Hurst effect”) in hydrology data. This discovery prompted researchers to develop models that would adequately account for this long-memory behavior.

## 11.1 Long Memory

In this section, we give definitions and characterizations of long-memory models. We begin with the following definition.

## Definition 11.1

A stationary time series defined over $t = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots$ is said to be long memory if $\sum_{h} |\gamma_h| = \infty$, where $\gamma$ is the autocovariance of the process. Otherwise, the time series is said to be short memory.

For example, if the autocovariance decays hyperbolically, that is, $\gamma_k \simeq k^{-\alpha}$ as $k \to \infty$ for some $0 < \alpha \le 1$, then the process is long memory. Of course, other correlation structures are possible that would satisfy the conditions of long memory in Definition 11.1. A frequency-domain definition of long memory that is a slight extension of Definition 11.1.

## Definition 11.2

A stationary time series defined over $t = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots$ is said to be long memory for some frequency, $f \in [0, 0.5]$, the power spectrum, $P(f)$, becomes unbounded.

This definition characterizes memory in the frequency domain as the existence of a persistent cyclic component within the process. If the spectrum becomes unbounded, then the autocovariances are not absolutely summable since by Equation 1.29 it follows

$$|P(f)| < 2 \sum_{k} \gamma_k \cos(2\pi fk) \le 2 \sum_{k} |\gamma_k|$$

for all $f \in [0, 0.5]$. Miller (1994) gives a counter example to show that the converse is not true. From Definition 11.2 it follows that a short-memory process (e.g., stationary AR) has a bounded spectrum.

An example of long-memory processes (based on Definition 11.2) is the firstly of the so-called 1/f processes (1/f noise, flicker noise, pink noise) whose spectrum behave like $1/|f|^\alpha$ where the primary interest is $0 < \alpha < 1$. See Bullmore et al. (2001) for a discussion of 1/f noise in fMRI brain data, and Wordell (1993, 1996) for a discussion of the use of the wavelet transform (to be discussed in Chapter 12) for decorrelating data with 1/f noise. 1/f-type data have been modeled using fractional Gaussian noise (fGn), which is a discrete version of fractional Brownian motion. See Mandelbrot (1968) and Mandelbrot and Wallis (1969). The fractional difference and FARMA models, discussed in Section 11.2, are useful tools for modeling Iff-type data. Notice that Definition 11.2 defines long-memory behavior at different frequencies from zero, that is, “cyclic long memory”. In Section 11.3, we discuss the Gegenbauer and GARMA models for cyclic long-memory behavior, and in Section 11.4, we discuss the k-factor Gegenbauer GARMA models for data with a variety of types of long-memory behavior. In Sections 11.5 and 11.6, we discuss estimation and forecasting for k-factor GARMA models, and in Section 11.7, we apply the results to atmospheric CO2 data.

## 11.2 Fractional Difference and FARMA Processes

A widely used tool for analyzing long-memory time series is the fractional difference, $(1 - B)^\alpha$, which for the (noninteger) $\alpha$ is defined by

$$(1-B)^\alpha f(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k \binom{\alpha}{k}B^k f(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k \binom{\alpha}{k}f(t-k),$$

where

$$\binom{\alpha}{k} = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha-k+1)k!}$$

and $\Gamma(z) = \int_0^{\infty} t^{z-1}e^{-t} dt$ is the gamma function. Using the fact that $\Gamma(x+1)=x\Gamma(x)$ for real $x$, it follows that

$$(1-B)^\alpha f(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k \frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha-k+1)k!}f(t-k) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \binom{\alpha}{k}f(t-k).$$

Remark: The fractional difference can be extended to the case where $\alpha$ is any real or complex number. For our purposes it suffices for $\alpha$ to be real. For a more general definition of the fractional difference and its applications, Gray and Zhang (1988), Granger and Joyeux (1980), Hosking (1981), and Geweke and Porter-Hudak (1983) proposed the fractional model defined in Definition 11.3.

## Definition 11.3

If $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} |\Gamma(k+d)| / |\Gamma(d)| < \infty$, then the linear process defined by

$$X_t - \mu = (1-B)^{-d}a_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\Gamma(k+d)}{\Gamma(d)k!}a_{t-k}, \quad t=0, \pm1, \pm2, ...$$

where $a_t$ is white noise, is called a fractional process. If $X$ is invertible, then the fractional model is written

$$(1-B)^d(X_t - \mu) = a_t.$$

We typically express the fractional model in the form given in Equation 11.5. Theorem 11.1 gives basic properties of the fractional model including stationarity and invertibility.

## Theorem 11.1

Let $X$, be a fractional process of the form Equation 11.4.
1. If $d < 0.5$, then $X$ is causal and stationary.
2. If $d > -0.5$, then $X$ is invertible.
3. The spectral density of $X$, is given by $S(f) = [4[\sin \pi f]^2]-d$, $0 < f \leq 0.5$ and $\lim_{f\to0} f^{2d}S(f)$ exists and is finite.
4. If $d \in (-0.5, 0.5)$, the autocorrelation function is given by

$$\rho_k = \frac{\Gamma(-d+1)\Gamma(k+d)}{\Gamma(d)\Gamma(k-d+1)}, \quad k=0, \pm1, ...$$

and $\rho_k \approx k^{2d-1}$ as $k \to \infty$, $\sigma_X^2 = \Gamma(-2d+1)/\Gamma(-d+1)$.
5. If $0 < d < 0.5$ then $X$, is long memory.

Proof: See Hosking (1981). The long-memory result in (5) follows from using Definition 11.1 and from (3) using Definition 11.2.

In Definition 11.4, we extend the idea of a FARMA process that can have both short and long-memory behavior.

## Definition 11.4

If $X$ is a causal, stationary, and invertible process satisfying

$$\phi(B)(1-B)^d(X_t-\mu) = \theta(B)a_t, \quad (11.6)$$

where $a_t$ is white noise, and $\phi(z)$ and $\theta(z)$ are $pth$ and $qth$-order polynomials, respectively, that share no common factors, $X$ is a fractional ARMA (FARMA($p,d,q$)) process.
