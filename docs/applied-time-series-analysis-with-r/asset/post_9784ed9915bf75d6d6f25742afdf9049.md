
## Applied Time Series Analysis with R

As we have indicated, the role of the autocorrelation in time series analysis is a central one. However, when it is desirable to determine the frequency or cyclic content of data, the relevant information in the autocorrelation is more readily conveyed through the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation, called the spectral density, rather than through the autocorrelation itself. The power spectrum and spectral density, the primary tools for examining frequency content in time series data, are defined in the following.

## Definition 1.11

Let $[X(t); t \in (-\infty,\infty)]$ be a stationary time series.

- 1. The power spectrum, $P_X(f)$, is defined by

$$P_X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i f h}\gamma(h)dh, \quad (1.23)$$

$-\infty < f < \infty, i = \sqrt{-1}$, is the covariance function.

- 2. The spectral density is a normalized version of $P_X(f)$. Specifically, we define the spectral density, $S_X(f)$, by

$$S_X(f) = \frac{P_X(f)}{\sigma_X^2} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i f h}\rho(h)dh. \quad (1.24)$$

The power spectrum will often be referred to as simply the spectrum. Definition 1.11 requires the index set $T$ to be continuous. It is possible to give a more general definition that also includes the discrete case. However, to do so would require us to use the Stieltjes integral in the definition. The reader should refer to Priestley (1981) and Koopmans (1974) for this approach. We have chosen instead to consider the discrete parameter case separately.

## Definition 1.12

Let $[X_i; t=0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots]$ be a stationary time series and let $|f| \le 0.5$.

- 1. The power spectrum of $X_i$ is defined by

$$P_X(f) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i k f}\gamma_k. \quad (1.23)$$

## Stationary Time Series

2. The spectral density is defined by

$S_X(f) = \frac{P_X(f)}{\sigma_X^2} = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i k f}\rho_k. \quad (1.26)$

If

$\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} |\gamma_k| < \infty, \quad (1.27)$

then it can be shown that the series in Equations 1.25 and 1.26 converge and

$\gamma_k = \int_{-0.5}^{0.5} e^{2\pi i k f}P_X(f)df, \quad (1.28)$

that is, the spectrum and the autocovariance function for Fourier transform pairs. Consequently, given the spectrum of a stationary process satisfying Equation 1.27, the autocovariance function can be obtained mathematically from it and vice versa. The results in Equations 1.25 and 1.28 show that the process conveys this information about the process. The autocovariance conveys this information in the time domain while the spectrum expresses the equivalent information in the frequency domain. We will see that for a particular application, one of these may provide more understandable information about the the process than the other does. A similar relationship exists between the autocorrelation and the spectral density.

We leave it as an exercise to show that $P_X(f)$ and $S_X(f)$ in Definition 1.12 can be written as

$P_X(f) = \sigma_X^2 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \gamma_k \cos(2\pi k f), \quad (1.29)$

and

$S_X(f) = 1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho_k \cos(2\pi k f), \quad (1.30)$

again with the restriction $|f| \leq 0.5$.

Nyquist frequency: Definition 1.12 seems the rather natural discrete version of Definition 1.11 except for the restriction on the range of the parameter $f$. For discrete time data sampled at unit increments, the shortest period that can be observed is a period of 2, that is, the highest frequency is 0.5. This is called the Nyquist frequency. For example, consider the two signals $Y_t = \cos(2\pi(0.3)t)$ and $Z_t = \cos(2\pi(1.3)t)$, which have frequencies 0.3 and 1.3, respectively. These signals are defined along a continuum, but we consider the case where they are sampled at the integers. In Figure 1.13, we show an overlay of these two signals, and it is interesting to note that the two signals are equal to each other at integer values of $t$. These common values are indicated by the circles on the plot. Consequently, the two signals are indistinguishable when sampled at the integers. This phenomenon is called *aliasing*, since the frequency $f=1.3$ “appears” to be $f=0.3$. For discrete data, because would be impossible to detect a period less than 2 sample units, it should not be surprising that we must restrict ourselves to $|f| \le 0.5$ in the discrete case. It should be noted that if higher frequencies are present, in order to be able to detect them we would need to sample more rapidly. This motivates the following definition.

## Definition 1.13

If data are sampled at increments of $\Delta$, then the Nyquist frequency is $1/(2\Delta)$, and the shortest observable period is $2\Delta$.
