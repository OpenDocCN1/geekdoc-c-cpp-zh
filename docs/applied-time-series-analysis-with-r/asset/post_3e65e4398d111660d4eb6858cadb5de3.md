That is, since the time generating the realization in Figure 12.1a is not stationary, the spectral estimation resulting in Figure 12.1c was inappropriately applied. Also, since the spectrum and spectral density are defined for stationary processes, they do not show time-frequency behavior. That is, the spectral density estimates shown in Figure 12.1 do not give any indication that Realization 1 has two distinct frequencies, each over a separate time period. In Realization 2 the two frequencies are present simultaneously throughout the entire realization.

To further illustrate this point consider the linear chirp signal in Figure 12.2a (chirp). The frequency (autocorrelation) structure is clearly changing, and it is seen that the realization becomes increasingly higher frequency in time. For example, the first cycle in the data is roughly of length 50 while the last cycle is of length 6. That is, the frequency behavior goes from approximately $f=0.02$ at the beginning of the realization to around $f=0.14$ on the end. Although this is clearly a realization from a nonstationary process, we again apply the Parzen window spectral density estimator, and the resulting spectral density estimate is shown in Figure 12.2b, where we can see how the spectrum has been spread in an attempt to adjust for the changing frequencies. In Figure 12.2b the “peak” in the spectrum extends from $f=0$ to a little below $f=0.2$ reflecting the fact that the frequency behavior varies within this range across time. The concept of a “spread spectrum” was introduced in Example 1.16.

In summary, we note that since the spectrum “assumes” that the time series is stationary, its frequency behavior does not change over time, it does not provide insight regarding any-frequency behavior in the data. Note, for example, that the realization in Figure 12.2a was reversed in direction so the frequencies decreased in time, the Parzen spectral density estimate would not reflect the change in direction. In essence using classical spectral estimation methods, the frequency content at a given frequency is found by averaging over time. If characteristics of the signal depends on time, then these classical methods are not appropriate and can lead to misleading results.

## 12.2 Window-Based Methods that Localize the “Spectrum” in Time

A method for obtaining frequency information that is localized in time is needed in order to address the concerns raised in Section 12.1. In this section we discuss two methods: the Gabor spectrogram and the Wigner-Ville spectrum. These are both window-based modifications of unbiased Fourier transform-based methods. In Section 12.3 we discuss wavelets which provide an entirely different method for dealing with this situation.

The objective of window-based methods is to deal with the problem of TVF by breaking the time axis into time segments (windows) that are sufficiently narrow that stationarity is a reasonable assumption within the window. For example, the data in Figure 12.2a show a dramatic change in frequency behavior from the beginning of the series to the end. However, when restricted to smaller windows (say of width 25) there is not much frequency change within these windows. As a consequence, Fourier-based methods are reasonably appropriate within these time windows.

### 12.2.1 Gabor Spectrogram

The Gabor spectrum (or spectrogram) is a widely used window-based tool for spectral analysis. The continuous Fourier transform of the square integrable function, $g(x)$, is given by

```
$G(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi ifx}g(x)dx.$
```

Window-based methods, such as the Gabor spectrogram, are based on a window function, $h(t)$, for which $|h(t)| \to 0$ as $|t| \to \infty$. The short-time Fourier transform (STFT) is defined by

```
$G(t, f) = |\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-j2\pi fx}g(x)h(x-t)dx|    (12.4)$
```

The function $h(t)$ is defined in such a way that $h(x-t)g(x)$ localizes the signal around $t$ so that for a given $t, G(t, f)$ in Equation 12.4 represents a “Fourier transform” localized around $t$. We represent this by expressing the Fourier transform in Equation 12.4 as a function of two variables: $t$ and $f$. The Gabor transform is a special case of STFT that uses a Gaussian window

```
$h(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha}}e^{-t^2/4\alpha}    (12.5)$
```

The parameter, $\alpha$, controls the window width, and the Gabor spectrogram is a two-dimensional plot of

```
$S(t, f) = |G(t, f)|^2    (12.6)$
```

Figure 12.3a and $b$ show Gabor spectrograms for the data in Figure 12.1a and $b$, respectively. Note that time is plotted along the horizontal axis, frequency along the vertical axis, and $S(t, f)$ is plotted using a gray scale where darker shades indicate larger values. In Figure 12.3a we see that $t=1$ through 100, the largest values of $S(t, f)$ are around $f=0.08$ and for $t=101$ through 200 the largest values seem to be at $f=0.2$. This is consistent with the observations revealing the spectral behavior of the data in Figure 12.1a. In Figure 12.3b we see that strong frequency behaviors are observed for $t \in [1, 200]$ at about $f=0.08$ and $f=0.2$ which is in agreement with the fact that the data in Figure 12.1b were from an AR(4) model with frequencies at $f=0$ and $f=0.2$. Since the AR(4) model is stationary, the frequency behavior does not change with time and these two frequencies appear for all $t$.

Figure 12.4a shows the Gabor transform for chirp data in Figure 12.2a. The largest values of $S(t, f)$ change linearly from $f=0$ at time $t=1$ to about $f=0.17$ at $t=256$. The frequency at $t=256$ is consistent with the previous observation that the last cycle in the data is of length 6, that is, $f/6=0.17$. A “snapshot” of the spectrogram at $t=256$ could be graphed by plotting frequency on the horizontal axis and $S(256, f)$ on the vertical axis. The resulting “spectral-type” plot would have a peak at about $f=0.17$. The corresponding snapshot at $t=100$ would have a peak at about $f=0.08$.

### 12.2.2 Wigner–Ville Spectrum

Recall that the spectrum for a stationary discrete time series defined on $t = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$ is defined in Equation 1.25 to be

```
$P(f) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \gamma(k)e^{-2\pi i fk},    (12.7)$
```

$-0.5 < f < 0.5$. However, for time series whose autocorrelation associated with time, Condition 3 in (12.1) does not hold. Letting $\gamma(t_1, t_2)$ denote the autocovariance between $X_{t_1}$ and $X_{t_2}$, the discrete Wigner–Ville spectrum can be defined by

```
$W(t, f) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \gamma(t+k/2, t-k/2)e^{-2\pi i fk},    (12.8)$
```

If $X_t$ is approximately stationary in a neighborhood of $t$, then $\gamma(t+k/2), t-k/2$ can be approximated by use of a (time domain) window centered at $t$, see Martin and Flandrin (1985) and Boashash (2003). Figure 12.3c and d show the Wigner–Ville time–frequency representation associated with the data in Figure 12.2a and $b$, respectively. It is seen that the information concerning time–frequency relationships are consistent with those using the Gabor spectrum shown in Figure 12.3a and $b$. Similarly, the Wigner–Ville plot in 12.4b for the chirp shows the same linearly increasing time–frequency relationship shown in Figure 12.4a.

**Using R:** The Gabor transform can be obtained using cgt in the package `Rwave` which is available on CRAN, and the Wigner–Ville plot can be obtained using the `tswge` function `wge`.

## 12.3 Wavelet Analysis

In this section we give an overview of wavelet analysis and its application to statistical time series analysis of nonstationary data. Wavelets provide an alternative to the window methods for investigating frequency behavior that changes across time. Wavelet analysis is a relatively recent methodology developed primarily in the mathematical sciences and engineering, and it proved to be a powerful tool for the analysis of localized behavior of data whose characteristics change with time. These methods can be viewed as variations of classical Fourier analysis in short waves (or “wavelets") are used in place of the classical sines and cosines.
