# EXAMPLE 7.1: MM ESTIMATION FOR MA(1)

For an MA(1) model, Equation 7.20 becomes

$$\hat{\rho}_1 = \frac{-\hat{\theta}_1}{1+\hat{\theta}_1^2}\quad (7.22)$$

which can be written as the quadratic equation

$$\hat{\rho}_1\hat{\theta}_1^2+\hat{\theta}_1+\hat{\rho}_1=0.\quad (7.23)$$

The Roots of Equation 7.23 are

$$\hat{\theta}_1 = \frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1-4\hat{\rho}_1^2}}{2\hat{\rho}_1}\quad (7.24)$$

If $|\hat{\rho}_1| < 0.5$, then from Equation 7.24, there are two distinct real solutions of Equation 7.23. The invertible solution is given by

$$\hat{\theta}_1 = \frac{-1+\pm\sqrt{1-4\hat{\rho}_1^2}}{2\hat{\rho}_1}$$

In, if $\hat{\rho}_1=0.5$ then Equation 7.24 specifies two roots of $\hat{\theta}_1=-1$, which does not result in an invertible model. Similarly, $\hat{\rho}_1=-0.5$ results in the non-invertible model with $\hat{\theta}_1=-1$. If $|\hat{\rho}_1| > 0.5$, no real solutions of Equation 7.23 exist, and the MM estimates are said to not exist. Recall that for an MA(1) process, if $|\hat{\rho}_1|$ is much larger than 0.5, an MA(1) is a question-model for the data.

In general, for $q>1$, the equations in 7.20 must be solved numerically using the Newton-Raphson algorithm or other procedures. MM estimates do not converge (and numerical root finding techniques do not converge) whenever there are no real solutions to 7.20. We discuss MM estimates here for completeness. We do not recommend their use. Because of the frequent failure of the numerical routines to converge, we recommend the use of the innovations algorithm (discussed in the following) to find preliminary estimates of the MA parameters.

## 2.2.2.2 MA(q) Estimation Using the Innovations Algorithm

Another approach to obtaining preliminary estimates of the MA parameters involves use of the innovations algorithm (see Blackwell and Davis, 1991).

For a stationary process, $X_t$, let $\hat{X}_m(1)$ denote the best one-step ahead predictor of $X_{m+1}$ given data $X_l, \dots, X_m$. These predictors can be shown to follow the relationship

$$\hat{X}_n(1) = 0, \text{if } n=0$$
$$\sum_{j=1}^n \theta_{nj}(\hat{X}_{n-1-j} - \hat{X}_{n-j}(1)), \text{if } n=1, 2, \dots \quad (7.25)$$

For some constants $\theta_{ij}=1, 2, \dots, n$. (see Blackwell and Davis, 1992). That is, the one-step ahead predictor is expressed as a linear combination of the prediction errors (innovations) $\hat{a}_{k+1} = X_{k+1} - \hat{X}(1)$. The estimated coefficients $\hat{\theta}_{m1}, \hat{\theta}_{mm}$ and noise variance $\hat{v}_m, m=1, 2, \dots$ are calculated recursively as follows:

$$\hat{v}_0 = \hat{\gamma}_0$$
$$\hat{\theta}_{mm-k} = \hat{v}_{k-1}^{-1} \left( \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \hat{\theta}_{m,m-j} \hat{v}_{k-1-j} - \hat{\gamma}_k \right)$$

for $k=0, 1, \dots, m-1$

$$\hat{v}_m = \hat{\gamma}_0 - \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} \hat{\theta}_{m,j}^2 \hat{v}_j$$

The innovations algorithm can be used to fit MA models such as

$$X_t = a_t - \hat{\theta}_{m1}a_{t-1} - \dots - \hat{\theta}_{mm}a_{t-m}$$

where $m=1, 2, \dots$ This technique is closely related to the use of the Durbin-Levinson algorithm given in Equation 7.9 which produces YW estimates of the AR parameters by recursively solving the YW equations. Unlike the YW estimates, the innovations estimates $\hat{\theta}_1, \dots, \hat{\theta}_q$ are not consistent estimates of $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_q$. In order to obtain consistent estimates, it is necessary to use $\hat{\theta}_{m1}, \dots, \hat{\theta}_{mq}$ where $m$ is substantially larger than $q$. In practice, $m$ is typically increased until $\hat{\theta}_{m1}, \dots, \hat{\theta}_{mq}$ stabilize, they do not change much as $m$ continues to increase. For $100 \le n \le 500$, sufficient stability will often be obtained for $m \le n/4$ (see Blackwell and Davis, 1991).

If the true autocorrelations are known, then the $p$ equations for $k=q+1, q+1, \dots, q+p$ can be solved by $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p$. These are called the extended WYW equations. The extended WYW estimates of the AR parameters are obtained by replacing the true autocorrelations, $\rho_j$, and solving the resulting system of $p$ equations in $p$ unknowns. The sample autocorrelations, $\hat{\rho}_j$, are used instead. The WYW estimates arise earlier from the special case in which $q=0$. However, unlike the case $q=0$, when $q>0$ the extended WYW estimates need not result in a stationary model. Choi (1986) gives an algorithm for solution of the extended WYW equations.

### 7.2.3 Tsay-Tiao Estimates of the AR Parameters

Tsay and Tiao (1984) obtained estimates of the AR parameters of an ARMA(p,q) model motivated by iterated AR(k) regressions, and recursively adding MA type terms to an AR(k) model. If $X_t$ follows an ARMA(p,q) model, then an AR(p,q) model

$$X_t = \sum_{j=1}^p \phi_{jp}^{(0)} X_{t-j} + e_{pt}^{(0)}$$

$t=p+1, \dots, n$ is initially fit to the data. The least squares estimates of the model parameters are denoted by $\phi_{jp}^{(p)}$. Based upon these estimates, the residuals

$$\hat{e}_{pt}^{(p)} = X_t - \sum_{j=1}^p \phi_{jp}^{(p)} X_{t-j}$$

are obtained. Note that if $q > 0$, then $\hat{\epsilon}_{p}^{(0)}$ is not an estimator of white noise and will contain information about the model. The first iterated regression model

$$X_t = \sum_{j=1}^p \hat{\phi}_j^{(1)} X_{t-j} + \beta_p^{(1)} \hat{\epsilon}_{p,j-1}^{(0)} + \hat{\epsilon}_{pt}^{(1)}$$

$t=p+2, \dots, n$ is formed. Let $\hat{\phi}_j^{(1)}$ and $\hat{\beta}_p^{(1)}$ denote the corresponding least squares estimates. If $q=1$, then the $\hat{\phi}_j^{(1)}$ is consistent for $\phi_j=1, \dots, p$. If $q>1$, the procedure is continued by calculating residuals

$$\hat{\epsilon}_{pt}^{(1)} = X_t - \sum_{j=1}^p \hat{\phi}_j^{(1)} X_{t-j} + \hat{\beta}_p^{(1)} \hat{\epsilon}_{p,j-1}^{(0)}$$

and forming a second iterated regression model. Tsay and Tiao (TT) estimates $\phi_j, j=1, \dots, p$ for an ARMA(p,q) model are given by $\hat{\phi}_j^{(q)}, j=1, \dots, p$. Tsay and Tiao (1984) showed that it is not necessary to go through this entire procedure, but that the TT estimates $\hat{\phi}_p^{(0)}$ can be obtained using the recursion

$$\hat{\phi}_{k+1}^{(m)} = \frac{\hat{\epsilon}_{t-k}^{(m-1)}}{\hat{\epsilon}_{t,k}^{(m-1)}} \hat{\phi}_{kk}^{(m-1)}$$

where $j=1, \dots, k$ with $k \geq 1$. The recursion is initialized with $\hat{\phi}_p^{(m-1)}=-1$ and with estimates $\hat{\phi}_j^{(0)}$ for the case $q=0$ obtained using a method for estimating parameters of an AR process. Tsay and Tiao (1984) used least squares to initialize this recursion. The TT estimates are asymptotically equivalent to the WY estimates when $X_t$ is stationary, and they are consistent for $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p$ when the characteristic equation $\phi(z)=0$ has roots on the unit circle. These estimates are not calculated in `tswge`. In the GW-WINKS software, the TT estimates are initialized using Bug estimates due to their stability properties. We refer to these as Bug/TT estimates in the text. GW-WINKS refers to them as Bug/Tiao estimates.

### 7.2.3.3 Estimating the MA Parameters

Consider the ARMA(p,q) model $\phi(B)X_t$. Since $Y_t = \theta(B)a_t$, preliminary estimates of the MA parameters of ARMA(p,q) can be obtained by first estimating the AR parameters (using a procedure such as Bug/TT) and then fitting an MA(q) model to $Y_t = \theta(B)X_t$. This procedure is not currently implemented in `tswge`.
