Ljung and Box (1978) show that $L$ in Equation 9.3 is approximately distributed as $\chi^2$ with $K$ degrees of freedom when the data are white noise. When the data to be tested are residuals from a fitted ARMA$(p,q)$ model, then the test is statistically $\chi^2$ with $K-p-q$ degrees of freedom. Examination of Equation 9.3 shows that the test statistic measures the size of the first $K$ sample autocorrelations as a group, so that large values of $L$ suggest the are are not white. Thus, the null hypothesis of white noise is rejected if $L > \chi^2_{1-\alpha}(K-p-q)$ where $\chi^2_{1-\alpha}$ denotes the $(1-\alpha) 	imes 100\%$ percentile of the $\chi^2$ distribution with $m$ degrees of freedom. In the examples that we use the Ljung-Box test with $K=24$ and $K=48$, which is consistent with the value of $K$ used by Box et al. (2008). For small sizes ($n \le 100$), Box et al. (2008) and Ljung (1986) recommend the use of smaller values of $K$.

# 9.1.3 Other Tests for Randomness

There are many other tests for white noise that could be used. Testing for white noise is essentially a test for randomness, so any such test (e.g., runs tests) could be used in this regard. See Davis and Jones (1968) for a test based on the log periodogram. Li (1988) and Chan (1994) have developed tests to be more robust than the Ljung–Box test to non-normal white noise. Peña and Rodriguez (2002) have developed a portmanteau that is claimed to be a more powerful alternative to the Ljung-Box test in the presence of normal noise.
