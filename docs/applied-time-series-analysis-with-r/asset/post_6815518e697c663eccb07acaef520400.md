## EXAMPLE 3.5: AUTOCORRELATIONS OF AN AR(1) MODEL

For the case of the AR(1) model $X_t - \phi_1 X_{t-1} = a_t$, the autocorrelation $\rho_k$ satisfies the equation

$$\rho_k - \phi_1\rho_{k-1} = 0, k > 0. \quad (3.50)$$

Since there is only one root (and no repeated roots), we use Equation 3.49, and $\rho_k$ satisfies. $\rho_k = C_{r_1}^k, k > 0$ Also, for an AR(1) model, $r_1 = 1/\phi_1$, so this can be rewritten

$$\rho_k = C_1\phi_1^k, k > 0. \quad (3.51)$$

Since $\rho_0 = 1$, it follows from Equation 3.50 that $\rho_1 - \phi_1\rho_0 = 0$. Thus, $\rho_1 = \phi_1$, and thus $C_1 = 1., so

$$\rho_k = C_1\phi_1^k, k \ge 0. \quad (3.52)$$

Since $\rho_k = \rho_{k}$, the autocorrelation function for an AR(1) process satisfies $\rho_k = C_1\phi_1^k$ which is the result derived earlier in Equation 3.32.

NOTE: In general, for the $m$-order difference equation with no repeated roots, the values $C_1, ..., C_m$ are chosen in such a way that the starting values, $g_0, g_1, ..., g_{m-1}$ also satisfy Equation 3.46 although they do not satisfy Equation 3.44.

NOTE: In Equation 3.77, we will give an insight into Equation 3.49 that provides better insight into the behavior of the autocorrelation function of an AR(p) model.

### 3.2.6 Spectral Density of an AR(p) Model

Since $\psi(z) = \phi^{-1}(z)$ for an AR(p) process, it follows from Equation 2.8 that the spectrum is given by

$P_X(f) = \sigma_a^2 |\psi(e^{-2\pi f})|^2$
$\frac{\sigma_a^2}{|\phi(e^{-2\pi f})|^2}$
$|1 - \phi_1 e^{-2\pi f} - \phi_2 e^{-4\pi f} - \dots - \phi_p e^{-2p\pi f}|^2$.

Consequently, the spectral density is

$S_X(f) = \frac{\sigma_X^2}{|1 - \phi_1 e^{-2\pi f} - \phi_2 e^{-4\pi f} - \dots - \phi_p e^{-2p\pi f}|^2}$.

where $\sigma_X^2$ is given in Equation 3.43.

### 3.2.7 AR(2) Model

Understanding the features of an AR(1) and AR(2) model is critical to gaining insight into the more general AR(p) model. We have already discussed the AR(1) model, so, in this section, we consider in some detail the AR(2) model

$X_t - \phi_1 X_{t-1} - \phi_2 X_{t-2} = a_t$.

#### 3.2.7.1 Autocorrelations of an AR(2) Model

For $k > 0$, the autocorrelation function of an AR(2) model satisfies the difference equation

$\rho_k - \phi_1 \rho_{k-1} - \phi_2 \rho_{k-2} = 0$.

When the roots are distinct, the autocorrelation function is given

$\rho_k = C_1 r_1^{-k} + C_2 r_2^{-k}, k > 0$,

where $C_1$ and $C_2$ are uniquely determined by $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$. These first two autocorrelations can be obtained by solving the Yule–Walker equations

$\rho_1 = \phi_1 + \phi_2\rho_2$
$\rho_2 = \phi_1\rho_1 + \phi_2$,

which gives

$\rho_1 = \frac{\phi_1}{1-\phi_2}$
$\rho_2 = \frac{\phi_1^2 + \phi_2 - \phi_2^2}{1-\phi_2} \quad (3.56)$

The roots $r_1$ and $r_2$ in Equation 3.55 are (possibly complex) solutions to the characteristic equation $1 - \phi_1z - \phi_2z^2 = 0$.
Therefore,

$r_j = \frac{-\phi_1 \pm \sqrt{\phi_1^2 + 4\phi_2}}{2\phi_2} , \quad j=1,2$.

Note that the characteristic equation can be written in factored form as

$1-\phi_1z - \phi_2z^2 = (1-r_1^{-1}z)(1-r_2^{-1}z)$
$=-(r_1^{-1} + r_2^{-1})z + r_1^{-1}r_2^{-1}z^2$,

and thus

$\phi_1 = r_1^{-1} + r_2^{-1}. \quad (3.57)$

$\phi_2 = -r_2^{-1}. \quad (3.58)$

When $X_t$ is stationary, Theorem 3.2 implies that $|r_1^{-1}| < 1$ and $|r_2^{-1}| < 1$, so it follows from Equation 3.58 that $|\phi_2| < 1$.
Additionally, from Equations 3.43 and 3.56, it follows that

$\sigma_X^2 = \frac{(1-\phi_2)\sigma_{\eta}^2}{(1+\phi_2)[(1-\phi_2)^2-\phi_1^2}} \quad (3.59)$

Case 1—Roots are distinct: As previously mentioned, in this case, $\rho_i$ is given by Equation 3.55 with $C_1$ and $C_2$ uniquely determined by Equation 3.56. So it follows that

$\rho_1 = C_1r_1^{-1} + C_2r_2^{-1} = \frac{\phi_1}{1-\phi_2}$
$\rho_2 = C_1r_1^{-2} + C_2r_2^{-2} = \frac{\phi_1^2+\phi_2-\phi_2^2}{1-\phi_2}$

Denoting $\alpha_1 = r_1^{-1}$ and $\alpha_2 = r_2^{-1}$, and solving these equations

$C_1 = \frac{\alpha_1(1-\alpha_2^2)}{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)(1+\alpha_1\alpha_2)}$

and

$C_2 = \frac{-\alpha_2(1-\alpha_1^2)}{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)(1+\alpha_1\alpha_2)}$

which yields

$\rho_k = \frac{(1-\alpha_2^2)\alpha_1^{k+1}-(1-\alpha_1^2)\alpha_2^{k+1}}{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)(1+\alpha_1\alpha_2)}, \quad k \ge 0. \quad (3.60)$

We consider two cases in which the roots are distinct.

- 1. Real roots, that is, $\phi_1^2+4\phi_2>0$: In this case, the characteristic polynomial can be written in factored form

$\phi(z) = (1-r_1^{-1}z)(1-r_2^{-1}z)$

$r_1$ and $r_2$ are real, and $\rho_k$ in Equations 3.55 and 3.60 can be seen as a mixture of damped exponentials or damped oscillations that decay to zero as $k$ increases, since $|\alpha_1|<1$ and $|\alpha_2|<1$.

- 2. Complex roots, that is, $\phi_1^2+4\phi_2<0$: In this case, the second-order characteristic polynomial is irreducible (i.e., $r_1$ and $r_2$ are not real). The roots $r_1$ and $r_2$ are complex conjugates and thus so are $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$.

We use the notation $\alpha_1 = a + bi$ and $\alpha_2 = a - bi$. Now, $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ are given by

$a \pm bi = \frac{\phi_1}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{-(\phi_1^2 + 4\phi_2)}}{2}} i.$

$R = |\alpha_1| = \sqrt{-\phi_2}, \quad (3.61)$

and $\cos 2\pi f_0 = a/\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \phi_1/(\sqrt{-\phi_2})$. Substituting into Equation 3.60, we get

$\rho_k = (\sqrt{-\phi_2}) \left\ \frac{\sin((k+1)2\pi f_0 + \phi_2\sin(k-1)2\pi f_0)}{(1-\phi_2)\sin 2\pi f_0} \right\}.$

Let $\psi$ be such that $tan \psi = (1-\phi_2)/(1+\phi_2)\tan 2\pi f_0$, we can rewrite $\rho_k$ as

$\rho_k = (\sqrt{-\phi_2}) \left\ \frac{\sin(2\pi k f_0 + \psi)}{\sin \psi} \right\., \quad (3.62)$

where $\cos 2\pi f_0 = \phi_1/(\sqrt{-\phi_2})$. That is, the second factor of Equation 3.62 is periodic with frequency

$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cos^{-1} \left( \frac{\phi_1}{2\sqrt{-\phi_2}} \right), \quad (3.63)$

$\rho_k$ is a damped sinusoidal function, and we call $f_0$ the *natural frequency* or system frequency.
