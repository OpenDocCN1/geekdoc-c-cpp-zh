Long-Memory Processes

# 11.8 Modeling Atmospheric CO2 Data
Using Long-Memory Models

Figure 11.15a shows 382 monthly atmospheric CO2 readings from March 1958 until December 1989 at the summit of Mauna Loa in Hawaii (Keeling et al., 1989). The CO2 data show a clear increase during this frame of 31 years and 10 months. Close examination of CO2 levels shows that there is an annual pattern in CO2 levels which might be high in the summer and low in the winter. Figure 11.1b and c show the sample autocorrelations and a spectral density estimated by the Parzen window with truncation point M = 50. Clearly the sample autocorrelations damp very slowly, and there are three distinct peaks in the spectral density. As first step in modeling the data we use the overfit procedure discussed in Chapter 3 and fit an AR(16) model for which the associated factor is shown in Table 11.3. Other models were examined and were consistent with those observed. The three sets of complex conjugate roots with frequencies 0.084, 0.167, and 0.001 are very close to the unit circle. The first two correspond to 12 and 6 months cycles (1/0.084 ≈ 12 and 1/0.167 ≈ 6), while the third pair corresponds to either a long cycle, or more, to unit roots. We will the use of a k-factor GARMA to fit these data which have long-memory or nonstationary characteristics. The fact that these are monthly data and there is some annual variation in the data suggests using a factor of the form 1 – B¹² or (1 – B)² because of the upward trend. However, comparison of the factor table in Table 11.3 with the factor table for 1 – B¹² in Table 5.5 shows that although the factors associated with expected 1 – B¹² are visible, the first three factors in Table 11.3 are much closer to the unit circle than other factors. This differs from the factor table for the log airline data in Table 8.5 which showed a strong tendency for the roots of (1 – z)(1 – z⁻²) = 0 to be about the same distance
