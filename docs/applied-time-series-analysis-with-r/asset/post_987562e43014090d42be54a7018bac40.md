## 11.8 Modeling Atmospheric CO2 Data Using Long-Memory Models

Figure 11.15a shows 382 monthly atmospheric CO2 readings from March 1958 until December 1989 at the summit of Mauna Loa in Hawaii (Keeling et al., 1989). The CO2 data show a clear increase during this frame of 31 years and 10 months. Close examination of CO2 levels shows that there is an annual pattern in CO2 levels which might be high in the summer and low in the winter. Figure 11.1b and c show the sample autocorrelations and a spectral density estimated by the Parzen window with truncation point M = 50. Clearly the sample autocorrelations damp very slowly, and there are three distinct peaks in the spectral density. As first step in modeling the data we use the overfit procedure discussed in Chapter 3 and fit an AR(16) model for which the associated factor is shown in Table 11.3. Other models were examined and were consistent with those observed. The three sets of complex conjugate roots with frequencies 0.084, 0.167, and 0.001 are very close to the unit circle. The first two correspond to 12 and 6 months cycles (1/0.084 ≈ 12 and 1/0.167 ≈ 6), while the third pair corresponds to either a long cycle, or more, to unit roots. We will the use of a k-factor GARMA to fit these data which have long-memory or nonstationary characteristics. The fact that these are monthly data and there is some annual variation in the data suggests using a factor of the form 1 – B¹² or (1 – B)² because of the upward trend. However, comparison of the factor table in Table 11.3 with the factor table for 1 – B¹² in Table 5.5 shows that although the factors associated with expected 1 – B¹² are visible, the first three factors in Table 11.3 are much closer to the unit circle than other factors. This differs from the factor table for the log airline data in Table 8.5 which showed a strong tendency for the roots of (1 – z)(1 – z⁻²) = 0 to be about the same distance from the unit circle.

We conclude that an “airline model” (i.e., a model containing the factors $(1 - B - B^2)$) is not appropriate for these data.

Since the factor table in Table 11.3 strongly suggests two positive unit roots, we begin modeling by twice differencing the data. The twice differenced data and the associated autocorcorrelations are shown in Figure 11.16a and b. The sample autocorcorrelations of the twice data, shown in Figure 11.16b, have the form of a slowly damping of two sinusoidal components. This is suggestive of a 2-factor GARMA model, and we will proceed to model the data using this model. The two frequencies associated with roots close the unit circle in Table 11.3 are 0.084 and 0.166. For the annual cycle, we use $f_1 = 1/12$ and $f_2 = 1/6$. This $u_1 = \cos(2\pi f_1) = \cos(\pi/6) = 0.866$ and $u_2 = 0.5$. Then, will consider models containing either the Gegenbauer factors $(1 - 1.732B + B^2)^{k_1}$ and $(1 - B + B^2)^{k_2}$ or the nonstationary ARMA factors $1 - 1.732B + B^2$ and $1 - B + B^2$. Note that the two differences were applied to the data using as a factor $(1 - B)^2$. Therefore, the 2-factor GARMA model suggested for this data is given by:

$$\phi(1-B)^2(1 - 1.732B + B^2)^{k_1}(1 - B + B^2)^{k_2}(X_t - \mu) = \theta(B)_{a_t}, \quad (11.33)$$

where $\phi(B)$ and $\theta(B)$ are of orders $p$ and $q$. Woodward et al. (1998) examined MLE estimation and the use of the estimates $\hat{\lambda}_1 = \hat{\lambda}_2 = 0.49$ shows that the best job of removing a long-memory property 12-month cycle. The data were transformed using $(1-B)^2(1-1.732B+B^2)^{0.49}(1-B+B^2)^{0.49}$, and usual identification techniques selected an AR(7) for the transformed data. The final 2-factor GARMA model for this set is:

$$(1 - 1.732B + B^2)^{0.49}(1 - B + B^2)^{0.49}\phi(B)(X_t - \bar{X}) = a_t,$$

where $\phi(B)$ represents the Burg estimates:

$$\hat{\phi}(B)=1+2.32B+3.23B^2+3.52B^3+3.24B^4+3.27B^5+3.25B^6-3.31B^7+3.29B^8+3.09B^9+2.86B^{10}+2.54B^{11}+1.83B^{12}+0.91B^{13}+0.17B^{14}-0.21B^{15}-0.20B^{16}-0.08B^{17},$$

with $\bar{X}=2.57$ and $\hat{\sigma}_2^2=1.5$. Forecasts based on this model for the last 60 months are shown in Figure 11.15a with the actual values. It can be seen that the forecasts are excellent, maintaining the cycles and upward trend in the data.

As an alternative model for the $\text{CO}_2$ data, we consider the ARUMA(11,6,0) process suggested by the factor table in Table 11.3. The transformed by the sixth-order nonstationary factor $(1-B)(1-1.732B+B^2)(1-B+B^2)$ were modeled as an AR(11), and $\phi_t(B)$ based on the Burg estimates is given by:

$$\hat{\phi}(B)=1+3.64B+6.99B^2+8.92B^3+7.84B^4+4.07B^5-2.28B^6-2.99B^7-2.14B^9-0.90B^{10}-0.20B^{11}.$$

Figure 11.17b shows the forecasts for the same 60 observations considered in Figure 11.17a. The forecasts based on Equation 11.34 have the same cycle behavior as the data, but they fail to follow the upward trend after one or two cycles. Woodward et al. (1998) considered the use of an AR(17) to model the transformed data in this case, and the associated forecasts were poorer than those obtained with the AR(11). Woodward et al. (1998) show that for this data set the ARUMA model tends to have smaller SME in short-term forecasts while the forecasts based on the 2-factor GARMA model perform better in the long term.

## Appendix 11A: R Commands

### 11A.1 Basic Computation and Plot Routines for Chapter 11

- a. `true.farma.aut.wge(d,phi,theta,lag.max,vara,trunc)` calculates the true autocorrelations and autocariances associated with a fixed GARMA model of the form in Equation 11.5.
  - d is the fractional difference parameter Equation 11.6
  - phi is a vector of AR parameters (using signs as in this text)
  - theta is a vector of MA parameters (using signs as in text)
  - lag.max is the maximum lag at which the autocorrelations and autocovariances will be calculated (default=25)
  - vara is the white noise variance (default=1)
  - trunc is the truncation point in the infinite series expansion of the FARMA autocovariance (default=1000). See proof of Theorem 2.4 in Hosking (1981).
  - plot is a logical variable: TRUE (default) = plot autocorrelations, FALSE = no plot

- The function outputs:
  - acf (the true autocorrelations from lags 0 to lag.max)
  - acv (the true autocovariances from lags 0 to lag.max)

NOTES:

- 1. You may want to use the utility function mult.wge if you know the factors of the AR or MA model instead of the coefficients.
- 2. max(p,q+1) $\le 25$
