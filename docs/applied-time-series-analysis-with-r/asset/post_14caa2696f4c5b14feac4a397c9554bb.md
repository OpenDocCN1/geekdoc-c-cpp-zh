## 6.9 Forecasts Using Multiplicative Seasonal ARIMA Models

In this section, we briefly explain the forecasting technique for ARIMA models discussed in Section 6.8 to the more general case of the nonstationary multiplicative seasonal model

$\phi(B)\lambda(B^s)\Lambda(B^s)(X_t - \mu) = \theta(B)\Theta(B^s)a_t, \quad (6.47)$

where the roots of $\phi(z) = 0, \Phi(z) = 0, \theta(z) = 0$, and $\Theta(z) = 0$ all lie outside the unit circle, while the roots of $\lambda(z) = 0$ and $\Lambda(z) = 0$ lie on the unit circle. We let

$\phi^{(s)}(B) = \phi(B)\lambda(B^s)\Lambda(B^s)$

so that $\phi^{(n)}(B)$ is a $(p+d+P_s+D)$-th order operator with coefficients, $\phi_1^{(n)}, \phi_2^{(n)}, \dots, \phi_{p+d+P_s+D}^{(n)}$. Viewing Equation 6.47 as a list of stationary models whose roots of the characteristic equation approach the roots associated with the nonstationary components of Equation 6.47, we obtain a forecast equation analogous to Equation 6.36.

### EXAMPLE 6.6: FORECASTS FROM A BASIC SEASONAL MODEL

Consider the simple nonstationary seasonal model

$(1-B^s)(X_t - \mu) = a_t, \quad (6.48)$

which was previously discussed in Section 5.3. That is, in this case $p=d=P=q=0$ and $D_t=1$. Realizations of Equation 6.48 will be characterized by a strong positive correlation between observations separated by multiples of s. Forecasts from Equation 6.48 are given by

$$\hat{X}_t(\ell) = \hat{X}_t(\ell-s) + (1-\ell)\hat{X}$$
$$\hat{X}_t(\ell-s),$$

which take the form

$$\hat{X}_{t-s+1} = \hat{X}_t(s+1) = \hat{X}_t(2s+1) = ...$$
$$\hat{X}_{t-s+2} = \hat{X}_t(s+2) = \hat{X}_t(2s+2) = ...$$
$$\hat{X}_t(s) = \hat{X}_t(2s) = \hat{X}_t(3s) = ...$$

Thus, for the simple model in Equation 6.48, the last s data values are repeatedly forecast into the future. A realization of length $n=48$ from the model

$$(1-B^{12})(X_t-10) = a_t,$$

is shown in Figure 6.8 with forecasts, $\hat{X}_{48}(\ell)$, $\ell=1, 2, ..., 24$. The nature of the seasonal of the data and the forecasts is clear.
Forecasts from the more general seasonal model

$$\phi(B)\Phi(B^s)(X_t-\mu) = \theta(B)\Theta(B^s)a_t,$$

for which the nonstationary components are seasonal, that is, $d=0$, will behave similarly, but the forecasts in this case will not be a precise copy of the last s data values observed.

## EXAMPLE 6.7: FORECASTING MONTHLY HOUSING STAR DATA

In 6.9, we show the new privately owned housing units authorized in the United States from January 1999 to December 2001 (months 1–36). The data set is included in base R under the name housing. The data are monthly and seems to be a clear seasonal trend, for example, small number of permits authorized in January, February, and December with the peak year being at about June. The model

$(1 - .0873B - .0802B^2 - .3941B^3)(1 - B^{12})(X - 135.9) = a_t$

was fit to the data (see [Chapters 7–9]() for estimation and modeling details), and the forecasts for the next 2 years are shown with the dotted lines. Although visually appears that the forecasts for the next 2 years are copies of the last year observed, this is not the case of the stationary AR(3) component in the model. For example in January 2001 there were 117 permits issued in January 2002 the prediction was that 120 permits would be issued.

## EXAMPLE 6.8: FORECASTING AIRLINE DATA

In Figure 1.25a, we showed the "log-airline data" examined by Box et al. (2008). These data consist of the natural logarithms of 144 monthly passenger counters, and a seasonal behavior can be clearly seen. That is, there is a strong relationship between the number of passengers in the current month and the number of passengers during the same month in the previous year, etc. Also, for the years represented, there seems to be a persistent upward trend.

In Section 5.3, we discussed our models of the form

$\phi(B)\Phi(B^5)(1-B^2)(X_t - \mu) = \theta(B)\Theta(B^5)a_t,$

and referred to as "airline models". Since +1 is a root of 1-z^2=0, the characteristic equation associated with the AR component of Equation 6.51 has two roots of +1. That is, the AR operator has a factor of (1-B)². Thus, in addition to the seasonal behavior associated with the model in Example 6.6, realizations from Equation 6.51 will tend to have persistent upward or downward trends much as we encountered in Example 6.5 for the model (1-B)^2(X_t-\mu)=a_t. Box et al. (2008) model the airline data with

$(1-B)(1-B^2)(X_t-\mu)=(1-0.4B)(1-0.6B^2)a_t,$

which is a special case of Equation 6.51. Other special cases of the model in Example 6.51 have been considered for the airline data. For example, Gray and Woodward (1981) considered the multiplicative seasonal ARIMA(12,1,0)×(0,1,0)_2 model

$\phi(B)(1-B)(1-B^2)(X_t-\mu)=a_t,$

where

$\phi_t(B)=1+0.36B+0.05B^2+0.14B^3+0.11B^4-0.04B^5-0.09B^6+0.02B^7$
$- 0.02B^8-0.17B^9+0.10B^{11}+0.38B^{12}$

Parzen (1980) proposed the ARIMA(1,0,0)×(1,1,0)_2 model of the form

$(1-0.74B)(1+0.38B^{12})(X_t-\mu)=a_t,$

Figure 6.10 shows forecasts, based on forecast origin t_0=108, for the multiplicative seasonal ARIMA(2,1,0)_2 model obtained by Gray and Woodward (1981). The data are shown as circles separated by solid lines and the forecasts for the last 36 values are shown as diamonds separated by dotted lines. These forecasts do an excellent job of forecasting both seasonality and the trending behavior in the data.
