## 12.3.1 Fourier Series Background

In order to fully appreciate wavelet methods, it is necessary to have some familiarity with the classical Fourier methods of wavelet analysis is an extension. For a brief review of Fourier series, see Appendix 12A.

As discussed in Section 1.5 and Appendix 12A, Fourier analysis involves expressing functions as linear combination of sines and cosines. 12A.1 states that a well-behaved function, $g$, defined on $[-\pi,\pi]$ can be expressed as a linear combination of the *basis functions* in the orthogonal systems $S_1=\{\cos kx, \sin kx; k=1, 2, ...\}$ or equivalently of $S_2=\{e^{ikx}, k=0, \pm1, ...\}$. The advantage of using $S_2$ is that the basis functions actually consist of one "fundamental" function, namely, $\phi(x)=e^{ix}$, and the individual functions are "integral dilations" of this function, that is, $e^{ikx}=\phi(kx)$. As $k$ increases, the function $\phi(kx)=\cos(kx)+i\sin(kx)$ increases frequency; that is, the integer $k$ controls the frequency content in the basis function $\phi(kx)$. Fourier analysis is based on the fact that when certain conditions hold, functions can be written as a linear combination of dilations of the single function $e^{ix}$, see Appendix 12A. ## 12.3.2 Wavelet Analysis Introduction

Suppose that $g$ is square integrable over the real line, that is, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)^2dx<\infty$. The class of such functions is denoted $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ where $\mathbb{R}=(-\infty,\infty)$. We desire a collection of basis functions (which are all variations of a single functional, $\psi(x)$ such that functions $g\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$ can be approximated arbitrarily using a finite linear combination of these elements. It should be noted that $\sin x, \cos x$ and $e^{ix}$ are not candidates for the desired basis functions since they are not in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$. In fact, a necessary condition for $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)^2dx<\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|g(x)|=0$. That is, the basis functions must decay to zero as $|x|\to\infty$, so intuitively short waves or "wavelets" are needed. In the wavelet analysis, it is common to use $\psi(2^{-jx})$, where $j$ is an integer, and $j$ controls the amount of damping in the basis function $\psi(2^{-jx})$ similar to the way $k$ controls the frequency behavior in the basis function $\phi(kx)$. Approximating functions $g$ that are defined over the entire real line would intuitively be difficult to accomplish simply using dilations of a single, quickly damping function. Thus, in wavelet analysis, we consider dilations and translations of the basis functions for purposes of estimating localized frequency behavior. That is, given a function $\psi(x)$, we use as our basis collection the

$\psi_{j,k}(x)=2^{-j}\psi(2^{-jx}x-k),$$ (12.9)

where $j$ and $k$ are integers that are referred to as the *dilation index* and *translation index*, respectively. The function $\psi(x)$ is called the mother wavelet since it “gives birth” to the entire collection of basis elements.

## 12.3.3 Fundamental Wavelet Approximation Result

The fundamental wavelet-based approximation result analogous to the Fourier series approximation is given in Theorem 12.1 that follows.

## Theorem 12.1

Let $\{\psi_{j,k}\}$ be an orthogonal wavelet basis, and let $g \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$. Then,

$g(x) = \sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty} \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} d_{j,k}\psi_{j,k}(x), \quad 12.15$

where the equality is in the mean-square sense and the wavelet transform coefficients, $d_{j,k}$, are given by

$d_{j,k} = \frac{1}{\|\psi||^2} \int g(t)\psi_{j,k}(t)dt, \quad 12.16$

where $|\psi||^2$ denotes $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(x)|^2dx$.

Proof: See Daubechies (1992) and Ogden (1997). Appendix 12A gives a formal derivation to show that if Equation 12.15 holds, then $d_{j,k}$ is given by Equation 12.16.

Equation 12.15 is analogous to the Fourier series representation in Equation 12A.3. Also, the coefficients, $d_{j,k}$ in Equation 12.1 correspond to the Fourier series coefficients, $\alpha_k$ in Equation 12A.4. In Fourier analysis large values of $\alpha_k^2$ are associated with data (in our time series) that have periodic content in the neighborhood of the frequency $f=k$. Similarly large values of $|d_{j,k}|$ in Equation 12.15 are associated with detail behavior of about $2^j$, that is, the "frequency" or "1/scale" is about $2^{-j}$. In Fourier analysis, $\alpha_k$ as defined in Equation 12A.4 are Fourier transforms of $g(x)$, and similarly the coefficients, $d_{j,k}$ defined in Equation 12.16 are referred to as (continuous) wavelet expressions.
