## 12.4 Concluding Remarks on Wavelets

This section on wavelets has a brief introduction of wavelet methods with a focus on time series applications. Wavelets have proven to be very useful in a variety of applications. We have discussed the use of wavelets for time–frequency analysis. We have discussed the fact that the wavelet transformation can provide a sparse representation of a data set, that is, to characterize a time series or image based on a small fraction of the number of values in the original data set. This capability proves to be very useful in applications. For example, these sparse representations lead to useful data compression methods such as the common JPEG image which is a compressed image format based on the discrete cosine transform. Strang and Nguyen (1997) for further discussion of data compression techniques and applications. Also consider the application of image analysis in which statistical decisions need to be made concerning the detection of certain features of an image. For example, in the analysis of a statistic map from an fMRI experiment, investigators wish to detect regions of brain activity. A typical two-dimensional brain slice from a functional magnetic resonance imaging

(fMRI) image has at around 3000 volume elements (or voxels), and preprocessing steps produce a statistic map of t-statistics for testing the null hypothesis of no activity at each voxel. The goal is to determine which voxels are activated (show brain activity). Methods such as the false discovery rate (FDR) have been developed to provide a reasonable method of dealing with the multiple testing problem, although in this case the large number of hypotheses to be tested is still a concern. Shen et al. (2002) proposed an expected false discovery rate (EFDR) procedure in which the image is transformed slice-by-slice into the wavelet domain using the two-dimensional DWT, the unnecessary wavelet coefficients are removed, FDR is performed on the remaining (much smaller) set of wavelet coefficients to determine which are nonzero, and the image is recreated using the reduced set of coefficients. Modifications to the procedure were made by Shen et al. (2002), Sendur and Selesnick (2006), Sendur et al. (2007), and Pavlova et al. (2008). O'Hare (2010) examined the use of three-dimensional volume rather than using a slice-by-slice approach.

Another useful feature of the wavelet transformation is that it decorrelates data. That is, the wavelet coefficients, at finer levels, tend to be uncorrelated when the original data are correlated. For example, as mentioned in Chapter 11, Norwall (1993) has used the wavelet transform to decorrelate data with 1/f noise. See also Percival and Walden (2000), Section 9.1 of Flandrin (1992), and Tewfik and Kim (1992). A key application is in the analysis of two-dimensional or three-dimensional fMRI brain imaging data. The statistic maps discussed in the preceding paragraph often take the form of a signal buried in 1/f or otherwise correlated noise. Thus, in some situations it is useful to transform the wavelet domain to avoid the problem of dealing with the correlation structure in the original data. Bullmore et al. (2001) and Breakshear et al. (2004) make use of the decorrelating property to sample uncorrelated units by (a) transforming to the wavelet domain, (b) resampling the wavelet coefficients, and (c) creating the bootstrap data set by performing the inverse transform on the new set of wavelet coefficients. The goal of such resampling procedure is to estimate the null distribution of the original data while preserving its correlation structure. We also Tang and. the problems encountered with this “Wavestrap” procedure. Wiecke (1998) showed that the discrete wavelet packet transform can be used to decorrelate data from 1/(f-f₀ processes which are processes whose spectra have 1/(f-f₀) that, have infinite peaks at some frequency f₀, not necessarily zero.

Finally, we mention that the wavelet transform has been shown to be for estimating long-memory parameters. Specifically, McCoy and Walden (1996) and Jensen (1999a,b) have used it to estimate the parameter d in the FARMA model given in Equation 11.6. Additionally, Whittaker (2004) and Wiecke (1998) use the discrete wavelet packet transform to estimate the parameter λ in the GARMA model in Equation 11.16.

## Appendix 12A: Mathematical Preliminaries for This Chapter

The following mathematical review lists some definitions and results that are critical to understanding the material in Section 12.2 on wavelets.

### 12A.1 Classical Fourier Series

Consider the basis set $S_k = \{1, \cos kx, \sin kx, k=1, 2, \dots \}$. In 1807 Joseph Fourier made the astounding assertion, that to fairly loose restrictions, an arbitrary function $f(x)$ could be written as a linear combination of sines and cosines, that is, a linear combination of basis elements from $S_k$ (see James, 2011). Later, it was shown that a function $f(x)$ satisfying the Dirichlet conditions on a closed interval (i.e. $f(x)$ has a finite number of discontinuities and only a finite number of maxima and minima on the interval), can be expanded in a Fourier series which converges to $f(x)$ at points of continuity and to the mean of the left- and right-hand limits at points of discontinuity. Letting the domain of $f(x)$ be $[-\pi, \pi]$, we can write

$f(x) \sim \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (a_k \cos kx + b_k \sin kx), \quad (12A.1)$

$\sim$ denotes the aforementioned type of piecewise convergence under conditions such as the Dirichlet conditions. The coefficients in Equation 12A.1 are given by

$a_k = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(t) \cos kt \ dt, k=0, 1, \dots,$

$b_k = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(t) \sin kt \ dt, k=1, \dots,$ \quad (12A.2)

It is often convenient to express functions, $f$, defined on $[-\pi, \pi]$ as linear combinations of the basis functions in $S_2 = \{e^{ik}, k=0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots ,\}$ where in this case the Fourier expansion becomes

$f(x) \sim \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \alpha_k e^{ikx}, \quad (12A.3)$

where

$\alpha_k = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(t) e^{-ikt} \ dt. \quad (12A.4)$
