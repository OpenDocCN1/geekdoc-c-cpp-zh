
g(x) = \sum_{k=-\infty} s_{j,k}\phi_{j,k}(x) + \sum_{j=-\infty} \sum_{k=-\infty} d_{j,k}\psi_{j,k}(x), \quad (12.17)

where $\phi_{j,k}$ dilates and translates of a father wavelet or scaling function, $\varphi(x)$, corresponding to the mother wavelet, $\psi(x)$. Scaling functions (father wavelets) have the property

\int \varphi(x)dx = 1, \quad (12.18)

and their dilates and translates are defined analogously to Equation 12.9, that is,

\varphi_{j,k}(x) = 2^{-j/2}\varphi(2^{-j}x-k), \quad (12.19)

for integer $j$ and $k$. Consequently, it follows that for all integer $j$ and $k,

\int \varphi_{j,k}(x)dx = 1. \quad (12.20)

As an example, the Haar wavelet is

\varphi(x) = 1, \quad x \in[0, 1] = 0, otherwise.

The right-hand side of Figure 12.6 shows the father wavelets corresponding to each of the mother wavelets shown in the left-hand panel. Their behavior mimics the mother wavelets as $k$ gets larger, the father wavelet becomes smoother and the support widens.

It should be noted that wavelets (mother and father) usually have no analytic form. They are typically calculated using a dilation equation. See Daubechies (1992), Bruce and Gao (1996), and Strang and Nguyen (1997). Equation 12.17 is written as

g(x) = S_j(x) + \sum_{j=-\infty} D_j(x), \quad (12.21)

where $S_j(x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} s_{j,k}\varphi_j(x)$ and $D_j(x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} d_{j,k}\psi_j(x)$. The first term in Equation 12.21 gives a “smooth” approximation to $g(x)$ to level $j$ (i.e., involves the higher values of $j$). The $s_{jk}$ are called smooth coefficients. As we have previously discussed, the $d_{j,k}$ are the detail coefficients, and smaller values of $j$ refer to finer detail.

## 12.3.4 Discrete Wavelet Transform for Data Sets of Finite Length

For clarity of presentation, the discussion up to this point regarding wavelets has involved data on a continuum. However, for analyzing discrete data, $\mathbf{x}=(x_1, x_2,...,x_n)$, of finite length (such as a realization from a discrete time series), we will use the discrete wavelet transform (DWT). Given an orthogonal wavelet basis set, the DWT maps the vector $\mathbf{x}=(x_1, x_2,...,x_n)$ to a vector of $n$ wavelet coefficients. Letting $\mathbf{w}$ denote vector of wavelet coefficients, then it can be shown that

$$\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{Mx}, \quad (12.22)$$

where $\mathbf{M}$ is an orthogonal matrix that can be written as

$$\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{M}_1 \ \mathbf{M}_2 \ \vdots \ \mathbf{M}_{j_0} \ \mathbf{V}_{j_0} \end{pmatrix} \quad (12.23)$$

where

$$\mathbf{M}_1=\begin{pmatrix} \tilde{\Psi}_{1,1}(1) & \tilde{\Psi}_{1,1}(2) & \dots & \tilde{\Psi}_{1,1}(n) \ \tilde{\Psi}_{1,2}(1) & \tilde{\Psi}_{1,2}(2) & \dots & \tilde{\Psi}_{1,2}(n) \ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \ \tilde{\Psi}_{1,n/2}(1) & \tilde{\Psi}_{1,n/2}(2) & \dots & \tilde{\Psi}_{1,n/2}(n) \end{pmatrix}, \ \mathbf{M}_2=\begin{pmatrix} \tilde{\Psi}_{2,1}(1) & \tilde{\Psi}_{2,1}(2) & \dots & \tilde{\Psi}_{2,1}(n) \ \tilde{\Psi}_{2,2}(1) & \tilde{\Psi}_{2,2}(2) & \dots & \tilde{\Psi}_{2,2}(n) \ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \ \tilde{\Psi}_{2,n/2^2}(1) & \tilde{\Psi}_{2,n/2^2}(2) & \dots & \tilde{\Psi}_{2,n/2^2}(n) \end{pmatrix}, \ \mathbf{M}_{j_0}=\begin{pmatrix} \Phi_{j_0,1}(1) & \Phi_{j_0,1}(2)\cdot\dots & \Phi_{j_0,1}(n) \end{pmatrix}, \ \mathbf{V}_{j_0}=\begin{pmatrix} \Phi_{j_0,1}(1) & \Phi_{j_0,1}(2)\cdot\dots & \Phi_{j_0,1}(n) \end{pmatrix}$$

Since M is orthogonal when it also follows that

$$x = M'\mathbf{w}, (12.24)$$

where M' is the transpose of M. That is, knowing M allows us to transform from the data to the wavelet coefficients (the DWT), and M' allows us to perform an inverse discrete wavelet transform (IDWT), that is us to transform from the wavelet coefficients to the data.

NOTE: While the DWT can be applied to data sets whose length is not a power of 2, in the illustrative material, we will assume that n = 2^Jo for some J0.

For a discussion of discrete wavelets and scaling functions, see Percival and Walden (2000), Strang and Nguyen (1997), and Bruce and Cao (1996). It should be noted that the discrete version of a wavelet takes the general form of the continuous wavelet, but is not simply a discretized version of it. Most authors refer to the elements of M as wavelet and scaling filter coefficients. Since the wavelet and scaling filter coefficients are close approximations to the corresponding discretized versions of the continuous dilated and translated wavelets, we use the notation \psi_{j,k} and \phi_{j,k} (see Percival and Walden, 2000, Equations 469 and 477a).

Using the partitioning in 12.23 we can write

| w1 | M1 |
| w2 | M2 |
| : | : |
| w Jo | MJo |
| v Jo | VJo | = x_r, (12.25)

where

| w1 = (d_{1,1} d_{1,2} d_{1,n/2}), |
| w2 = (d_{2,1} d_{2,2} ... d_{2,n/2}), |
| w Jo = d_{Jo,1} v Jo = s_{Jo,1}. |

Consider Percival and Walden (2000) for a discussion of the issues involved in constructing the matrix M.

## 12.3.5 Pyramid Algorithm

While the wavelet coefficients can be computed from Equation 12.22, the pyramid algorithm introduced in the wavelet setting by Mallat (1989) provides an efficient method for calculating them. The pyramid algorithm is outlined in the following for a set of length $n=2^l:

**Step 1:** Beginning with the data values $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ (which is convenient to denote as $s_{0,1}, s_{0,2}, \dots, s_{0,n}$), 'down-sample' these into two sets, each containing $n/2$ values to obtain

- $n/2$ coefficients: $d_{1,k}, k=1, \dots, n/2$
- $n/2$ coefficients: $d_{1,k}, k=1, \dots, n/2$

**Step 2:** Retain $d_{1,k} k=1, \dots, n/2$ and down-sample $s_{1,k} k=1, \dots, n/2$ to obtain

- $n/4$ coefficients: $s_{2,k}, k=1, \dots, n/4$
- $n/4$ coefficients: $d_{2,k}, k=1, \dots, n/4$

**Step 3:** Retain $d_{2,k} k=1, \dots, n/4$, and down-sample $s_{2,k} k=1, \dots, n/4$ to obtain

- $n/8$ coefficients: $s_{3,k} k=1, \dots, n/8$
- $n/8$ coefficients: $d_{3,k} k=1, \dots, n/8$
