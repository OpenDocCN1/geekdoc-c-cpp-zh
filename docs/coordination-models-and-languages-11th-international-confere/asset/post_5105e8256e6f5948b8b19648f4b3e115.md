In order to obtain the stress for phases I and II, integral calculus of Equation (4) is expressed as

$$
\tau_{zx}^I = \frac{\Delta P}{L} x + C_1^I \\
\tau_{zx}^{II} = \frac{\Delta P}{L} x + C_1^{II}
\tag{6}
$$

where \(C_1\) is constant and superscripts I and II mean phases I and II. Here, continuity of shear stress is assumed as a boundary condition. Namely,

$$
\tau_{zx}^I = \tau_{zx}^{II} \text{ at } x = 0
\tag{7}
$$

By substituting Equation (6) with Equation (7), the following relationship is obtained.

$$
C_1^I = C_1^{II} = C_1
\tag{8}
$$

Here, Newton's law of viscosity is used,

$$
\tau_{zx} = \mu \frac{dv_z}{dx}
\tag{9}
$$

where \(v_z\) is flow velocity in the z-direction. By using Equations (6)–(9), the velocities of phases I and II, \(v^I\) and \(v^{II}\), are expressed as

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_z^I &= \frac{\Delta P}{2\mu^I L}x^2 + \frac{C_1}{\mu^I}x + C_2^I \\
v_z^{II} &= \frac{\Delta P}{2\mu^{II} L}x^2 + \frac{C_1}{\mu^{II}}x + C_2^{II}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}

Multiphase microflows are dominated by pressures (Aota et al., 2007a, 2009a). One important parameter needed to describe the multiphase microflows is the pressure that drives the fluids. The pressure decreases in the downstream part of the flow because of the fluids’ viscosity. When two fluids in contact with one another have different viscosities, the pressure difference (\(\Delta P_{\mathrm{flow}}\)) between the two phases is a function of the contact length and the flow velocity. Another important parameter is the Laplace pressure (\(\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}\)) caused by the interfacial tension between two phases. The position of the interface is fixed within a point in the microchannel by the balance established between the \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}\) and \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{flow}}\).

Figure 13a illustrates the pressure balance at the liquid–liquid interface of the two-phase microflows. The liquid–liquid interface curves toward the organic phase on a glass surface because of the hydrophilicity of the glass. \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}\) is generated at the curved liquid–liquid interface.

On the basis of the Young–Laplace equation, \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}\) is estimated as follows:

$$
\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}=\gamma\left(\frac{1}{R_1}+\frac{1}{R_2}\right) \tag{22}
$$

where \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) are the curvature radii of the liquid–liquid interface in direction vertical and parallel to the liquid stream. For parallel multiphase microflows, the equation is simplified as follows.

$$
\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}=\gamma\left(\frac{1}{R_1}\right)=\frac{2\gamma\sin(\theta-90^\circ)}{d} \tag{23}
$$

where \(\theta\) and \(d\) are the contact angle and depth of the microchannels. The contact angle is restricted to the values between the advancing contact angle of the aqueous phase \(\theta_{\mathrm{aq}}\) and that of the organic phase \(\theta_{\mathrm{org}}\). Therefore, \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}\) is restricted as follows:

$$
\frac{2\gamma\sin(\theta_{\mathrm{aq}}-90^\circ)}{d}<\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}<\frac{2\gamma\sin(\theta_{\mathrm{org}}-90^\circ)}{d} \tag{24}
$$

When \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{flow}}\) exceeds the maximum \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}\), the organic phase flows toward the aqueous phase (Figure 13b). When \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{flow}}\) is lower than the minimum \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{Laplace}}\), the aqueous phase flows toward the organic phase (Figure 13c). When the flow rate ratio is changed, the pressure balance is maintained by changing the position of the liquid–liquid interface. This model indicates that the important parameters for microfluidic control are the interfacial tension, the dynamic contact angle, and the depth of the microchannel. This model can also be applied to gas–liquid microflows.

Considering the interfacial pressure model, the flow rates should be between the higher and lower limits. \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{flow}}\) can be evaluated by considering the pressure loss in the microchannels. Assuming that the pressure at the opened outlet is atmospheric pressure, \(P_{\mathrm{atm}}\), then the pressure \(P\) of each phase from the pressure loss \(\Delta P\) is expressed as follows:

$$
P=P_{\mathrm{atm}}+\Delta P_{\mathrm{tube}}+\Delta P_{\mathrm{channel}}
$$
$$
=P_{\mathrm{atm}}+\frac{2f\rho v_{\mathrm{tube}}^2L_{\mathrm{tube}}}{d_{h,\mathrm{tube}}}+\frac{2f\rho v_{\mathrm{channel}}^2L_{\mathrm{channel}}}{d_{h,\mathrm{channel}}} \tag{25}
$$

where \(f\), \(\rho\), \(v\), and \(L\) are the friction factor, the density, the mean velocity of the fluid, and the length of the tube and channel correspond to the parts of the outlet tube and the microchannel. When the gas–liquid and liquid–liquid microflows are considered to be laminar flow, \(f\) is expressed as follows:

$$
f=\frac{16}{\mathrm{Re}}=\frac{16\mu}{\rho v d_h} \tag{26}
$$

Therefore, \(\Delta P_{\mathrm{flow}}\) can be expressed as follows:

$$
\Delta P_{\mathrm{flow}} = P_{\mathrm{org}} - P_{\mathrm{aq}}
= \frac{32\mu_{\mathrm{org}}V_{\mathrm{channel,org}}L_{\mathrm{channel,org}}}{d_{h,\mathrm{channel,org}}^{2}} + \frac{32\mu_{\mathrm{org}}V_{\mathrm{tube,org}}L_{\mathrm{tube,org}}}{d_{h,\mathrm{tube,org}}^{2}}
- \frac{32\mu_{\mathrm{aq}}V_{\mathrm{channel,aq}}L_{\mathrm{channel,aq}}}{d_{h,\mathrm{channel,aq}}^{2}} - \frac{32\mu_{\mathrm{aq}}V_{\mathrm{tube,aq}}L_{\mathrm{tube,aq}}}{d_{h,\mathrm{tube,aq}}^{2}} \tag{27}
$$

### 3.2 Methods of stabilization of multiphase microflows

Disturbances in the flow rates of pumps can destabilize multiphase microflows. However, the control method utilizing the microchannel structure and surface energy is effective for maintaining robust control over microfluidics. Researchers have suggested that multiphase microflows can be stabilized by altering the structure or adding features to the microchannels, specifically by including a guide structure (Fokeshi et al., 2002) or a pillar structure (Maruyama et al., 2004) along the microchannel, which permit a wider range of contact angle for the fluid interface than a flat surface. Researchers can design various microfluidic chemical processes using liquid-liquid microflows.

Other groups have proposed selective chemical surface modification for stabilization of the multiphase microflows (Aota et al., 2007a, 2007b; Hibara et al., 2002, 2003, 2005, 2008; van der Linden et al., 2006; Zhao et al., 2001, 2002a, 2002b, 2003). Figure 16 illustrates the shape of the liquid-liquid interface in a microchannel with chemically patterned surfaces. The contact angle of water on a hydrophobic surface can be larger than 90°. Therefore, multiphase microflows in microchannels with patterned surfaces can form under a wider range of conditions compared to microchannels with a guide structure or pillar structure.
