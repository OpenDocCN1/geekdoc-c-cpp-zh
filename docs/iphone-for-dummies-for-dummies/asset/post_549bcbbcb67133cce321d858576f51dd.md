
## Taking Your Best Shot

As with many apps on the iPhone, you find the Camera app icon on the Home screen. Unless you moved things around, the Camera app is positioned on the upper row of icons, all the way to the right and adjacent to its next of kin, the Photos icon. We tap both icons throughout this chapter.

Might as well snap an image now:

1. Fire up the camera itself. Choose one of the following:
- On the Home screen, tap the Camera app icon. Or from the Lock screen, swipe from right to left.
- Drag Control Center from the bottom of the screen and tap the Camera icon in here.
- On the Lock screen, drag the screen from right to left.
2. Keep your eyes peeled on the iPhone display. 
You are taken to the viewfinder window. We get to the controls that frame that window shortly.
3. Aim the camera at whatever you want to shoot.
Use the iPhone's display as your viewfinder. We marvel at the iPhone's display throughout this book; the Camera app gives us another reason to do.
You have several shooting formats. You move from one format to another by swiping right or left (in portrait mode) or swiping up or down (in landscape mode). Your choices are Video (kindly read the next chapter for more), Photo (your basic snapshot), Square (for a picture formatted to make nice with Instagram photo-sharing app), Pano (short for panorama), Time-Lapse (accelerated sequence), and Slo-Mo mode. You find out about them in the next chapter.
4. When you've chosen a shooting format and are satisfied with what's in the frame, do one of the following to snap the picture:
- Tap the round shutter button. The button is at the bottom center of the screen in portrait mode (see Figure 9-1) or on the right or the left in landscape mode. As we show you in a moment, you'll be able to change the point of focus if necessary.
- Press the physical volume up or volume down button. These buttons are on the side of the phone (portrait mode) or at the top of the device (landscape mode). We generally consider this method a better way of shooting because it mimics the experience of capturing photos with a regular camera, at least when you're holding the phone sideways.
- Use buttons on the EarPods that come with your phone. If you're an iPhone traditionalist, however, don't let stop you from continuing to use the on-screen shutter release method.

That's: You've snapped your first iPhone picture.

5. Repeat Steps 3 and 4 to capture additional images.

## Making Photos Come to Life

One of the coolest features added to the 6s and 6s Plus (and beyond) is called Live Photos. Essentially, you snap a still image as always, a snippet of video (and sound) is captured about a second and a half before you tap the shutter. Think of a live photo as an animated GIF.

Models with live photos, you'll see a glyph when you fire up the camera (see Figure 9-1). If the glyph is yellow, the Live Photos feature is enabled, as it is by default. Otherwise, the glyph will be white. You can tap the glyph to toggle the feature on or off. You see a live indicator in a yellow box when the iPhone is capturing a scene.

You can view your live photo on your iPhone or on other devices by leaving your finger pressed down on the screen. You can also use a live photo as your Lock screen iPhone wallpaper.

According to Apple, a live photo claims roughly the same amount of space as two photos with the live feature turned off do. That means storage shouldn't become a major issue for most iPhone owners who snap a decent amount of photos, even you with the smallest capacity. Of course, you'll find out in this chapter, photos need not stay on your phone; you can store images on the cloud via iCloud.

> TIP We recommend that you think of live photos as videos. The 12-megapixel still image is at the heart of what you're trying to capture. That said, you may want to think about keeping the camera steady for a second or so longer after you snap the photo.

## Keeping Things in Focus

The camera in the iPhone can detect up to faces in a scene, placing a rectangle on top of each face (the focus box in Figure 9-1). Behind the scenes, the camera is balancing the exposure across each face. If you want to take a picture, press and hold your finger until the rectangle pulses. AE/AF Lock will appear on the screen. Tap the screen again to make AE/AF Lock disappear.

If you have the 6 Plus, 6s, 7, or 7 Plus, your photography skills get a boost from optical stabilization, which uses complex algorithms to help you compensate for the shakes. The 6 Plus works with the A8 chip, gyroscope, and M8 motion processor inside the phone. The 6s and the SE beef up with A9 chip and M9 motion processor. The iPhone 6, 6s, SE employ a somewhat less effective digital stabilization method to compensate for jittery shooting.

When a sun is visible, you can drag your finger up or down the screen to increase or decrease the brightness in a scene. You can change the exposure settings of a given shot up to four f-stops. You can lighten or darken scenes for both stills and video. All iPhone 6 models and later include focus pixels. This is under the hood to help the cameras focus faster and better. The 6s, SE, and 7 Plus raised the megapixel count on the rear iSight camera to 12 (from 8mp on prior models) and have engineered pixels, Apple say, to produce even sharper images. The 7 Plus gets a second 12-megapixel camera, which we address further in this chapter. The front cameras on the 6s and 6 Plus now boast a 5mp sensor. The SE is still at 2mp. The FaceTime cameras on the 7 and 7 Plus jump to 7mp.

You can also exploit a feature known as HDR, or high dynamic range photography. Tap the HDR button (labeled in Figure 9-1) to turn on HDR. The HDR feature takes separate exposures (long, normal, short) and blends the best parts of the three shots into a single image. In Settings (under Photos & Camera), you can choose to keep the “normal” photo along with your HDR result or just onto the latter.

On iPhone 6, SE, and 7 models, you can take advantage of HDR on the front-facing camera as well. This, too, can be applied to both stills and videos.

The cameras on the 7 and 7 Plus bring other goodies. With an f/1.8 aperture and six-element lens, they perform especially well in low light. And the image processor in them—think camera brain—can power more than 100 billion operations. That's what Apple tells us anyway, but don't ask us to list them.

While you're in the Photos & Camera settings, note that you can also turn on grid lines, which help you frame a shot using the photographic principle known as the Rule of Thirds.

## From the front to the rear — and back

We figure that most of the time, you'll use the main rear camera while shooting pictures (or). But you may want to capture a selfie, or a shot of your pretty face, to post on a social network such as Facebook. Not problem; just tap the front/rear camera switch at the bottom-right corner of the screen (labeled in Figure 9-1) to toggle between the front and rear cameras.

Apple calls the front camera because you can use it for the FaceTime video-calling feature discussed in Chapter 4. The rear camera is called the iSight camera.

## Firing the flash

The iPhone has an LED (light-emitting diode) flash that controls pictures taking with the rear camera. Because no flash is associated with the front-facing camera on the 6 or 6 Plus, you won't see the flash button (labeled in Figure 9-1) when you're using that. As we get to a moment, you will see a flash icon when you're using the front camera on the 6s and 6 Plus, SE, and 7 and 7 Plus. When the button is available, tap it to change the setting to On, Off, or Auto. We suggest using the Auto setting, which lets the iPhone decide when it's a good idea to fire up the flash.

The iPhone has not one but two rear flashes as a flashy flash Apple refers to as True Tone flash. The flashes—one white, one amber—work in tandem to match the flash to the ambient lighting in your shooting environment. The system determines the intensity and quality of the two flashes to fire off automatically, with more than 1,000 possible combinations, Apple says.

Apple claims the Quad-LED True Tone flash on the 7 and Plus is 50 percent brighter than the 6 models.
