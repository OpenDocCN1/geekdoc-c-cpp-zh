If you have the 6 Plus, 6s, 7, or 7 Plus, your photography skills get a boost from optical image stabilization, which uses complex algorithms to help you compensate for a case of the shakes. The 6 Plus works with the A8 chip, gyroscope, and M8 motion coprocessor inside the phone to improve the results of your pictures in dimmer light

The 6s and the SE beef up the specs with an A9 chip and M9 motion coprocessor.

The iPhone 6, 6s, SE employ a somewhat less effective *digital image stabilization* method to compensate for a jittery shooter.

The 7 and 7 Plus have an A10 Fusion chip from Apple.

When a sun is visible, you can drag your finger up or down the screen to increase or decrease the brightness in a scene. You can change the exposure settings of a given shot by up to four f-stops. You can lighten or darken scenes for both stills and video.

All iPhone 6 models and later include a *focus pixels* sensor. This is it of a under-the-hood tool to help the cameras focus faster and better.

The 6s, SE, and 7 Plus raised the megapixel count on the rear iSight camera to 12 (from 8mp on prior models) and have engineered pixels, Apple say, to produce even sharper images. The 7 Plus gets a second 12-megapixel camera, which we address further later in this chapter.

The front FaceTime cameras on the 6s and 6 Plus now boast a 5mp sensor, from 1.2mp. The SE is still also at 1.2mp. The *FaceTime* cameras on the 7 and 7 Plus jump to 7mp.

You can also exploit a feature known as HDR, or *high dynamic range*, photography. Tap the HDR button (labeled in Figure 9-1) to turn on HDR. The HDR feature takes separate exposures (long, normal, short) and blends the best parts of the three shots into a single image. In Settings (under Photos & Camera), you can choose to keep the “normal” photo along with your HDR result or just hang onto the latter.

On iPhone 6, SE, and 7 models, you can take advantage of HDR on the front-facing FaceTime HD camera as well. This, too, can be applied to both stills and videos.
