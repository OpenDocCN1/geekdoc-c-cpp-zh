# Chapter 11
Going On a Mobile Safari

“The Internet in your pocket.”

That's what Apple promised the iPhone would bring to the public when the product was announced in January 2007. In the years since, Apple has come tantalizing close to delivering on that pledge.

For years, the cellphone industry offered a watered-down version of the Internet, but the approaches typically fell far short of what people had come to experience while sitting in front of a computer.

Apple was a pioneer in replicating the real-deal Internet on a phone. Web pages on an iPhone look like web pages on a Windows PC or Mac, right down to swanky graphics and pictures — and at least some video and web-based games.

In this chapter, you find out how to navigate through cyberspace on your iPhone.

# Surfin' Dude

The version of the Apple Safari web browser is a major reason that the Net on the iPhone is very much like the Net you've come to expect on a computer. Safari for the Mac (and for Windows) is one of the best web browsers in the computer business. And in our view, Safari is one of the very best cellphone browsers, especially with its edge-to-edge full-screen design.

# Blasting off into cyberspace

We tell you how great web pages look on the iPhone, but so we're eager to get going. We won't hold you back much longer.

Apple brought the previously separate address bar and search fields together into a single convenient unified strip called the *smart search field*, following the path taken on most popular web browsers for PCs and Macs. When you unify the smart search field (as you will in a moment), the virtual keyboard appears. But you'll also see icons for sites you frequent most often, and you can tap any of those icons to jump immediately to that site.

Otherwise, the moment you tap a single letter, you see a list of web addresses that match those letters. For example, if you tap the letter s (as we did in the example in Figure 11-2), you see listings for Sites Illustrated (s.i.com), Staples, and Sears, among others. Tapping U or H instead may show listings for USA TODAY or the Houston Chronicle (shameless plugs for the media properties where Ed and Bob are columns). Scroll to see more suggestions and the virtual keyboard slides out, as shown in Figure 11–2.

The iPhone has two ways to determine websites when you tap certain letters. One method is the websites you already bookmarked in the Safari or Microsoft Edge browser on your computer (and synchronized, as described in Chapter 3). See on more bookmarks later in this chapter.

The second method iPhone uses when suggesting websites when you tap a particular letter is to suggest sites from the History list—those cyberdestinations where you hung your hat. Because history repeats itself, we also tackle that topic in this chapter. In Figure 11–2, results from Bookmarks and History are lumped together.

You might as well open your first web page now. It's a full HTML page, to borrow from techie lingo. Do the following:

1. Tap the Safari icon at the bottom of the Home screen. This icon is another member of the Fantastic Four (along with Phone, Mail, and Music).

2. Tap the smart search field (labeled in Figure 11-1). If you can't see the smart search field, tap the status bar or scroll to the top of the screen.

3. Begin typing your web address on the virtual keyboard that slides up from the bottom of the screen. The web address is also called the URL (Uniform Resource Locator, for trivia buffs).

4. Do one of the following:

- To accept one of the bookmarked (or other) sites that show up on the list, merely tap the name. Safari automatically fills in the URL in the address field and takes you where you want to go.
- Keep tapping the keyboard characters until you enter the complete web address for the site you have in mind, and then tap Go in the lower-right corner of the keyboard.

It's not necessary to type www. at the beginning of a URL. So, if you want to visit www.theonion.com, for example, typing theonion.com or, in this case, just onion.com or onion is sufficient to transport you to the humor site or at least a link that puts a single tap from the site.

# I Can See Clearly Now

If you know how to open a web page (if you don't) these neat tricks:

- Double-tap the screen so that the portion of the text you want to read fills the entire screen: it takes just a second before the screen comes into focus. By way of example, check out Figure 11-3. It shows two views of the same TODAY web page. In the first view, you see what the page looks like when you open it. In the second view, you see how the picture takes over much of the screen after you double-tap. To return to the first view, double-tap the screen again.
- Pinch the page: Sliding your thumb and index finger together and spreading them apart (or, we like to say, unpinching)) zooms out and in of a page. Again, wait a moment for the screen to come into focus.
- Press on a page and drag it in all directions, or click through a page from top to bottom: You're panning and scrolling, baby.
- Rotate the iPhone to its side: What happens to the White House website, in 11-4. It reorients from portrait to a widescreen view. The keyboard is also wider, making it a little easier to enter a new URL.

# Opening multiple web pages at a time

When we surf the web on a desktop PC or laptop, we rarely go to a single web page and call it day. In fact, we often have multiple web pages open the same time. Sometimes, several pages are open because we choose to hop around the web without closing the pages we visit. Other times, a link (see the next section) auto-opens a new page without shuttering the old. (If these additional pages are advertisements, they aren't always welcome.)
