# Locking the iPhone

Carrying a naked cellphone in your pocket is asking for trouble. Unless the phone has some locking mechanism, you may inadvertently dial a phone number. Try explaining to your boss why he or she got a call from you at 4 a.m. Fortunately, Apple makes it a cinch to lock the iPhone so this pocket-dialing scenario won't happen to you.

In fact, you don't need to do anything to lock the iPhone; it happens automatically, as long as you don't touch the screen for one minute. (You can change this duration in iPhone Settings, a topic in Chapter 14.)

## CHAPTER 2 iPhone Basic Training

# TRAINING YOUR DIGITS

We've already told you how to train your digits to use Touch ID. Well, your fingers play a prominent role throughout your iPhone experience, tapping, flicking, pinching, and dragging. Fortunately, these gestures are not challenging, so you'll be mastering many of the iPhone's features in no time:

- **Tapping:** Tap serves multiple purposes, as becomes evident throughout this book. You can tap an icon to open an app from the Home screen. Tap to start playing a song or choose the photo album you want to look through. Sometimes you'll double-tap (tapping twice in rapid succession), which has the effect of zooming in (or out) of web pages, maps, and emails.
- **Flick:** A flick of the finger on the screen lets you quickly scroll through lists of songs, emails, and thumbnails. Tap the screen to stop scrolling, or merely wait for the list to stop.
- **Pinch/spread:** On a web page, pinch your fingers to shrink the image, or spread apart to enlarge it. Pinching and spreading (what we call unpinching) are cool gestures that are easy to master and sure to wow unfamiliar audience.
- **Drag:** Slowly press your finger against the touchscreen and, without lifting your finger, you might drag a map that is too large for the iPhone's display.

Reachability helps owners of the larger-display iPhones. By double-tapping (but not double-pressing) the Home button, the top portion of the screen shifts toward the bottom of the display so that you can tap icons and items that were previously out of reach.

To lock the iPhone immediately, press and hold the sleep/wake button. To unlock it, you can press the sleep/wake button again. Either way, press the Home button to open your phone. If you set up the iPhone to use Touch ID, you can merely press your finger against the Home button to unlock. Prior to iOS 10, Slide to Unlock appeared near the bottom of the screen and to unlock, you drag your finger to the right and then, in some cases, also enter a passcode, another topic reserved for Chapter 14. The iOS 10 upgrade brings another capability to the most recent iPhones: the capability to wake up the screen by raising the phone. The Raise-to-wake feature, which you can turn on or off inside Settings, requires an iPhone with an A9 processor or later, which as of this writing means the iPhone 6, 6s, SE, 7, or 7 Plus have an embedded A10 Fusion chip.

# Understanding Pressure-Sensitive

Speaking of feel, the arrival of the iPhone 6s and 6s Plus years ago introduced owners to pressure-sensitive technology known as 3D Touch. Another way, the amount of pressure you apply when touching the display changes the outcome of what happens on the screen. For example, if you press against an email in inbox, you can peek at the message. Press harder and you have peek and pop. On the Home screen, you can use 3D Touch to employ shortcuts, including selfie, marking a location in Maps, and searching for what's nearby. Apple calls these shortcuts quick actions. As mentioned, you can also use 3D Touch to interact with notifications inside Notification Center. As you discover later, you can use 3D Touch in apps such as Mail or Notes to transform your on-screen keyboard into a trackpad.

Force-touch technology is baked into iOS 10 on compatible iPhones, so you'll find yourself using it in many ways. And though there's a bit of learning curve before you get the hang of it, we think you'll get comfortable soon enough. Apple tries to help by giving you tactile feedback along the way.

# Mastering the Multitouch Interface

The iPhone, like smartphones nowadays, dispenses with physical buttons in favor of a multitouch display. (The iPhone was a pioneer in popularizing multitouch.) This display is the heart of many things you do on the iPhone, and the controls depend on the task at hand.

Unlike other phones with touchscreens don't bother looking for a stylus. You are instead at the risk of another ancient slogan "let your fingers do the walking."

It's important to note that you have at disposal keyboard layouts in English, all variations on the alphabetical keyboard, the numeric and punctuation keyboard, and the more punctuation and symbols keyboard. Three keyboards are shown in Figure 2-1 in the Notes app and in Safari.
