## Setting You Straight on Settings

Are you a control freak? Do you have to have it your way? Boy, you have landed in the right chapter.

Throughout this book, you have occasion to drop in on Settings, which is the makeover factory for the iPhone. For example, we show you how to open Settings (by tapping its Home icon) to set ringtones and text tones, change the phone's background or wallpaper, and specify your search engine of choice. We also show you how to alter settings in Safari, tailor email to your liking, and get a handle on how to fetch or push new data.

The Settings area on the iPhone is roughly analogous to Control Panel in Windows and System Preferences on a Mac.

But you won't have to visit Settings in every case because Control Center grants you immediate access to some of the settings and controls that used to require a separate visit to Settings, including airplane mode and display brightness, which we address in this chapter. But even with Control Center, we expect to run into you in Settings time to time. Because we cover some settings elsewhere, do not dwell on every setting. But you can still discover plenty to help you make the iPhone your own.

## Airplane mode

First, even though you're reading about airplane mode, we're obliged to tell you that you can turn this setting on or off also in Control Center. Because you're here-anyway, come, fly with us.

> Figure 14-1: Presenting lists of settings.

Using a cellphone to make calls on an airplane is a no-no. But nothing's verbose about using an iPhone to listen to music, watch videos, and peek at pictures—and the rules that make you want to do so until the craft has reached altitude have been relaxed. So how to take advantage of the iPhone's built-in Music player (or other capabilities) while temporarily turning it off, email, and Internet functions? The answer is, by turning airplane mode. To do, merely tap airplane mode on the Settings screen to turn the switch (which is showing green). That act disables the iPhone's wireless radios (Wi-Fi, EDGE, 3G, 4G, LTE) and Bluetooth. While your iPhone is in airplane mode, you can't make receive calls, surf the web, or do anything that requires an Internet connection. The good news is that airplane mode makes your battery running longer—particularly useful if you are flying half-around the world.

The appearance of a tiny airplane icon on the status bar in the top-left corner reminds you that airplane mode is turned on. Just remember to turn it off when you're back on the ground.

Since many flights now offer Wi-Fi, you can go into airplane mode and separately turn on Wi-Fi, using the method addressed in the next section.

## Wi-Fi

As we mention in Chapter 11, Wi-Fi is typically the fastest wireless network you can use to surf the web, send email, and perform other Internet tricks on your iPhone, including (in some cases) making calls. You use the Wi-Fi setting to determine which Wi-Fi networks are available to you and which one to exploit based on its signal.

Tap Wi-Fi, and you see any Wi-Fi networks in range, as shown in Figure 14–2.

A signal-strength indicator can help you choose the network to connect to if more than one is listed; tap the appropriate Wi-Fi network when you reach a decision. If a network is password-protected, you see a lock icon.

You can turn on or off the Ask to Join Networks working. Networks that the iPhone is already familiar with joined automatically, regardless of which one you use. If the Ask feature is on, you're asked joining a new network. If it's off, you have to select a new network manually.

If you used a particular network automatically in the past but no longer want your iPhone to join it, tap the i-in-circle to select (in Wi-Fi settings), and then tap Forget Network. The iPhone developer provides a quick case of selective amnesia. If you haven't selected a given Wi-Fi network, you'll see the option to Join Network instead of Forget This Network.

Sometimes, you may want to connect to a network that's closed and not shown on the Wi-Fi list. If that's the case, tap Other and use the keyboard to enter the network name. Then to choose the type of security the network is using (if any). Your choices are WEP, WPA, WPA2, WPA Enterprise, and WPA2 Enterprise. If no Wi-Fi network is available, you have to rely on a cellular network. If a cellular network isn't available either, you can't rocket into cyberspace unless you regain access to a network.

You can enable Wi-Fi even in airplane mode. Turn on airplane mode, tap the Wi-Fi switch to turn Wi-Fi back on, and select your network.

## Bluetooth

You can use Bluetooth, a short-range wireless technology, to communicate wirelessly with a compatible Bluetooth headset or hands-free car kit — not to mention the Apple Watch.

Bluetooth also plays a role in Apple Pay, AirDrop, CarPlay, and ibeacons, Apple's technology for responding to location information and services transmitted by beacons out in the real world.

Meanwhile, headsets and car kits have become more of a big deal as a number of states and municipalities around the United States make it illegal to hold a phone to your mouth or ear while you're driving. To ensure that the iPhone works with one of these devices, it has to be wirelessly paired, or coupled, with the device device

If you're using a third-party accessory, follow the instructions that come with that headset or car kit so that it becomes discoverable, or ready to be paired with your iPhone. Then turn Bluetooth in Settings so the iPhone can find nearby devices and the device can find the iPhone. You can also turn Bluetooth on Control Center. The technology works up to a range of 30 feet.

You know Bluetooth is turned on when you see the Bluetooth icon on the status bar. If the symbol is black or white, the iPhone is communicating wirelessly with a connected device (The color differences provide contrast to whatever is behind the Icon). If it's gray, Bluetooth is turned on on the iPhone but a paired device isn't nearby or isn't turned on.
