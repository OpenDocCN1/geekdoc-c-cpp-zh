In that section, we write a naive C++ program for multiplying matrices using general classes for vectors and matrices and show that it is more than an order of magnitude slower. The culprit here is the naivity of the programmer and not the C++ language. A C++ program written with an idea of how compilers work, and the kind of instruction they generate, will be faster than a Fortran program written without that knowledge. The C/C++ languages provide every available means for writing optimized programs, and there is no programming language or paradigm, including Fortran, that is remotely comparable to them in this respect.

The skill of writing loops explained in section 3.2 is about 40% of the typical scientific programmer needs to know to produce well-optimized programs. Another 40% is about optimizing memory accesses in a single-core program, a closely related topic discussed in the next chapter. Many subtle concepts and techniques come up when we study threads and networks. However, in optimizing scientific programs, much of the game is in structuring loops carefully, as explained in this chapter, and in organizing accesses, as explained in the next chapter. For example, when we optimize a program to effect a matrix transpose in chapter 6, of the optimization boils down to structuring loops and organizing accesses to memory, even though the program makes sophisticated use of networking and threading.

The brief summary, in the preceding paragraph, of the relative weight of various techniques of optimization is for scientific programs, which generally tend to work with amounts of data that are laid out in regular patterns. In computer science, data tends to be dynamic and highly unpredictable in extent. Dynamic data structures such as linked lists, trees, and graphs are commonly employed. When such data structures are used, the relative weight of optimization techniques changes considerably. Optimizing for memory, which is the topic of the next chapter, is considerably more important. However, more and more scientific programs are taking on qualities of systems programming, and, and versa, conversely, some newer areas of computer science such as data analysis and image processing give rise to programs that are similar to traditional scientific programs.
