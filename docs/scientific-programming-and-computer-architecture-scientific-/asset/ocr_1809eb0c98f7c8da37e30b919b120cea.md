# 3.3. Optimizing the instruction pipeline

This section is a prelude to the discussion of matrix multiplication, which begins in the next section. We understand matrix multiplication on modern processors in two steps. In the next section, we take the first step by writing programs or microkernels²²³ to multiply $4 \times n$ matrices with an $n \times 4$ matrices for $n$ such as $n=1, 4, 200$. The microkernels with $n=50, 100, 200$ approach peak performance if the matrices are assumed to be in. The final stage, which is to use a microkernel as the building block of a program for multiplying large matrices in DRAM memory, is one of the examples discussed in the next chapter.

Understanding matrix multiplication on modern processors will take us more deeply into computer hardware than is customary in textbooks on scientific computing or even computer architecture. The programs run on a 2.6 GHz SSE processor and a 3.6 GHz AVX2 processor (see table A.1 for their full names; neither processor has in-core acceleration of the clock). Although the programs are not optimized for the AVX2 pipeline, they are still more three times faster than compiled code.

Optimization for the most recent AVX2 instruction set, which is yet to be introduced in the main x86 line, is dealt with in chapter 7. An advantage of SSE is that our programs run on almost all x86 computers. Because the SSE2 instructions are simpler than AVX2 instructions, Most of the points that we make about latencies and throughputs of instructions, such as `addpd` and `mulpd`, remain valid on AVX and AVX2 machines, sometimes with minor modifications. It is a little surprising that the SSE2 programs we discuss have not become dated with the advent of AVX and AVX2. Of course, these programs no longer approach performance. Yet the concepts they bring out related to instruction-level parallelism remain valid in the context of the specific programs found in our discussion, and the programs will continue to run.
