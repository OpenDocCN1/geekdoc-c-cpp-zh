$\sigma_i$ and $\sigma_j$ are the standard deviations of $X_i$, and $X_j$, respectively. The correlation is defined only if $\sigma_i$ and $\sigma_j$ are finite and nonzero. As with covariance, $ho_{ij}$ is more often seen in the form of a correlation matrix$^{15}$ $ho$,:

$\rho = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho_{13} & \dots & \rho_{1n} \ \rho_{21} & 1 & \rho_{23} & \dots \ \rho_{31} & \rho_{32} & 1 & \dots \ \dots & \dots & \dots & \dots \ \rho_{n1} & \dots & \dots & 1 \end{pmatrix} (4.20)$

Clearly, correlation inherits the symmetric property of covariance i.e. $ho_{ij} = \rho_{ji}$ and $ho_{ii} = 1.0$ for $i = j$. Since correlation measures the relative strength of variability between two sets of random numbers, it is a very useful tool for determining the degree of diversification in a portfolio of hedge funds i.e. a FoHF. Moreover, the correlation matrix representing a portfolio of hedge funds should ideally have low values indicating a well-diversified portfolio of funds. Source 4.9 shows the Corr() member function and method implementation for the FoHF monthly returns in `10_hedge_funds.dat`. The correlation values range between -0.303 and 0.266 with the majority close to zero indicating a small degree of positive and negative variability between these sets of hedge funds. Obviously, the correlation of a hedge fund with itself is one.
