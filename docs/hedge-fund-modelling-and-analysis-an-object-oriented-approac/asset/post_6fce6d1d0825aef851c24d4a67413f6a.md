The Monte-Carlo (MC) method assumes that a series of hedge fund returns can be characterised by a stochastic (or probabilistic) model. MC methods are widely used of classical algorithms for simulating the behaviour of various physical and mathematical systems having been popularised by John von Neumann and Nicholas Metropolis to name but two. MC methods are distinguished from other simulation-based methods by being of a stochastic nature, that the MC model includes a non-deterministic component that introduces a degree of uncertainty or randomness into the process through the use of random generators. Such a model is primarily driven by the mean, and $\sigma$ standard, of the hedge fund return distribution from historical data as well as the inclusion of a degree of subjective knowledge (i.e. market experience) into the model necessary. The stochastic model is the fundamental building block to MC simulations being used extensively throughout the financial markets. For this reason, the MC method is a very powerful and much better technique for estimating VaR within the hedge fund community. Not only is the method robust and probabilistically strong, it is also an excellent way of building in nonlinearity into the distribution and facilitating a better understanding of the characteristics of derivatives within the portfolio with greater confidence. However, the MC method can easily become mathematically challenging and computationally hungry when dealing with the inherent complexities of a particular hedge fund portfolio and strategy.

# 7.4 MODIFIED VaR

Despite the use of tests and scenario analysis, the most erroneous (potentially damaging) assumption using traditional VaR methods is that of hedge fund returns following a normal distribution. Clearly, this is invalid since it is well known that hedge fund returns generally have fatter tails and an asymmetric return distribution i.e. the presence of skewness and excess kurtosis. In order to address this issue many extensions to the traditional VaR methods have been put forth as better estimators of hedge fund market risk. Such methods explicitly incorporate skewness and kurtosis into the model or focus primarily on the left-tails of the distribution where most of the extreme returns (i.e. large losses) occur. One such extension is the modified VaR (MVaR). MVaR explicitly takes account the third and fourth moments of the return distribution, similarly skewness and kurtosis. That is, there is a branch of mathematics that is involved with power series expansions of quantile functions (see Box 7.1) as those related to VaR. Indeed, the higher moments of the distribution are incorporated into the VaR measure using the celebrated Cornish-Fisher expansion (1937), so that the following power series can be obtained for the first few:

```
z_{cf} \approx z_{a} + \frac{1}{2}(z_{a}^2 - 1)s + \frac{1}{24}(z_{a}^3 - 3z_{a})k - \frac{1}{36}(2z_{a}^3 - 5z_{a})s^2 \quad (7.4)
```

where $z_{cf}$ is the Cornish-Fisher critical value from the normal distribution at the respective significance level, $\alpha$. Note that when the return distribution is normally distributed, $s$ and $k$ will both be zero and therefore:

```
z_{cf} \approx z_{\alpha} \quad (7.5)
```

The VaR at a confidence level 100 (1 - α) is given by:

> $MR_{1-\alpha} = \mu + \zeta_{\alpha}\sigma$  (7.6)

where $\mu$ is the mean, $s$ is the sample skew, $k$ is the sample excess kurtosis and $\zeta_{\alpha}$ is the critical value from the normal distribution at the required significance level, $\alpha$. It turns out that (7.6) leads to a more accurate measure of the VaR, however, there are limitations to this estimation. The higher the confidence level (e.g. 99%) the deeper we are in the left-tail of the distribution and this can lead to inaccurate results since the Cornish-Fisher expansion breaks down at these levels. VaR is also unreliable when the returns distribution is highly skewed and fat-tailed. Source 7.5 shows that the monthly $VaR_{\alpha}$ is $136,085.
