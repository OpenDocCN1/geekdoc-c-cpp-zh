## SOURCE 1.30: OVERLOADING THE (<<) OPERATOR

```
// Vec2D.h
#pragma once

#include <iostream>
using std::ostream;

class Vec2D
{
public:
    int m_x, m_y;

    Vec2D();
    Vec2D(int, int);

    // Overloaded operator declared constant and by reference
    Vec2D operator+(const Vec2D&) const;

    // Overload (<<) operator
    // ostream is not a member of the Vec2D class but wants to
    // consume its
    // member variables so we make it a friend of the Vec2D class.
    friend ostream& operator<<(ostream& stream, const Vec2D&);

    virtual ~Vec2D();
};
// Vec2D.cpp
#include "Vec2D.h"

#include <iostream>
using std::cout;

Vec2D::Vec2D() : m_x(0), m_y(0)
{
}

Vec2D::Vec2D(int x, int y) : m_x(x), m_y(y) {}
```

```
Vec2D Vec2D::operator+(const Vec2D& v) const

    // Uses an anonymous Vec2D object
    return Vec2D(m_x + v.m_x, m_y + v.m_y);

ostream& operator<<(ostream& stream, const Vec2D& v)
```

With the statement:

cout << "\n" << c << "\n";



the first << invokes the left shift operator and shifts c into cout, then it reads the next << and invokes the left shift operator again and shifts "\n" into cout i.e. it left -> right. In 1.30, since we are constantly modifying the ostream object (i.e. using multiple insertion operations) we must pass it by reference so that it takes into account this operator chaining process. Note also that ostream is not a member (i.e. it is a non-member) of the Vec2D class but would be able to access the private members of the class. In this class, we make ostream friend of the Vec2D class. By default, a non-member cannot access the private member data of a class unless the class explicitly states that function is allowed to do so, i.e. a class must give away its friendship, a friend cannot be simply assumed by a non-member function.

## The Hedge Fund Industry

The global credit crisis originated from a growing bubble in the US real estate market which eventually froze in 2008. This led to an overwhelming default of mortgages linked to subprime debt which financial institutions reacted by tightening credit facilities, selling off bad debts at huge losses and pursuing fast foreclosures on delinquent mortgages. A liquidity crisis followed in the credit markets and banks became increasingly reluctant to lend to another causing risk premiums on debt and credit to become scarcer and costly. The global financial markets went into meltdown as a continuing spiral of worsening liquidity ensued. When the market froze, hedge fund managers were unable to get their hands on enough capital to meet investor redemption requirements. Not until the early part of 2009 did the industry start to experience a resurgence in activity realising capital inflows and growing investor confidence.

The aftermath of the financial crisis has clearly highlighted many shortcomings of the hedge fund industry and heightened the debate over the need for increased regulation and monitoring. Nevertheless, it has since been widely accepted that hedge funds only a small part in the global financial collapse and suffered at the hands of a highly regulated banking system.

Chapter 2 introduces the concept of hedge funds and how they are structured and managed as a discussion of the current state of the global hedge fund industry in the aftermath of the last financial crisis. Several key investment techniques that are used in managing hedge fund strategies are also discussed. This chapter aims to build a basic working knowledge of hedge funds, and along with Chapter 3, develop the fundamentals necessary in order to approach and understand the more quantitative and theoretical aspects of modelling and analysis developed in later chapters.

While working for Fortune magazine in 1949, Alfred Winslow Jones began researching an article on various fashions in stock market forecasting and soon realised that it was possible to neutralise market risk¹ by buying undervalued securities and short selling² overvalued ones. Such an investment scheme was the first to employ a hedge to eliminate the potential for losses by cancelling out adverse market moves, and the technique of leverage³ to greatly improve profits. Jones generated an exceptional amount of wealth through his hedge fund over the 1950s and 1960s and continually outperformed traditional managers. Jones refused to refuse the hedge fund with the Securities Act of 1933, the Investment Advisors Act of 1940 or the Investment Company Act of 1940, with the main argument being that the fund was a private entity and one of the laws associated with the three Acts applied to this type of investment. It was essential that such funds were treated separately from other regulated markets since the use of specialised investment techniques, such as short selling and leverage were not permitted under these Acts; nor the ability to change performance fees to investors.

So that the funds maintained their private status, Jones would never advertise or market the funds but only investors through word of mouth keeping everything as possible. It was not until 1966, through the publication of a news article about Jones' exceptional profit-making ability, by Wall Street and High Net Worth (HNW) individuals finally caught on and within a couple of years there were over 200 active hedge funds straying from the original market neutral strategy used by Jones and employed other merely seeing volatile strategies. The investors associated with highly volatile investments discouraging them from entering hedge funds. Moreover, the onset of the turbulence in financial markets experienced in the 1970s completely wiped the hedge fund industry altogether. Improving market conditions in the 1980s, only a handful of hedge funds remained active over this period. Indeed, the lack of hedge funds around the market during this time changed the regulator's views on enforcing stricter regulation on the industry altogether. Not until the 1990s did the hedge fund industry begin to rise to prominence and attract renewed investor confidence.

Nowadays, hedge funds are still considered private investment schemes (vehicles) with a collective pool of capital only to a small range of institutional investors and wealthy individuals and having minimal regulation.
