Interpreting the main distribution in terms of the parent and then a child distribution within the parent gives us some qualitative understanding of how EVT is applied.

Consider Figure 7.9 that shows a hypothetical plot of the losses for a particular hedge fund over a 100-day period. Given such information the next step is to try to characterise the losses over this period in some mathematical way. EVT offers two fundamental methods for such characterisations, namely the block maxima and point over threshold methods.¹¹²

The block maxima (BM) method is based on subdividing the time period into a set of buckets (or blocks) of equal size. For example, Figure 7.10 reproduces the loss data given in Figure 7.9 for the hypothetical hedge fund over the 100-day period but divides the time into 10 blocks each block is a 10-day period that will contain a certain number of losses. Taking the maximum in each of the 10-day blocks gives us 10 local block maxima i.e. 10 data points which can be used to characterise (or fit) a probability distribution. This is often known as a generalised extreme distribution (GED).

Within the hedge fund area, the block maxima method is the less preferred estimation of VaR using the EVT.

The peaks over threshold (POT) method is a more modern and widely accepted method for estimating VaR although mathematically somewhat demanding. The basic

idea behind the POT method is to choose a numerical threshold for which every loss over that threshold is considered an extreme loss. The number of data points over the threshold can be used to characterise (or fit) a probability distribution i.e. in this case it is known as the generalised Pareto¹¹³ distribution (GPD). Figure 7.11 shows an example of a typical threshold in which only those losses exceeding $90,000 will be considered extreme relative to the value of the portfolio.

We have already seen in Chapter 4 how to consider a probability distribution in terms of a CDF i.e.

$F(x) = P(X \le x)$ (7.10)

![](img/2d1ac831a270b8b08f8b8ec2205913dc_284_0.png)

i.e. for a given value, $x$, $F(x)$ is the probability that observed value of a random variable $X$ will be at most $x$ less than or equal to $x$. For EVT there is a different function to consider based on a conditional probability (in a similar way to how the conditional expectation models the ES). That is, for an excess distribution of the return variable $X$ over a certain threshold $u$, the conditional probability distribution of $y = X - u$ such that $X > u$ can be written as:

$F_u(y) = P(X - u \le y|X > u)$ (7.11)

The CDF is now the probability that $y = X - u$ i.e. the excess (or exceedance) of the threshold is less than or equal to conditional on $X$ exceeding the threshold $u$ (Figure 7.12). One of the main theorems of EVT developed by Pickards (1975) states that for a reasonably high threshold, $u$, $F_u(y)$ can be approximated by the GPD which can be written as:

```
$G(X) = \begin{cases} 1 - (1 + \frac{\xi y}{\beta})^{-1/\xi} & \text{if } \xi \neq 0 \\ 1 - \exp(-\frac{y}{\beta}) & \text{if } \xi = 0 \end{cases} \quad (7.12)$
```

where $y = X - u, \xi = 1/\alpha$ is the shape parameter, $\alpha$ the tail index and $\beta$ a simple scaling parameter. There are several approaches to estimating the parameters in (7.12), such as *maximum likelihood, elemental percentile method and the method of moments* described in the studies by Hosking and Wallis (1987), Grimshaw (1993) and Castillo and Hadi (1997).

An estimate of the VaR the GPD approach described above can be written as:

```
$VaR_{1-\alpha} = u + \frac{\beta}{\xi} \left( \left( \frac{N}{n_u} \right)^{-\xi} - 1 \right) \quad (7.13)$
```

where $N$ is the total number of data points, and $n_u$ the number of data points which exceed the threshold $u$. Furthermore, the method can be extended so that the expected shortfall $ES_{1-\alpha}$ can be stated in terms of that is:

```
$ES_{1-\alpha} = \frac{VaR_{1-\alpha} - \beta - \xi u}{1 - \xi} \quad (7.14)$
```

For hedge funds, the amount of data in the tail of the returns distribution is often small and leads to broad confidence intervals and weak significance estimates. Both the BM and POT method suffer from the problem of limited data although although it is possible to reduce the time division in the BM or lower the threshold for the POT technique to produce more data points to fit to the desired distribution.

In this chapter we have provided an introduction to the main quantitative risk measures from the traditional VaR approaches to some of the more advanced and challenging theoretical hedge fund market models. We have seen throughout the book that hedge fund returns usually have fatter tails and an asymmetric return distribution which critically violates the assumption of a normal distribution that underlies traditional measures. In order to address such limitations many extensions to the traditional VaR methods have been developed as better estimates of hedge fund market risk. Such methods explicitly incorporate skewness and kurtosis into the model or focus primarily on the left-tails of the return distribution where most of the large losses occur. Despite the availability of more robust and potentially accurate market models, it must be pointed out that the analysis covers only one component of the risks associated with hedge funds. A more complete treatment would involve incorporating equally important risks into the analysis. For example, the monitoring, management and reporting of credit, liquidity and operational risk should also be considered alongside market risk in a robust and effective hedge fund management process.
