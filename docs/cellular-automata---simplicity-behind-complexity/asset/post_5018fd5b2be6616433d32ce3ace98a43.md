## Network Systems Modelled by Complex Cellular Automata Paradigm

Paweł Topa

*AGH University of Science and Technology*
*Poland*

### 1. Introduction

Most historical sources say that Cellular Automata were discovered by John von Neumann and Stanisław Ulam in the forties of the twentieth century. Von Neumann was investigating the problem of self-reproducing systems in biology. His initial kinematic model appeared to be too complicated and he followed the suggestion from Stanisław Ulam, who at the same time worked on problems of crystal growth. Von Neumann and Ulam defined an abstract universe in the form of two-dimensional regular mesh with interacting entities Neumann (1966).

In the seventies mathematician John Conway published his set of rules called “Game of Life”. His work was popularized by Martin Gardner in the pages of *Scientific American*. The rules of “Game of Life” implemented on computers showed an amazing world of living and dying automata. Its dynamics appeared to be so surprisingly complex that it became an area of intensive studies. A vast amount of work concentrated on searching for initial conditions that evolve into non-trivial behaviour of Cellular Automata. One of the most important achievements was proving that “Game of Life” can simulate a Universal Turing Machine i.e. can act as an abstract model of any computer.

In the eighties Stephen Wolfram spent part of his scientific career on investigating properties of one-dimensional Cellular Automata. His important achievement was the definition of 256 rules for one-dimensional Cellular Automata. He also introduced the first classification scheme based on long-period observation. The crowning achievement of his works was a book “A New Kind of Science” Wolfram (2002). In this monumental work Wolfram outlines a new paradigm for modelling complex phenomena with the Cellular Automata approach.

Scientific research related to Cellular Automata was not limited only to an abstract mathematical “toy”. Toffoli, Margolus and Fredkin postulated application of cellular automata for modelling physical systems Chopard & Droz (1998); Toffoli & Margolus (1987). They noticed that physical laws can be encoded in rules of interaction in temporally and spatially discrete universe.

The milestone in Cellular Automata theory was the lattice gas model HPP developed by Hardy et al. (1976). The model, which is in fact a cellular automaton, consists of a simple and fully discrete system of moving and colliding particles. Although the idea of lattice gas automata was invented independently, the Cellular Automata theory provided a clear conceptual framework. HPP model and its successor FHP (Frisch et al., 1986) were successful in modelling complex phenomena as flows in porous media, spreading of a liquid droplet and wetting phenomena.

### 2.1 Rivers and anastomosing river systems

Anastomosing rivers are interesting dynamic systems created as a result of interaction between constructive and destructive processes. Anastomosing systems are formed in the middle part of the river system. The slope on these areas is very small — approximately 10 cm per 1 km. The flow rate is also very small; the river does not erode the terrain. Organic and non-organic materials carried by the river are mostly transported through this part of the river and deposited later in the lower reaches. Waters rich in nutrients penetrate the soil in the surrounding banks. Fertile areas near river channels are characterized by a significant increase in vegetation. Products of plant decay are deposited in this area as peat (approximately 1–1.5 mm/year Gradziński et al. (2003)). Peat accumulation lasting for thousands of years may produce a peat bog with a depth of several meters.

The processes that occur on river channel banks are crucial for the creation of anastomosing systems. Figure 3 illustrates these processes. It presents a cross-section of a river channel. The rate of peat accumulation depends on the distance from the river channel. Water penetrates the soil near the channel and supplies nutrients. Nutrients are gathered by plants that vegetate on the river banks. In the areas that are more distant from the water, the vegetation is less abundant than in the areas that directly neighbour the channel. Consequently, the rate of peat deposition decreases with distance from the channel. Deposition on river banks is accompanied by deposition on river bottoms. As a result, the whole river channel is raised (see Fig. 3). The water level is raised above the average valley ground level. Sometimes the channel can also be partly blocked by clusters of floating plants, tree trunks etc. It is a metastable state that can be easily destroyed. High levels of water that usually occur during spring floods can cause river banks to break above obstacles. The river can create a bypass channel that evades the narrowness on the primary channel. The shape of the river in the valley usually forces the new channel to join the primary channel somewhere downstream.
