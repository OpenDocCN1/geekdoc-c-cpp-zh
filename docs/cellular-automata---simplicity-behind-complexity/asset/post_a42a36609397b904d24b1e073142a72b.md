
A CNF formula is represented by the family of linear inequalities corresponding to its clauses. Thus for example the formula $(x_1 \lor \bar{x}_2 \lor x_3) \land (\bar{x}_1 \lor x_3 \lor \bar{x}_4 \lor \bar{x}_5)$ is represented by the inequalities $x_1-x_2+x_3 \ge 0$ and $-x_1+x_3-x_4-x_5 \ge -2$. The axioms of the proof system are $x_i \ge 0$, $-x_i \ge -1$. The rules of inference are:

- (a) $\dfrac{\sum a_i \cdot x_i \ge A \;\land\; \sum b_i \cdot x_i \ge B}{\sum (a_i+b_i)\cdot x_i \ge A+B}$
- (b) $\dfrac{\sum a_i \cdot x_i \ge A}{\sum (c\cdot a_i)\cdot x_i \ge c\cdot A}$
  where $c \ge 1$ is an arbitrary integer;
- (c) $\dfrac{\sum (c\cdot a_i)\cdot x_i \ge A}{\sum a_i\cdot x_i \ge \left\lceil \frac{A}{c} \right\rceil}$
  where $c>1$ is an arbitrary integer.

A derivation $D$ of the inequality $I$ from inequalities $I_1,\ldots,I_m$ is a sequence $D_1,\ldots,D_n$ such that $I=D_n$ and for all $i \le n$ either $D_i$ is an axiom, or one of $I_1,\ldots,I_m$, or inferred from $D_j,D_k$ for $j,k<i$ by means of a rule of inference. A CP refutation of $I_1,\ldots,I_m$ is a derivation of $0\ge 1$ from $I_1,\ldots,I_m$.

We have seen above the soundness and the completeness of Resolution for CNF formulas, see Theorem 2.15. Soundness in the sense that given any formula $\phi$ which has a resolution refutation $\pi$, $\phi$ is not satisfiable and completeness in the sense that given any unsatisfiable formula $\phi$, there is a resolution refutation $\pi$ of $\phi$. Theorem 2.19 can be proved exploiting the completeness of $R$, since CP easily simulates resolution as observed in (23).

**Theorem 2.19.** *The proof system CP is sound and complete with respect to CNF formulas.*

For the soundness part we can argue as follows. Let $\phi$ be a CNF formula with a CP refutation $\gamma$. Suppose $\phi$ is satisfied by the assignment $\alpha$. Instantiate each inequality in $\gamma$ of $\phi$ by assigning the Boolean variables their value under $\alpha$. By induction on the length of $\gamma$ we can prove that each instantiated inequality in the resulting $\gamma$ holds. This is a contradiction, because we cannot have the inequality $0\ge 1$ as the last element of the refutation. Goerdt (30) proved that Frege systems polynomially simulate the CP proof system.

Other examples of mathematical proof systems are for instance the Nullstellensatz system introduced in (4), the Polynomial Calculus (15) and the Gaussian Calculus first defined in (5). At the end of this chapter a new mathematical proof system using cellular automata will be proposed.

## 3. Applications of propositional logic to cellular automata

Cellular automata can be described as large collections of simple objects locally interacting with each other. A $d$-dimensional cellular automaton consists of an infinite $d$-dimensional array of identical cells. Each cell is always in one state from a finite state set. The cells change their states synchronously in discrete time steps according to a local rule. The rule gives the new state of each cell as a function of the old states of some finitely many nearby cells, its neighbours. In the literature one can find different types of neighbourhoods depending upon which group of cells are taken into consideration during the application of the local rule. In the figure below the von Neumann, Moore and Smith neighbourhoods are displayed in Figure 1.

Fig. 1. The Moore neighborhood of the cell c, the von Neumann neighborhood of the cell c′ and the Smith neighborhood of the cell c″.

The automaton is homogeneous so that all its cells operate under the same local rule. The states of the cells in the array are described by a configuration. A configuration can be considered as the state of the whole array. The local rule of the automaton induces a global function that tells how each configuration is changed in one time step.¹⁷ In the literature cellular automata take various names according to the way they are used. They can be employed as computation models (27) or models of natural phenomena (61), but also as tessellation structures, iterative circuits (12), or iterative arrays (18). The study of this computation model was initiated by von Neumann in the ‘40s (46), (47). He introduced cellular automata as possible universal computing devices capable of mechanically reproducing themselves. Since that time cellular automata have also acquired some popularity as models for massively parallel computations.

¹⁷ For surveys on cellular automata the interested reader can see (35), (36).

Cellular automata have been extensively studied as discrete models for natural systems; they have several basic properties of the physical world: they are massively parallel, homogeneous and all interactions are local. Other physical properties such as reversibility and conservation laws can be programmed by selecting the local rule suitably. They provide very simple models of complex natural systems encountered in physics and biology. As natural systems they consist of large numbers of very simple basic components that together produce the complex behaviour of the system. We may compose $A$ with $B$ as follows: first run $A$ and then run $B$. Denoting the resulting cellular automaton by $B \circ A$ we have

Notice that this composition can be performed effectively. If $N_A$ and $N_B$ are neighbourhoods of $A$ and $B$, and $G_A$ and $G_B$ the global functions, then a neighbourhood of $G_B \circ G_A$ consists of vectors $\vec{x} + \vec{y}$ for all $\vec{x} \in N_A$ and $\vec{y} \in N_B$.

Intuitively, the shift functions translate the configurations one cell down in one of the coordinate directions. Formally, for each dimension $i = 1, \ldots, d$ there is a corresponding shift function $\sigma_i$ whose neighbourhood contains only the unit coordinate vector $\vec{e}_i$ and whose rule is the identity function. Translations are compositions of shift functions.

In the literature quite often a particular state $q \in S$ is specified as a quiescent state (which usually simulates empty cells). The state must be stable, i.e. $f(q, q, \ldots, q) = q$. Thus a configuration $c$ is said to be quiescent if all its cells are quiescent, $c(\vec{x}) = q$.

**Definition 3.5.** A configuration $c \in S^{\mathbb{Z}^d}$ is called finite if only a finite number of cells are non-quiescent, i.e. the set (support),

$$ \{ \vec{x} \in \mathbb{Z}^d \mid c(\vec{x}) \neq q \} $$

is finite.

Let $C_f$ be the subset of $C$ that contains only the finite configurations. Finite configurations remain finite in the evolution of the cellular automaton, because of the stability of $q$, hence the restriction $G^f$ of $G$ on the finite configurations is a function $G^f : C_f \to C_f$.

**Definition 3.6.** A spatially periodic configuration is a configuration that is invariant under $d$ linearly independent translations.

This is equivalent to the existence of $d$ positive integers $t_1, \ldots, t_d$ such that $c = \sigma_i^{t_i}(c)$ for every $i = 1, \ldots, d$. We denote the set of periodic configurations by $C_p$. The restriction $G^p$ of $G$ on the periodic configurations is hence a function $G^p : C_p \to C_p$.

Finite and periodic configurations are used in effective simulations of cellular automata on computers.
