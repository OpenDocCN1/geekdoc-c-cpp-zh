**Inline Exercise 7.4:** Let $f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ be a linear function. Let $a = f(e_1)$, $b = f(e_2)$, and $\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}$. Show that for any vector $\mathbf{v}$, we have $f(\mathbf{v}) = \phi_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{v})$. You’ll need to use the fact that $f$ is linear.

The collection of all such functions, that is,

$\mathbb{R}^{2*} = \{\phi_{\mathbf{w}} : \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^2\},$ (7.62)

forms a vector space: The sum of any two linear functions is again linear; the zero element of the vector space is $\phi_{\mathbf{0}}$; and the additive inverse of $\phi_{\mathbf{w}}$ is $\phi_{-\mathbf{w}}$. Scalar multiplication deserves a brief comment. What does it mean to multiply a function from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{R}$ by, say, 11? If $f$ is such a function, then $g = 11f$ is the function defined by

$g : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R} : \mathbf{v} \mapsto 11f(\mathbf{v}),$ (7.63)

that is, one multiplies the output of $f$ by 11.

**Inline Exercise 7.5:** Suppose that $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Explain why $3\phi_{\mathbf{w}} = \phi_{3\mathbf{w}}$.

This space of linear functions from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{R}$ is called the **dual space of $\mathbb{R}^2$**, and its elements are sometimes called **dual vectors or covectors**. This same idea generalizes to $\mathbb{R}^3$ or even $\mathbb{R}^n$. There’s an obvious correspondence between elements of $\mathbb{R}^2$ and elements of $\mathbb{R}^{2*}$, namely we can associate the vector $\mathbf{w}$ with the covector $\phi_{\mathbf{w}}$. So why not just call them “the same”? It will turn out that treating them distinctly has substantial advantages; in particular, we’ll see that when we transform all the elements of a vector space by some operation like rotation, or stretching the $y$-axis, the covectors transform differently in general.

In coordinate form, if $\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}$, then the function $\phi_{\mathbf{w}}$ can be written

$\phi_{\mathbf{w}} : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R} : \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} a & b \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}.$ (7.64)

Some books therefore identify covectors with row vectors, and ordinary vectors with column vectors.

Note that in designing software, just as it made sense to distinguish `Point` and `Vector`, it makes sense to have a `CoVector` class as well.

Covectors are particularly useful in representing triangle normals (the normal to a triangle is a nonzero vector perpendicular to the plane of the triangle, and is used in computing things like how brightly the triangle is lit by light coming in a certain direction). Although people often talk about normal vectors, such vectors are almost always used as covectors. To be precise, when we have a vector $\mathbf{n}$ normal to a triangle, we will almost never add $\mathbf{n}$ to another vector or a point, but we’ll often use it in expressions like $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{\ell}$ (where $\mathbf{\ell}$ might be, for instance, the direction that light is arriving from). Thus, it’s really the covector

$\mathbf{u} \mapsto \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{u}$ (7.65)

that’s of interest.
