Consider the upper half-cylinder \(H = \{(x,y,z): x^2 + z^2 = 1,\ 0 \le y \le 1\}\), which projects to \(\Omega\) under the axial projection map \(p\). We can perform a change of variables in the integral, and express \(B\) as

\[B = \int_{(x',y',z')\in H} y\,|Jp(x',y',z')|\,dA'. \tag{26.24}\]

where \((x,y,z)=p(x',y',z')\), and \(dA'\) is area on \(H\), and \(|Jp|\) is the Jacobian for the change of variables (i.e., it represents how areas at \((x',y',z')\) are stretched or contracted to become areas at \((x,y,z)\)). The theorem that \(p\) is area-preserving means that \(|Jp|=1\), so the integral becomes

\[B = \int_{(x',y',z')\in H} y\,dA'. \tag{26.25}\]

Since in the formula for \(p\), \(y\) does not change, we have \(y=y'\), so this becomes

\[B = \int_{(x',y',z')\in H} y'\,dA'. \tag{26.26}\]

By circular symmetry, this is just \(2\pi\) times the integral of \(y'\) from 0 to 1. That integral is \(1/2\), so \(B=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\pi=\pi\).

If instead we wanted to know the average of \(y\) over the upper hemisphere, we’d need to divide its integral (\(\pi\)) by the area of the hemisphere (\(2\pi\)). The average is thus \(\frac{1}{2}\).
