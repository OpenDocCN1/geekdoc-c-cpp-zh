In general, you can imagine controlling the attitude of an object by specifying a rotation using \(\theta\), \(\phi\), and \(\psi\). If you change any one of them, the rotation matrix changes a little, so you have a way of maneuvering around in \(\mathbf{SO}(3)\). The \(\cos\theta = 0\) situation is tricky, though. If \(\theta = \pi/2\), for instance, we find that multiple \((\phi,\psi)\) pairs give the same result; varying \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) turns out to not produce independent changes in the attitude of the object. This phenomenon, in various forms, is called **gimbal lock**, and is one reason that Euler angles are not considered an ideal way to characterize rotations.

## 11.2.3 Axis-Angle Description of a Rotation

One way to rotate 3-space is to pick a particular axis (i.e., a unit vector) and rotate about that direction by some amount. The matrix \(R_{xy}\) does this when the axis is the \(z\)-axis, for instance. We show, in the web materials, that every rotation in 3-space is rotation about some axis by some angle. Rodrigues [Rod16] discovered a formula to build a rotation for any axis and angle of rotation, and thus to produce any rotation matrix. We let

\[
\boldsymbol{\omega} = \begin{bmatrix} \omega_x \\ \omega_y \\ \omega_z \end{bmatrix} \tag{11.15}
\]

denote the unit-vector axis of rotation and \(\theta\) the amount of rotation about \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) (measured counterclockwise as viewed from the tip of \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) looking toward the origin).

To express the rotation we seek, we’ll need to use cross products a good deal. The function \(\mathbf{v} \mapsto \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}\) is a linear transformation from \(\mathbb{R}^3\) to itself; the matrix for this transformation is

\[
\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & -\omega_z & \omega_y \\
\omega_z & 0 & -\omega_x \\
-\omega_y & \omega_x & 0
\end{bmatrix}. \tag{11.16}
\]

**Inline Exercise 11.4.** (a) What’s \(\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \boldsymbol{\omega}\)? (b) Show that \(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\omega} = \mathbf{0}\) as expected. (c) Suppose that \(\mathbf{v}\) is a unit vector perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\). Explain why \(\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}\) is perpendicular to both, and why \(\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}) = -\mathbf{v}\).

The rotation matrix we seek is then

\[
\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{I} + \sin(\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + (1-\cos\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^2. \tag{11.17}
\]

From Inline Exercise 11.4, it’s clear that \(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\omega} = \boldsymbol{\omega}\). And if \(\mathbf{v}\) is perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\), then

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}
&= \mathbf{I}\mathbf{v} + \sin(\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\mathbf{v} + (1-\cos\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^2\mathbf{v}\\
&= \mathbf{v} + \sin(\theta)\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v} + (1-\cos\theta)\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v})\\
&= \mathbf{v} + \sin(\theta)\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v} + (1-\cos\theta)(-\mathbf{v})\\
&= \sin(\theta)\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v} + \cos(\theta)\,\mathbf{v}.
\end{aligned} \tag{11.18--11.21}
\]

which is just the rotation of \(\mathbf{v}\) in the plane perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) by an angle \(\theta\). Since \(\mathbf{M}\) does the right thing to \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) and to vectors perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\), it must be the right matrix, as per the Transformation Uniqueness principle (see Figure 11.2). In coordinate form, it’s

\[
\mathbf{M} =
\sin\theta
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & -\omega_z & \omega_y \\
\omega_z & 0 & -\omega_x \\
-\omega_y & \omega_x & 0
\end{bmatrix}
+ (1-\cos\theta)
\begin{bmatrix}
-\omega_y^2-\omega_z^2 & \omega_x\omega_y & \omega_x\omega_z \\
\omega_x\omega_y & -\omega_x^2-\omega_z^2 & \omega_y\omega_z \\
\omega_x\omega_z & \omega_y\omega_z & -\omega_x^2-\omega_y^2
\end{bmatrix}
+ \mathbf{I}. \tag{11.22--11.23}
\]

## 11.2.4 Finding an Axis and Angle from a Rotation Matrix

As we said earlier, it’s a theorem that every rotation of 3-space has an axis (i.e., a vector that it leaves untouched). We can use Rodrigues’ formula to recover the axis from the matrix. We’ll follow the approach of Palais and Palais [PP07].

We know every rotation matrix has an axis \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) and an amount of rotation, \(\theta\), about \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\); Rodrigues’ formula tells us the matrix must be

\[
\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{I} + \sin(\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + (1-\cos\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^2. \tag{11.24}
\]

The trace of this matrix (the sum of the diagonal entries) is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M})
&= \operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{I} + \sin(\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + (1-\cos\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^2\right)\\
&= \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{I}) + \sin(\theta)\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}) + (1-\cos\theta)\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^2)\\
&= 3 + (1-\cos\theta)\left(-2(\omega_x^2+\omega_y^2+\omega_z^2)\right)\\
&= 3 + (1-\cos\theta)(-2)\\
&= 1 + 2\cos\theta.
\end{aligned}
\]

so we can recover the angle of rotation by computing

\[
\theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M})-1}{2}\right). \tag{11.25}
\]

Two special cases arise at this point, corresponding to the two ways that \(\sin\theta\) can be zero.

1. If \(\theta = 0\), then *any* unit vector serves as an “axis” for the rotation, because the rotation is the identity matrix.
2. If \(\theta = \pi\), then *twice* the rotation has angle \(2\pi\), and thus is the identity, that is, our rotation \(\mathbf{M}\) must satisfy \(\mathbf{M}^2 = \mathbf{I}\). From this we find that

\[
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{I}) = \mathbf{M}^2 + \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M} + \mathbf{I}. \tag{11.26}
\]

That means that when we multiply \(\mathbf{M}\) by \(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{I}\), every column of \(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{I}\) remains unchanged. So any nonzero column of \(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{I}\), when normalized, can serve as the axis of rotation. We know that at least one column of \(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{I}\) is nonzero; otherwise, \(\mathbf{M}=-\mathbf{I}\). But this is impossible, because the determinant of \(-\mathbf{I}\) is \(-1\), while that of \(\mathbf{M}\) is \(+1\).

In the general case, when \(\sin\theta \ne 0\), we can compute \(\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}^{\mathsf T}\) to get

\[
\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}^{\mathsf T}
= \left(\mathbf{I}+\sin(\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}+(1-\cos\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{2}\right)
- \left(\mathbf{I}^{\mathsf T}+\sin(\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\mathsf T}+(1-\cos\theta)(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{2})^{\mathsf T}\right). \tag{11.27}
\]

Because \(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\mathsf T}=-\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\) and \((\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{2})^{\mathsf T}=\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{2}\), this simplifies to

\[
\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}^{\mathsf T}=2\sin(\theta)\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}. \tag{11.28}
\]

Dividing by \(2\sin\theta\) gives the matrix \(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\), from which we can recover \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\). The code is given in Listing 11.2.

## 11.2.6 Rotations and the 3-Sphere

The set \(\mathbf{SO}(3)\) of all \(3 \times 3\) rotation matrices can be difficult to understand. It is, in some sense, a subset of \(\mathbf{R}^9\): Just read the nine entries of the matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) in order to get the point in \(\mathbf{R}^9\) that corresponds to \(\mathbf{M}\). In a web extra, we give considerable detail on this set and its properties. Here, we’ll give just the essentials. The main tool used to understand \(\mathbf{SO}(3)\), to make computations involving \(\mathbf{SO}(3)\) more numerically robust, and to let us reason about operations in \(\mathbf{SO}(3)\) (like interpolation) by means of a familiar space, is \(\mathbf{S}^3\), the 3-sphere, or the set of all points \([w \quad x \quad y \quad z]^T\) in 4-space whose distance from the origin is 1. We’ve shuffled the coordinates on purpose to make some of what we say below involve less shuffling. We’ll also, in this section, talk about *points* of \(\mathbf{S}^3\), but we’ll always write them as *vectors* so that we can form linear combinations of them.

Just as you can wrap a line segment into a circle (joining the two boundary points into one point of the circle, as in Figure 11.3), or wrap a disk into a sphere (with the whole boundary circle becoming one point of the sphere, as in Figure 11.4), you can wrap a solid ball in 3-space into a 3-sphere, by collapsing the whole boundary sphere to a point. To do so, you must work in the fourth dimension, but the idea is to reason about the 3-sphere by analogy.

For instance, if we take two perpendicular unit vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) in the unit circle and construct all points of the form \(\cos(\theta)\mathbf{u} + \sin(\theta)\mathbf{v}\), these points cover the whole circle (see Figure 11.5). Similarly, in the 2-sphere, if we have two perpendicular unit vectors, their cosine-sine combinations form a **great circle**, that is, the intersection of the sphere with a plane through its center (see Figure 11.6). In fact, the same thing is true for the 3-sphere as well: Cosine-sine combinations of perpendicular vectors traverse a great circle on the 3-sphere. And the arc of this circle from \(\mathbf{u}\) to \(\mathbf{v}\) (i.e., from \(\theta = 0\) to \(\pi/2\)) is the shortest path between them, just as in the lower dimensions.

There’s a mapping from \(\mathbf{S}^3\) to \(\mathbf{SO}(3)\), given by

\[
K : \mathbf{S}^3 \to \mathbf{SO}(3) \tag{11.32}
\]

\[
\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \end{bmatrix}
\mapsto
\begin{bmatrix}
a^2+b^2-c^2-d^2 & 2(bc-ad) & 2(ac+bd) \\
2(ad+bc) & a^2-b^2+c^2-d^2 & 2(cd-ab) \\
2(bd-ac) & 2(ab+cd) & a^2-b^2-c^2+d^2
\end{bmatrix}. \tag{11.33}
\]

The map \(K\) has several nice properties.

- It’s *almost* one-to-one. In fact, it’s a two-to-one map: for any \(\mathbf{q} \in \mathbf{S}^3\), \(K(\mathbf{q}) = K(-\mathbf{q})\), as you can see by looking at the formula.
- Great circles on \(\mathbf{S}^3\) are sent, by \(K\), to geodesics (paths of shortest length) in \(\mathbf{SO}(3)\).
- \(K([1 \quad 0 \quad 0 \quad 0]^T) = \mathbf{I}.\)

This mapping arises from a definition of a kind of "multiplication" on \(\mathbf{R}^4\), closely analogous to the way we can treat points of \(\mathbf{R}^2\) as complex numbers and multiply them together. The multiplication on \(\mathbf{R}^4\) is *not* commutative, which causes some difficulties, but otherwise it’s closely analogous to multiplication of complex numbers. The set \(\mathbf{R}^4\), together with this multiplication operation, is called the **quaternions** (which is why we use a boldface \(\mathbf{q}\) for a typical element of \(\mathbf{S}^3\)).
