This simple model of mirror reflection ignores the Fresnel equations that arise from the polarization of light. There is no physical surface that actually reflects like a perfect mirror (or one with constant reflectivity 0 < ρ < 1) for all angles of incoming and outgoing light. We'll return to a more sophisticated version of mirror scattering when we discuss physically based models.

# ### 27.5.2 Lambertian Reflectors

A Lambertian surface has the property that when it's illuminated, the outgoing radiance in every (reflected) direction is the same (there's no transmission). Furthermore, this outgoing radiance varies linearly with irradiance: Whether we reduce the incoming illumination or make it arrive at a grazing angle, the outgoing radiance varies in the same way. Thus, the BRDF is constant; we usually write L(P, ω_o) = ρ/π, where ρ is a constant indicating what fraction of the arriving light energy is scattered.

Let's now check for which values of ρ this reflector will be energy-conserving. Imagine (as shown in Figure 27.11) that light arrives at a small, rectangular region, R, of material with area A, from a source like the sun: All incoming rays are in some small, solid angle Ω, and the radiance along each ray in a direction from Ω is the same constant ℓ. Then the total rate of energy arrival at the surface region R is the integral over R and Ω of the arriving radiance, multiplied by the dot product of the incoming direction and surface normal:

Power = Energy arrival rate  (27.4)
     = ∫_{P∈R} ∫_{ω_i∈Ω} L(P, -ω_i)(ω_i · n) dω_i dP  (27.5)
     = ∫_{P∈R} ∫_{ω_i∈Ω} ℓ(ω_i · n) dω_i dP  (27.6)
     = ℓA ∫_{ω_i∈Ω} (ω_i · n) dω_i  (27.7)
     ≈ ℓA ∫_{ω_i∈Ω} cos(θ) dω_i  (27.8)
     = ℓA m(Ω) cos(θ),  (27.9)

where m(Ω) denotes the measure of the solid angle Ω, and θ is the angle between a typical vector ω ∈ Ω and the (constant) surface normal n. As Ω gets small, the approximation of the dot product by a single central dot product gets increasingly accurate.
