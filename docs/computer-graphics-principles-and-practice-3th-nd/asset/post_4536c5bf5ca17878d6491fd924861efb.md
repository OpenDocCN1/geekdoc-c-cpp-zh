
We can see by considering a perfect Lambertian reflector, for which \(f_s(\omega_i, \omega_o) = 1/\pi\) for all \(\omega_i, \omega_o \in S_+^2\). (We’ll soon see why \(1/\pi\) is the right constant.)

Suppose for a moment that a photon arriving from a source in direction \(\omega_i\), the probability density of scattering in direction \(\omega_o\) were the same in all directions \(\omega_o\). To estimate the radiance at a point \(Q\) on the unit hemisphere around some surface point \(P\) (see Figure 27.8), we’d take a solid angle \(\Omega_1\) at \(Q_1\) and measure the density of the light energy arriving in that solid angle. For radiance to be constant, as we know it is for a Lambertian surface, we’d have to get the same value when we estimated it at \(Q_2\) with a solid angle \(\Omega_2\) of the same measure. But the amount of reflecting surface subtended by the two solid angles varies like the inverse of the “outgoing cosine,” leading to an extra \(1/(\omega_o \cdot n)\) factor in the outgoing radiance. The probability density of an incoming photon being scattered in direction \(\omega_o\) from a Lambertian surface must therefore be proportional to \(f_s(\omega_i, \omega_o)(\omega_o \cdot n)\).

## 27.5 Empirical and Phenomenological Models for Scattering

We now introduce a few basic scattering models that will serve several functions. The mirror and Lambertian models are the basis for several microfacet-based models that we’ll discuss when we examine physically based models. And the Blinn-Phong model, although not strictly physically based, is very widely used in practice.

### 27.5.1 Mirror “Scattering”

An ideal mirror-reflecting plane (for which all light is reflected rather than absorbed), shown in Figure 27.10, reflects light from a source in such a way that the emitted light distribution is exactly the same as would be produced by an identical source at some position behind the mirror’s location (assuming the mirror was removed). Because the outgoing radiance along each ray in these two situations is the same, the mirror-reflection process evidently results in no change in the net light energy in the scene.

Perfect mirrors are rare. More commonplace is that a mirrored surface in fact absorbs some amount of light, and reflects the remainder. The outgoing radiance along the mirror direction \(\omega_r = \omega_i - 2(\mathbf{n} \cdot \omega_i)\mathbf{n}\) is therefore a constant multiple of the incoming radiance:

\[L(P, \omega_o) = \rho L(P, -\omega_i), \tag{27.2}\]

where the *reflectivity* \(\rho\) is a number between 0 and 1. All that’s needed to specify the mirror reflectance from a surface is the normal vector \(\mathbf{n}\) and the reflectance constant \(\rho\), which is unitless. This constant may, however, have some spectral dependence (i.e., light at different wavelengths may be reflected differently).

The spectral dependence of reflectance depends on the underlying material. In broad terms, for insulators like plastic, the mirror-reflected light has the same spectral distribution as the incoming light, while for conductors, certain frequencies of light are preferentially reflected. This is why a polished piece of plastic has white highlights, while a polished piece of gold has gold-colored highlights. We’ll return to this in Section 27.8.3.

\[

0 & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\tag{27.3}
\]

where \(\lambda\) represents the wavelength of the light as usual.

This simple model of mirror reflection ignores the Fresnel equations that arise from the polarization of light. There is no physical surface that actually reflects like a perfect mirror (or one with constant reflectivity \(0 < \rho < 1\)) for all angles of incoming and outgoing light. We’ll return to a more sophisticated version of mirror scattering when we discuss physically based models.

### 27.5.2 Lambertian Reflectors

A Lambertian surface has the property that when it’s illuminated, the outgoing radiance in every (reflected) direction is the same (there’s no transmission). Furthermore, this outgoing radiance varies linearly with irradiance: Whether we reduce the incoming illumination or make it arrive at a grazing angle, the outgoing radiance varies in the same way. Thus, the BRDF is constant; we usually write \(f_r = \rho/\pi\), where \(\rho\) is a constant indicating what fraction of the arriving light energy is scattered.

Let’s now check for which values of \(\rho\) this reflector will be energy-conserving. Imagine (as shown in Figure 27.11) that light arrives at a small, rectangular region, \(R\), of material with area \(A\), from a source like the sun: All incoming rays are in some small, solid angle \(\Omega\), and the radiance along each ray in a direction from \(\Omega\) is the same constant \(\ell\). Then the total rate of energy arrival at the surface region \(R\) is the integral over \(R\) and \(\Omega\) of the arriving radiance, multiplied by the dot product of the incoming direction and surface normal:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Power} &= \text{Energy arrival rate} \tag{27.4}\\
&= \int_{P\in R} \int_{\omega_i\in\Omega} L(P,-\omega_i)(\omega_i\cdot n)\,d\omega_i\,dP \tag{27.5}\\
&= \int_{P\in R} \int_{\omega_i\in\Omega} \ell(\omega_i\cdot n)\,d\omega_i\,dP \tag{27.6}\\
&= \ell A \int_{\omega_i\in\Omega} (\omega_i\cdot n)\,d\omega_i \tag{27.7}\\
&\approx \ell A \int_{\omega_i\in\Omega} \cos(\theta)\,d\omega_i \tag{27.8}\\
&= \ell A\, m(\Omega)\cos(\theta), \tag{27.9}
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(m(\Omega)\) denotes the measure of the solid angle \(\Omega\), and \(\theta\) is the angle between a typical vector \(\omega \in \Omega\) and the (constant) surface normal \(n\). As \(\Omega\) gets small, the approximation of the dot product by a single central dot product gets increasingly accurate.
